\chapter{Stories from syntactic islands} \label{sec:extraction-islands}
The first islands in this book were literal. Those in this chapter are syntactic:
locations isolated inside clauses that a word can occupy but can't easily leave.

\term{Syntactic islands} is the name that John Robert Ross, widely known as Haj, gave to this phenomenon. Noam Chomsky had already noticed as early as 1964 that some natural-looking questions just can't be asked: you can ask \mention{What did she see?} but not \textsuperscript{*}\mention{What did she doubt the idea that he had seen?} Ross showed that these failures formed a pattern. His 1967 MIT dissertation, \textit{Constraints on variables in syntax} \citep{Ross1967}, was the rare dissertation that became part of the terrain. It didn't merely add a few exceptions to Chomsky's machinery. It turned scattered failures of extraction into a theory of syntactic borders: domains that dependencies could reach into but not escape from.

The dissertation is also a small character study. On the first page Ross thanks Chomsky in a way that doesn't undersell the debt: \enquote{What I owe to Noam Chomsky is incalculable.} A few pages later he defends his use of a William James anecdote that he admits may well be apocryphal, on the grounds that it has \enquote{bull's-eye relevance to the study of syntax.} An apocryphal story, used without apology, inside a formal dissertation: the gesture tells you something. Ross was doing exacting syntax with a mind that reached for image, anecdote, and metaphor. That combination helped make \term{islands} stick.

Ross had a gift for seeing syntactic patterns as scenes of motion. Words got \term{pied-piped}, like Browning's piper leading rats, when one word pulled other material along behind it. Clauses got \term{sluiced}, as if most of the sentence had been washed away and only the question word left behind, e.g., \mention{Someone left. Do you know who \textst{left}?} A verb leaving a coordinated structure left a \term{gap}, e.g., \mention{I chose one, and my sister \textst{chose} the other}. A \enquote{heavy} noun phrase shifted to the end of its sentence under its own weight, e.g., \mention{I gave \textst{the book that I bought last week} to my mom \uline{the book that I bought last week}}. The most consequential of his images involved a body of water that didn't exist, around a phrase that a word couldn't leave. 

All this movement was to be done by the syntactic machinery that Chomsky and his followers, Ross among them, were conjuring. And so, when they prepended an asterisk to a sentence like \textsuperscript{*}\mention{What did she doubt the idea that he had seen?}, they weren't merely reporting that it sounded wrong. They were saying the syntax couldn't have produced it. The mark was a structural verdict.

\bigskip

We don't usually think of words moving around, but compare \mention{\underline{What} did they see?} with \mention{They saw \underline{what}?} \mention{They saw what} might sound wrong, but you could ask it if I suddenly blurted out, \textit{they saw it}, without providing any context.

The important thing to notice is that the position of \mention{what} has changed. The first question, the normal one, starts with \mention{what}, but in a sense \mention{what}'s location in the second question, at the end, is its natural home. We say, \mention{I see it}, not \mention{it I see}. First comes a verb (\mention{see}), then the thing seen. So, we could think about \mention{what} leaving its natural position after \mention{see} and appearing instead at the beginning of the sentence. This becomes more apparent when the sentences are displayed one below the other, as in (\ref{ex:extraction-intro}).

\ea [] {
    \mention{They saw \underline{it}.}
    }
\ex [] {
    \mention{They saw \underline{what}?}
    }
\ex [] {
    \mention{\underline{What} did they see \underline{~~~}?}\\

In (\ref{ex:extraction-intro}c), the \mentionhead{\underline{~~~}} shows the position that \mention{what} left. And here's where Ross's islands come in. Consider \mention{She doubted the idea \ob that they had seen \underline{it}\cb}. The text in brackets is a syntactic island, and, because it's an island, \mention{it} can't become \mention{what} and get extracted. The syntax sinks, and we get the wonderfully strange question in (\ref{ex:island-intro}c).

\ea
\label{ex:island-intro}
\ea [] {
    \mention{She doubted the idea \textbf{that they had seen \underline{it}}.}
    }
\ex [] {
    \mention{She doubted the idea \textbf{that they had seen \underline{what}}?}
    }
\ex [*] {
    \mention{\underline{What} did she doubt the idea \textbf{that they had seen \underline{~~~}}?}
    }
\z\z

Upon reaching \mention{the idea} in this sentence, we stumble as the thread
of the sentence starts to unravel. We may read on, hoping that a solution
will present itself. We may go back and check if we'd misread it, but at
some point, we conclude that this sentence is just ungrammatical. The
syntax is snarled. It doesn't work.

Not only is it ungrammatical, but it's hard to fathom what it could
possibly mean.

This isn't an everyday error like \textsuperscript{*}\textit{How many people was there?} or \textsuperscript{*}\textit{She went school}. Often, we understand such sentences intuitively and can even conjure a quick correction without much thought. But even a good deal of thought may not help us with (\ref{ex:island-intro}c).

\section{What Grammar Is}

If we default to thinking about meaning when grammar comes up, we risk missing a good deal. Each of these~-- a cloud, a wagging tail, the smell of smoke, the distance we keep, a musical crescendo, the cadence of a heartbeat~-- means something to us, but none has a grammar. Signals like these signify relatively simple concepts, and we mostly lack conventional ways to recombine them to convey anything more complex or even slightly different.

Compare this, though, to the subtle difference between \textit{what is grammar} and \textit{what grammar is}. One conveys a question, the other an answer. The difference is clear, and the grammar seems to produce it.

Grammar is a conventional system linking what is said to what is meant.
The linkage goes both ways, connecting form to meaning and meaning to
form, or maybe connecting both form and meaning to some other
representation \citep[371]{Futrell2020}.

A breakdown in that connection seems to be the meaning of \mention{ungrammatical} in its first known use.

\begin{quote}
    It seems a great folly to declaim against us for denying the literal, natural sense, and yet that themselves should expound it in a sense which suffers a violence and a most unnatural, \uline{ungrammatical} torture; for if they may change the words from the right sense and case to the oblique and indirect, why may not we?\hfill\citep{taylor1654real}
\end{quote}

Taylor's phrase is worth pausing over because it doesn't accuse a
sentence of being malformed. It accuses an interpretation of forcing a
grammatical form to carry a meaning it doesn't normally bear. The torture
is interpretive.

That matters because the earliest known English use of
\mention{ungrammatical} already links form to meaning. It isn't just a
complaint about arrangement. It's a complaint that the arrangement has
been made to say the wrong thing.

Jeremy Taylor was born in 1613. Nothing at all is known of his mother, but his father was a barber. As a barber, he would have been something of a surgeon, and the son writes that his father was able to instruct his children in grammar and mathematics, suggesting a good level of education \citep[28]{worley_jeremy_taylor_1904}. 

Knowledge of grammar doesn't mean merely that the senior Taylor helped
his son understand subject--verb agreement, pronoun use, and how to form
relative clauses. A grammatical education also implied the study of Latin
and sometimes Greek, reflecting the Renaissance curriculum's heavy
emphasis on classical languages.

These languages were key to accessing a vast body of important religious,
philosophical, and scientific texts. They were also considered essential
for any scholarly or clerical career. Grammar teaching often involved the
arts of rhetoric and logic, seen as fundamental to effective
communication and reasoning. \textit{Grammar} meant a whole lot more than
it does today.

But even in today's understanding as the form and structure of language, the study of grammar held much greater social importance than it does today. It's only a small exaggeration to say that if you were an educator, philosopher, clergyman, or scientist, a printer, a publisher, a politician, or even a pub owner, you might have written a grammar. 

The American Quaker grammarian Goold Brown, in his massive
\textit{Grammar of English grammars}, lists almost 500 grammars, roughly
two a year since 1586, when the printer William Bullokar composed,
printed, and published the first grammar of English, mainly as a business
venture \citep{brown_grammar_grammars}.

\bigskip

One notable figure in this history was Ann Fisher, who publish \textit{A new grammar} in 1745. Fisher was a pioneering educator who ran a successful school for young ladies in Newcastle. Her grammar was innovative in its focus on practical language use and its attempt to make grammar accessible to a wider audience without dealing with Latin or Greek.

Fisher's book was both well regarded and popular, going through at
least 31 editions. Brown acknowledges its influence on his own work while appearing to
believe Fisher was a man: \enquote{Fisher also rejected the class of
neuter verbs, and called them all active. \uline{He} reduces the moods to
three} \citep[138]{brown_grammar_grammars}. Initially, Ann Fisher
published without attribution, conscious that her gender could limit its
appeal, but as her celebrity grew, her name came to adorn the title page for
all to see.

Her success seems to have inspired other women to enter the field. By the
late eighteenth century, at least six more women grammarians emerged:
Ellin Devis, Mrs. M.C. Edwards, Lady Eleanor Fenn, Jane Gardiner, Blanch
Mercy, and Mrs. Eves.

The most popular of their published books seems to have been the one by
Devis, who, like Fisher, was an educator and proprietor of a school for
girls. Devis's 1775 book also proved extremely popular, going through at
least 18 editions. It was called \textit{The Accidence or First Rudiments of English Grammar Designed for the use of Young Ladies}. The trend in book titles at the
time was to explain, in detail, what the book was about. Fisher's full
title, as given on its title page, is over 130 words long.

Not only did Fisher break ground for women, but she also first formulated the controversial rule promoting the use of the masculine singular pronoun \mention{he} to refer to both genders \citep{Tieken-Boon}. She wrote, \enquote{The Maſculine Perſon anſwers to the general Name, which comprehends both Male and Female; as, any \textit{Perſon who knows what he ſays}, \&c.} \citep[106]{Fisher1785}.

From a modern perspective, it may seem surprising that a path-blazing woman would propose such a rule. But no sooner had it been published than it was plagiarized by a man. John Kirkby was an educator and perhaps a serial plagiarist who ran a school and was, despite his plagiaristic tendencies, well regarded. Joseph Priestley
(better known for his work in chemistry and physics) was Kirkby's student before publishing his own grammar in 1761.

It wasn't until the eighteenth century's end that the frequent plagiarism
by early grammarians came to be seen as a genuine offence, largely
because of new conceptions of originality among the Romantic poets. Once
it did, plagiarism became a common accusation in the world of grammars,
and let us be clear: there was often good money to be made and good reason to credit the accusations.

In 1758, Robert Lowth, a prominent clergyman and grammarian, published his \textit{Short introduction to English grammar}. It started life as a book for his four-year-old son, and went on to become a standard text in schools. \enquote{Lowth's name consequently acquired almost mythical status, as the bishop who single‐handedly reduced the English language to rule, and to whom we still owe many linguistic strictures, which are generally experienced as unnecessarily constraining} \citep[1]{Ostade2011a}. Because of its popularity, it was both plagiarized and pirated outright.

In the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, writing a grammar was a
strangely common way to signal that one was erudite, but it was no
guarantee of actual erudition. Goold Brown, in his survey of grammars,
was scathing on this point.

\begin{quote}
    While some have thus wasted their energies in eccentric flights, vainly supposing that the learning of ages would give place to their whimsical theories; others, with more success, not better deserved, have multiplied grammars almost innumerably, by abridging or modifying the books they had used in childhood. So that they who are at all acquainted with the origin and character of the various compends thus introduced into our schools, cannot but desire to see them all displaced by some abler and better work, more honourable to its author and more useful to the public, more intelligible to students and more helpful to teachers. Books professedly published for the advancement of knowledge, are very frequently to be reckoned, among its greatest impediments; for the interests of learning are no less injured by whimsical doctrines, than the rights of authorship by plagiarism. Too many of our grammars, profitable only to their makers and venders, are like weights attached to the heels of Hermes. \\ \phantom{xxx}\hfill \citep[35]{brown_grammar_grammars}
\end{quote}

Brown is happy to point fingers. Among his list of grammars, he includes occasional snipes, such as \enquote{an egregious plagiarism}, \enquote{a miserable jumble}, \enquote{of no value}, \enquote{not worth a pin}, \enquote{a mere trifle}, \enquote{a sham}, \enquote{worthless}, and \enquote{fudge!} But along with trenchant observations like \enquote{this author can see others' faults better than his own}, Brown also had words of praise. Of Frederick Barnard's 1836 \textit{Analytic grammar; with symbolic illustration}, for instance, he wrote, \enquote{this is a curious work, and remarkably well-written.}

Lowth's grammar was both the target of plagiarists and of accusations of plagiarism. In his own grammar, Noah Webster, author of \textit{An American dictionary of the English language}, wrote, \enquote{since the days of \textit{Wallis}, who published a Grammar of the English Language, in Latin, in the reign of Charles II.[,] from which Johnson and Lowth borrowed most of their rules, \textit{little improvement} has been made in English grammar} (cited in Brown).

Lowth is also notable for being the first to base his grammar around the \enquote{false syntax} of published examples. He very confidently critiqued the grammar of the most eminent writers of the time: Shakespeare, Swift, Milton, Addison, and Pope. Even the King James Bible came in for criticism. And the basis of Lowth's accusations of false syntax, or forms that a modern Lowth might characterize as \enquote{ungrammatical}, was Latin grammar.

\bigskip

Fisher matters here for one more reason. Hers was the first grammar to be
published \enquote{with Exerciſes or Examples of bad English.} What
qualified as \enquote{bad English} was expressions such as \textsuperscript{*}\textit{Plumbs
is eaten}, \textsuperscript{*}\textit{This Men are exceeding wiſe}, \textsuperscript{*}\textit{Them Fellows
always ſtand by one another}, \textsuperscript{*}\textit{Thou art the moſt wiſeſt Boy I
ever ſaw}, and \textsuperscript{*}\textit{There is no Body ſo weak of Invention, which
cannot aggravate or make ſome little Stories to vilify his Enemy; and
there is few but has good inclinations to hear them.} Often these seem to
be targeting forms occurring in the vernacular speech of lower-class
speakers at the time.

This isn't surprising. Grammar teachers were often teaching English as a
second dialect. But it also sits uneasily with Fisher's own definition.
Her book begins by defining \mention{grammar} as \enquote{the Art of
expreſſing the Relation of Things in Conſtruction \dots according to the
Cuſtom of thoſe whoſe Language we learn} \citep[1]{Fisher1785}, an
admirable definition. Presumably, those speaking the lower-class
vernacular spoke it according to the custom of their parents and peers.
Perhaps the real question is whose custom counted, who \enquote{we} are.

The book includes the word \mention{ungrammatical}, but only in a list of five-syllable words. She does use \mention{grammatically}.

\begin{quote}
    \textit{T\textsc{he} Parts of Speech}, or \textit{Kinds} of \textit{Words}, which conſitute any one \textit{Language}, are the ſame in all others, i.e. whatever Words are \textit{Names}, or \textit{Noun Subſtantives}, \textit{Verbs}, \&c. in \textit{English}, are the ſame in \textit{Greek}, \textit{Latin}, \textit{French}, \&c. though expreſſed by different Terms ; alſo ſome general Rules of Grammar are univerſally applied to all Tongues. A Perſon, therefore, who understands \textit{English} \uline{grammatically}, must be allowed to have a good Notion of Grammar in general, i. e. that of every other Nation.\hfill \citep[iv]{Fisher1785}
\end{quote}

\begin{quote}
    \textit{I\textsc{f}} to be Maſter of any Language, ſo as to write it with \textit{Propriety} and \textit{Exactneſs}, is to understand it \textit{\uline{grammatically}}; it must certainly be a \textit{nearer} or \textit{more conciſe} \textit{Way} to the Perfection of ours to learn the \textit{English Grammar itſelf}, than to go about to learn the \textit{Latin one}, \&c. merely to come at the Knowledge of our own from the Nature of theirs.\hfill \citep[vii]{Fisher1785}
\end{quote}

\begin{quote}
    It is impoſſible for any one to ſpeak and write correctly who is unacquainted with Syntax, or has not learned the Language in a grammatical Way.\hfill\citep[xii]{Fisher1785}
\end{quote}

In each case, her meaning isn't `in a way that respects the structure of the language' as in \enquote{that's a grammatical sentence} but rather `in a way that relies on explicit knowledge of the rules of grammar,' as in \enquote{I can explain the sentence grammatically but not historically.}

\section{Explanations}

It's crucial to realize that syntactic islands don't crop up all that often. It's not just a matter of adding in a few extra bits and you've got an island. Consider (\ref{ex:non-island}), where \mention{what} has no problem leaving its home spot and appearing at the front.

\ea
\label{ex:non-island}
\ea [] {
    \mention{She doubted that I had seen \underline{it}.}
    }
\ex [] {
    \mention{She doubted that I had seen \underline{what}?}
    }
\ex [] {
    \mention{\underline{What} did she doubt that I had seen \underline{~~~}?}
    }
\z\z

And we can keep adding on without tripping up.

\ea
\label{ex:non-island-deep}
\ea [] {
    \mention{She doubted that I had heard that you had seen \underline{it}.}
    }
\ex [] {
    \mention{She doubted that I had heard that you had seen \underline{what}?}
    }
\ex [] {
    \mention{\underline{What} did she doubt that I had heard that you had seen \underline{~~~}?}
    }
\z\z

This contrast is Ross's problem in miniature. The bad sentence in
(\ref{ex:island-intro}c) isn't just odd. It is bad beside a nearly
identical sentence that works fine. Ross's move was to treat that
difference as a clue. His dissertation didn't offer a list of impossible
questions; it drew a map of failed escape routes and asked what sort of
border had been crossed.

That pattern cried out for explanation. We'll return to these islands
later in the chapter. For now, I want to think about what kind of
explanation would satisfy us.\footnote{Discourse constraints may also
limit long-distance dependencies in English and French (Winckel et al.
2025). Chapter 8 returns to this issue. % TODO: verify source -- add Winckel et al. 2025 to localbibliography.bib
}



A good explanation of islands has to do more than label the bad cases. It
has to say why some long-distance questions pass so easily while others
collapse. It should cover more than one island type, leave room for
differences across languages, predict new cases, and stay simple enough
to be worth believing.

That's why Ross can't merely stand at the door to this chapter. His
islands give us the first serious case in the book where the asterisk
looks like pure syntax: a sentence whose failure seems structural,
independent of politeness, schooling, register, or situation. The
temptation is understandable. The failure in (\ref{ex:island-intro}c)
feels hard and general. But a hard case can teach the wrong lesson. Once
we have found a border, we still have to ask what kind of fact the border
records.

Ross himself wasn't sure. The man who gave the formalist program its most architectural-looking border later moved toward a view in which syntax and meaning couldn't be cleanly separated \citep{Harris2021}. He called his own approach \term{Semantax}, on the conviction that syntax and semantics interpenetrate inseparably. The man who named the islands turned out to think the asterisks his discovery licensed didn't mean what most linguists took them to mean.



\section{The Tristan chord and acceptance} \label{sec:tristan}
In 1865, Richard Wagner's \enquote{Tristan und Isolde} premiered with a
chord that seemed to hang in the air without resolving. The Tristan
chord, F, B, D\(\sharp\), and G\(\sharp\), made no ordinary sense to
many listeners. Hector Berlioz, who had received an early copy of the
score from Wagner, found the Prelude confounding, noting, \enquote{There
is no theme other than a kind of chromatic moan, filled with dissonant
chords whose long appoggiaturas in place of the true harmonic note
further intensify the painfulness.} % TODO: verify source -- Berlioz quote, primary source À travers chants (1862)
% https://thelistenersclub.com/2020/06/08/wagners-tristan-und-isolde-prelude-and-liebestod/

But even though it made no sense in the framework of musical harmony, some people were able to find a kind of narrative sense in it. For them, that unfulfilled tension mirrored the opera's story of unrequited love. And, as a result, this innovative use of (dis)harmony disrupted the conventions of Western music. Wagner's composition didn't just utilize the existing musical language~-- it expanded it, challenging the ear and setting the stage for the even more radical departures from tonality that the 20th century would bring.

Herbie Hancock tells the following story:\footnote{https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FL4LxrN-iyw}

\begin{quote}
    I remember that we were playing \textit{So what}, one of Miles's compositions, you know, late '50s I guess. And Tony Williams was playing drums, Ron Carter bass, Wayne Shorter saxophone. It was a really hot night. The music was tight, powerful, innovative, and fun. We were having a lot of fun, and the music was \textit{on}. Tony Williams was bringing it on his drums.
    
    So, right in the middle of Miles's solo, when he was playing one of his amazing solos, and I'm trying, you know, I'm in there, and I'm playing right in the middle of his solo, I played the wrong chord. A chord that just sounded completely wrong. It sounded like a big mistake. 
    
    And I did this, and I went like this. I put my hands around my ears, and Miles paused for a second, and then he played some notes that made my chord right. He made it correct, which astounded me. I was, I couldn't believe what I heard. Miles was able to make something that was wrong into something that was right with the power of his choice of notes that he made and that feeling that he had. 
    
    And so, I couldn't play for about a minute. I couldn't even touch the piano, you know. But what I realize now is that Miles didn't hear it as a mistake. He heard it as something that happened, just an event, and so that was part of the reality of what was happening at that moment, and he dealt with it. He found something that, since he didn't hear it as a mistake, he felt it was his responsibility to find something that fit, and he was able to do that.
\end{quote}

Some linguists would argue that *\mention{\underline{What} did she doubt the idea \textbf{that I had seen \underline{~~~}}?} is ungrammatical in English in the same way that $2+2=3$ is incorrect in arithmetic. Others, though, would argue that the ungrammaticality is much more akin to the way the \enquote{Tristan Chord} was experienced when audiences first heard Wagner's Tristan and Isolde.

One side reads the asterisk as math: there's a rule, the sentence breaks it, that's the end. The other side reads it the way audiences came to read the Tristan chord: as a piece of dissonance that a community hadn't yet learned to hear. What's wrong now might not always be.

Ross's own career runs both sides of this debate. The 1967 dissertation made the arithmetic reading look right; his later work, \term{Semantax}, sits on the Tristan side. The man who gave the formal program its cleanest evidence didn't end up believing the cleanness was the lesson.

\section{More than one meaning}

\section{The model of grammaticality}\label{sec:model-of-grammaticality}

\subsection{What's a construction?}

Language isn't just a collection of individual words, and it's not just the words that hold meaning. Consider phrases like \textit{come here}, \textit{think about it}, or \textit{don't touch that}. The form conveys something more than the sum of the word meanings. Try to take a step back and see it in a new light.

First of all, did you notice that the phrases address `you'. If English had evolved a little differently, perhaps \textit{think about it} could mean `we think about it', `she thinks about it', or `they think about it'. In fact, in Japanese, the word-for-word translation often means `I'll think about it'. But not in English.

The word \mention{you} is absent and yet the directive is aimed at the person being spoken to. It is the form (the grammatical structure itself) that says `you', and even a slightly different structure (\mention{to think about it} or \mention{thinks about it}) means something different.

Secondly, I called it a directive, and it absolutely is. This may seem obvious, but the phrase means both `I want you to do this' and, at the same time, means `I believe that our social and contextual relationship is such that I can reasonably expect you to comply'. It includes an element of compulsion.

The form of \textit{think about it} didn't have to convey a directive. It's close to a request, but not quite. It could, perhaps, have been a declaration, an exclamation, or a question. It could even have been a conditional = `if you think about it'.

I hope this seems rather implausible to you. I hope you wonder how \textit{think about it} could possibly be conditional. I even hope you think the notion is utterly preposterous. Because, not only could it convey conditionality, but it does: \textit{think about it and you'll see what I mean} (= `\uline{if you think about it}, you'll see what I mean').

When linguists speak about \textsc{constructions}, this is what they have in mind: not just some kind of grammatical structure but one that means something, a form--meaning pairing.

\bigskip

Consider the age construction: \textit{I'm 55}, \textit{she's nine},
\textit{it's 112}. These don't indicate height or girth or any kind of
rating. They convey ages, specifically ages in years, and everybody knows
that. If you wanted to count some other period of time, you'd have to say
so (\textit{she's nine months}). And yet, if it's years you almost have
to omit the unit. \textsuperscript{?}\textit{I'm 55 years} is kinda
weird.

Of course, it gets much better if you append \mention{old}. Conversely,
if I wanted to say my age in French, \mention{ans} `years' is obligatory.
I would say \textit{j'ai 55 ans}, literally, `I have 55 years'.

That brings up another issue: a Francophone couldn't state her age in
English by directly translating the French as *\textit{I have 55} or even
*\textit{I have 55 years}. In context, the meaning might be clear, but
the statement is ungrammatical. It's not that the form is impossible. If
I ask, \textit{How many students do you have in your class}, then
\textit{I have 55} is a perfectly grammatical response.

The problem is that the form \textit{I have 55} doesn't mean `I'm stating
my age'. It means something else, and if you want to convey your age,
then you need to use the age construction, a specific form--meaning
pairing.

\bigskip

Or consider the difference between \mention{how beautiful is she?} and \mention{how beautiful she is!} Structurally, all we've done here is swapped the order of \mention{she} and \mention{is}, but emotionally, we've gone from flat to infatuated.

\bigskip

A construction is much more than just grammar in the old-school sense. It's a little package deal, a specific arrangement of words, their forms (like verb tenses or plurals), and even the way they're spoken, all bundled up with a specific meaning. That meaning can be straightforward, like a simple request for information, or it can be layered with subtle things like social cues (\textit{come here}) or emotion (\textit{how beautiful she is}).

\subsection{An analogy}

Many people treat grammaticality as if it were like the line around a
soccer field. The ball is in or out. The question is settled. That works
for some cases, but a better analogy is the handball rule. The rule is
real. Applying it depends on position, intention, advantage, and the speed
at which the referee has to judge what happened.

Grammaticality judgments often work that way. A construction may be
clearly inside the language, clearly outside it, or somewhere harder to
call. Whether a form is tolerated can depend on dialect, register,
processing pressure, context, and whether the speaker's meaning remains
recoverable. The rule matters, but so does the situation in which the
rule has to be applied.

\subsection{The model}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item At its most basic, a grammatical construction is one with an accepted form--meaning pairing within a specific language community, dialect, register, or situation.
    \item The meaning element has a semantic component and often includes a socio-pragmatic aspect. \\(e.g., \textit{Try it and you'll regret it.} conveys a threat)
    \item A grammatical construction may evolve additional accepted form--meaning pairings through misinterpretation or reanalysis of existing constructions. \\(e.g., the \textit{going to} futurate)
    \item Conversely, grammatical constructions may fall out of favour, becoming ungrammatical as they are socially deprecated. \\(e.g., the simple past tense in Standard spoken French)
    \item Grammaticality within a language community reflects what distinctions the community considers relevant to encode.\\(e.g., the progressive aspect is mandatory in certain contexts in English but not in French; evidentiality is mandatory in certain contexts in Turkish but not in English.)
    \item Grammaticality isn't required for meaning. Many ungrammatical constructions convey their intended meaning perfectly. \\(e.g., *\textit{It very good.})
    \item A construction is considered ungrammatical when:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item The form fails to pair with any meaning. \\(e.g., *\textit{Can the have running?})\\ Note that this isn't the same as pairing with an intentionally bizarre or nonsensical meaning, especially one stemming from incongruent lexical choices. Such a pairing can still be grammatical.
        \item The intended form--meaning pairing clashes because
        \begin{enumerate}
            \item A divergence is perceived between intended and expected meanings \\(e.g., *\textit{I have 35 years.} intended as `I am 35 years old.')
            \item A contradictory form--meaning pairing occurs in the same utterance \\(e.g., *\textit{I have been there yesterday.})
        \end{enumerate}
        \item A particular form--meaning pair is generally or potentially appropriate but an alternate form is preferred in a specific case, and no compelling reason justifies the deviation. \\(e.g., *\textit{We sheared three sheeps.})
        \item It's exceedingly rare, either because it's coming into or going out of fashion, or because the right conditions for a particular form--meaning pair rarely arise. % TODO: verify completion captures intended sense
        (e.g., syntactic satiation; \cite{Snyder2022})
    \end{enumerate}
    \item A grammatical construction can be wrongly judged ungrammatical when:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item the structure is misperceived \\(e.g., \textit{The old man the phones.} intended as `The elderly are responsible for handling the phones.')
        \item processing capabilities are exceeded by structural demands. \\(e.g., \textit{The bread the baker the apprentice helped made is delicious.})
    \end{enumerate}
    \item An ungrammatical construction can be wrongly judged grammatical when processing capabilities are exceeded by semantic demands. \\(e.g., *\textit{In Michigan and Minnesota, more people found Mr Bush's ads negative than they did Mr Kerry's.})
    \item Motivations for accommodating or reanalyzing ungrammatical constructions as grammatical include:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item Semantic motivations, such as metaphorical usage for novel meanings.
        \item Social motivations reflecting identity or status.
        \item Structural motivations due to
        \begin{enumerate}
            \item processing constraints \\(e.g., length, embedding, long dependency distances)
            \item lack of clarity \\(e.g., after loss of inflectional endings, Jespersen's cycle, etc.)
            \item structural analogy \\(e.g., \textsuperscript{?}\textit{I asked about what did she do.})
        \end{enumerate}
        \item Iconic usage in rare instances \\(e.g., trilled /r/)
    \end{enumerate}
    \item While most ungrammaticality involves form--meaning pairings that could potentially be reanalyzed through exposure, a small set of constructions (like the Left Branch Condition) involve operations that disrupt the basic structural integrity of the linguistic unit being manipulated. These constructions are impossible to grammaticalize, as evidenced by their complete resistance to satiation, even while formally similar constructions become more acceptable.
    \item Grammaticality judgments always involve syntax or morphology.
    \item Despite the focus on syntax and morphology, form--meaning mismatches may involve lexical or phonological elements, including intonation and prosody. \\(e.g., *\textit{Each con\textbf{tract} is different}, with stress on the second syllable as in the verb; perhaps malapropisms like *\textit{This library is a great suppository of knowledge.})
    \item Differing reasons for ungrammaticality and amount of difficulty establishing a clear form--meaning pairing may result in a range in the severity of grammaticality judgments.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Architectural constraints and syntactic inviolability}
Some limits may sit closer to the architecture of a language than to
etiquette. The Left Branch Condition is a useful warning case. English is
bad at pulling a modifier or possessor out of the left edge of a noun
phrase: *\textit{How big did you buy a house?} is much worse than
\textit{How big a house did you buy?}

That doesn't mean every language draws the same boundary. It does mean
that repeated exposure won't necessarily turn every structural mismatch
into a new convention. Some violations are awkward because we aren't used
to them. Others may be awkward because the grammar gives us no stable way
to recover the intended dependency.


\section{Emergence}

Grammaticality can't be found in the laws of physics. It's an emergent
property. But if you believe in people and puppies, in the scent of rain
on hot asphalt, in the first blooming lilac of spring, in orbits and
orders and other such stuff, then there's every reason to believe that
grammaticality is real.

That brings us back to Ross. His achievement was to make one kind of
grammatical boundary visible. The danger was that the boundary looked so
crisp on the page that it encouraged a larger conclusion: grammaticality
itself might be syntax in its pure structural form, and the asterisk
might apply, or fail to apply, in the abstract, for human language in
general. The analysis worked so well that it opened a garden path.

This book starts from the suspicion that the conclusion was too quick.
The asterisk draws a line in front of a sentence, but the line may mark
several different things: a failed dependency, a failed form--meaning
pairing, a social expectation, a processing collapse, or a convention not
yet learned. Grammaticality is real in the same sense Ross's islands are
real: a pattern stable enough to guide inquiry, but never separable from
the uses, speakers, histories, and situations that make the pattern
matter.
