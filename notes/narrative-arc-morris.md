## Proposed narrative arc: Morris's take

### The arc in one sentence

A linguist who has spent his career marking sentences with stars goes looking for what the star actually means, finds himself watching the field's best minds star a sentence that turns out to be fine, and walks out the other side with an account of grammaticality that nobody who marks asterisks has been brave enough to live with.

### The opening

Open on a classroom. International students in a Humber College ESL room, laughing. One of them has just said *the road is long long*. The teacher (Brett, but we don't know that yet) writes it on the board and puts a star next to it. The students stop laughing. One of them says, in halting English, that in her language this is the only way to say it.

That is the first shot. We hold on the asterisk on the board for a beat too long. The reader's silent question is the question Brett has been asking since 1995: what does that mark mean? Who said it gets to be there? Why is it there in this room and not in hers?

This is a Morris cold open. Object, witness, irreducibly strange fact. We do not yet know we are in a book about grammaticality. We think we are in a book about a teacher, a student, and a small mark on a whiteboard.

### The development

The witness is the asterisk itself, mute, used by everyone, examined by nobody. The storyteller is Brett, a working linguist who is willing to be on camera, who has marked thousands of those stars and is no longer sure what he was doing. The protagonist is the reader, who came in believing some sentences are simply impossible and is going to leave believing something stranger.

The suspect lineup forms slowly. Schleicher, who invented the asterisk to mark words that nobody had ever heard but that ought to have existed. Sweet, who flipped its meaning to mark words that nobody would ever say. Ross, age 28, drawing islands. Hankamer and Postal, two senior syntacticians in 1973 marking *someone whose had left* with a star. Then the camera pans to the *Cambridge Grammar*, marking the same star in 2002. Then to Cinque in 2020 calling it a residual puzzle. Each one in good faith. Each one working with the data and the tools they had.

In the middle, the case-files crack open in domains the reader doesn't expect. A Turkish woman calling the Pope very very good. Cory Doctorow emailing back "yup, it's colloquial." Tomo translating for his grandmother. Father Putnam in a Washington alley with a tape recorder in 1953. The reader realises, slowly, that everywhere people use the word *ungrammatical* they mean something different, and the differences are not noise.

The people inside the story keep failing to see what the camera sees. Hankamer doesn't see his own intuition fail. Postal doesn't either. The 2002 *Cambridge Grammar* repeats the failure. So does Cinque. The asterisk passes from hand to hand, getting heavier each time, and nobody picks it up to look at it.

### The turn

GPT-4, in 2024, is asked to rate *someone whose had left*. It says 6 out of 7. Brett searches a corpus and finds the construction in the wild. The sentence the field's most respected syntacticians have been starring for fifty-one years is something speakers actually use.

That is the moment the reader cannot un-see. Not because GPT-4 is the hero, it isn't, it is just a recording device. The hero of this scene is the asterisk, finally caught in the act. We have been watching trained, careful, honest linguists fail to hear their own language for fifty-one years. What does that say about every other star they have drawn? What does that say about the word *ungrammatical* on the cover of the book the reader is holding?

This is the moment, two-thirds in, where the reader confronts something strange about themselves. They have been trusting these stars their whole life. They drew some, in school. They corrected their children. The book has been gentle until now. Now it is asking the reader whether they know what they have been doing.

### The resolution

After the turn, the case closes fast. The morality, politics, fashion, swearing, codeswitching chapters are no longer a parade of analogies, they are a closing argument. Of course these domains use *ungrammatical* differently, the asterisk was never doing what we thought. The HPC chapter then arrives quietly, late, after the case is built, as the only model that fits what we have just watched. The reader recognises it because they have already seen it.

The last shot is a return to the classroom. Same student, same whiteboard. Brett erases the asterisk. The road is long long. He writes it without the star. The student does not look surprised. She has known all along.

### Why this arc serves the HPC argument

HPC theory is hard to teach by exposition because the moral of the story (that categories are clusters of co-occurring properties without an essence) sounds either trivial or slippery on the page. It only lands when the reader has watched essence fail in a specific case, slowly, with named people who were trying their best. The whose sequence does that. By the time the model arrives, the reader has felt the cluster account in their bones, and the formalism is recognition rather than instruction.

### Chapters in proposed order

1. The road is long long. The classroom cold open, ending on the asterisk.
2. Schleicher's mark and Sweet's flip. The asterisk's secret history.
3. Ross's islands. The boundary phenomena that broke the simple story, treated as a Morris-shaped scene with Ross, age 28, on camera.
4. Where grammar starts and where it stops. Merge of current 09 (no-grammar), 13 (varieties), 16 (codeswitching). Ethan, Riau, Tomo, dialect lines, all in one tightening sequence.
5. The generative case for the prosecution. Current 07, but with Chomsky as a named honest person at MIT in 1957, not a faceless ideologue. The strongest version of the essentialist position, charitably rendered.
6. Becoming and unbecoming. Merge of current 12 (becoming ungrammatical), 17 (impossible), 19 (efficiency). The mechanism case: how grammars change, what they cannot do, why they settle where they settle.
7. Real patterns and constructions. Current 10 plus current 15's constructional material. Hurford on camera. Doctorow's email.
8. **The whose chapter.** The turn. Hankamer 1973, Postal 1973, *Cambridge Grammar* 2002, Cinque 2020, GPT-4 2024, Brett in the wild. Folds in current 20.
9. The judgments domain. Merge of current 04 (morality), 05 (intuitions), 06 (politics), 14 (fashion), 18 (swearing). Now no longer a parade of analogies but a closing argument: every domain where humans say *ungrammatical*, refracted through what we just saw the linguists do.
10. The cluster. The HPC model, finally, arriving as recognition.
11. Back to the classroom. Brett erases the asterisk. The student does not look surprised.

Eleven chapters. One arc. One turn. One last shot.
