## Chapter 02: How grammatical can you get?

### Strengths

The Mr. Boyko anecdote - high school computer class, Jamie Ramsden, identical bubble-sort code, the cheeky 50% gambit - is the chapter's heart. Real, specific, with people in it. It earns the abstract point that comes after.

The dog-on-a-gradient examples (a) through (g) - "The big black dog fetched sticks at the park" sliding through "Black big dog no fetch park" to "Dog big no stick no no park fetch" - are pedagogically beautiful. A reader can feel the worsening on their own teeth. The follow-up table where each sentence has only one error is even better, because it isolates the variable.

The progressive-aspect history (Yesterday I was on hunting → a-hunting → hunting) gives the change a body. The frequency chart of "be trying" from 1560 to 1690 is the kind of evidence-on-the-page that a trade reader will appreciate. The Italian commuter who would never bother with *vado camminando al lavoro* is a small flash of the right kind of anchoring.

The *how beautiful is she?* / *how beautiful she is!* swap, going from flat to infatuated, is clean.

### Major concerns

The categorization detour - Pistorius blades, the Ontario pit-bull law, the Massachusetts burrito-vs-sandwich case, Brancusi's *Bird in Space* - is a spectacular set of stories that has very little to do with grammaticality. Each is its own essay. Together, they hijack the chapter for several pages and never come back to language. If the point is "categories are sometimes fuzzy," that's two sentences plus one example, not four legal cases.

The toy generative grammar feels like a textbook escapee. It's not wrong, it's just not vivid. We get the rules and the tree but no sense of why a reader should care. Compare it to how the Italian commuter example does the same work in a paragraph.

The Sampson "everything is grammatical" treatment is gestural ("they think the whole idea of grammaticality is nonsense") and unfair to either side. Sampson's actual position deserves at least a paraphrased sentence.

The chapter has placeholders ("[additional examples needed]"), an unfinished list ending with "It may", and a section-without-title, which suggests this draft hasn't yet found its outline.

The Adrian Tchaikovsky / *Children of Time* anecdote about reading-the-same-book-differently is doing nothing the bubble-sort already did better. Cut it.

### Priority fixes

1. The categorization section needs to be cut, or radically compressed (one paragraph, one example), or moved to its own short chapter on category fuzziness.
2. Decide whether the toy generative grammar pulls its weight. If yes, motivate it (why are we building this?). If no, cut it.
3. Fill in or strike the "[additional examples needed]" tags and the truncated list.
4. The progressive-aspect section is good enough that the chapter could organize around it as the central case study of gradient grammaticality across time, with the dog gradient as the synchronic counterpart.

### One concrete suggestion

For the progressive aspect, find a person. The ~1560 first-attestation in the Early English Books Online corpus - "while they were trying by the sword" - was written by someone, in a particular book, about a particular siege or argument. Walk the reader to that book. Who was the author? Was it routine prose or, perhaps, a place where he was reaching for a meaning his standard syntax couldn't quite handle? A construction's first known fossil deserves the kind of attention paleontologists give *Tiktaalik*. Right now we only see it as a graph point. Show us the page it lived on.
