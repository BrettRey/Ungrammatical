## Chapter 21: A long, long way

### Strengths
This is the most successful chapter in the book. It opens with a Hollies lyric ("It's a long, long road / From which there is no return"), then asks why we can't say *the road is long long*, then takes the reader on a careful, satisfying historical tour through Old and Middle English to explain how the reduplicative-intensification construction got into the prenominal slot but not the predicative one.

The explanation is excellent: Old English couldn't put two adjectives in a row at all; compound nouns like *burggeat* (city-gate) provided a sneaky way to have two modifiers; speakers gradually reanalyzed compounds as adjective-noun sequences; this opened up multiple-adjective NPs; the *small little* and *long long* uses then emerged in the 1500s-1600s; the predicative position never got the same opening because nothing analogous to compound-noun reanalysis happened there. This is a real philological story, told with empirical anchors at every step.

The cross-linguistic survey at the end (Jamaican Creole *blakblak*, North-East Ambae *qi-qilodo*) is exactly the right closing move: showing that the asymmetry isn't a human-cognitive universal, just a historical English (and Japanese, and Russian) accident. The Pullum suggestion ("Geoff Pullum suggested I look at Jamaican Creole") is the right kind of intellectual hospitality.

The Turkish-pope example from Pullum (*John Paul very very good*) is the chapter's fun sociolinguistic anecdote, with the L1-Turkish *çok çok* doing the work and a calque carrying the construction over.

The boxed "Breaking it down" section (lines 28-37) does pedagogical work: it pauses to explain the grammar terms (attributive modifier vs predicative complement) for a reader who might not have them. This is the right move for a trade book.

### Major concerns
The chapter has a strange duplicate at lines 1-3: `\chapter{A long, long way}` followed by `\newpage` followed by `\section{A long long way}`. The chapter title appears twice (with different punctuation). One should be cut.

The DALL-E images (lines 110, 117) are fine for a draft but a non-illustrator reader will note that the *blackbird* image is doing pedagogical work that line drawings could do better, and that *folks near a burggeat* is essentially a Tolkien-image-bank illustration. This isn't a deal-breaker, but consider whether the figures earn their place.

There are several `%`-commented passages that contain interesting material (the *īsernbȳrne* example at line 138; the *six hundred* example at line 142; the Tyrkkö medical-writing material at line 248). The author has clearly cut these for length, which is the right instinct, but some of them are doing work the chapter doesn't quite do without them. Decide which can come back.

The Russian example (lines 60-69) has *krasivye krasivye* with a comma between them; this is a slightly different construction (perhaps right-dislocation rather than reduplication). The chapter doesn't address whether the comma changes anything. A Russian-speaking reader might object.

The chapter's argument that English students "extrapolate from the evidence they've seen in their time learning English" (line 244) is reasonable but could be developed. The students laugh at *she's good good* because they've never seen two adjectives back-to-back after *is*, but they accept *good good person* because they've seen plenty of pre-noun adjective stacks. That's a clean account; the chapter could state it more explicitly.

### Priority fixes
- Resolve the duplicate chapter/section header at lines 1-3.
- Decide on the figures (line drawings or DALL-E art).
- Address the Russian example: is the comma a problem? Does the same construction exist without it? If not, replace with a Russian example that's purely reduplicative.
- The closing claim ("we're socialized to do so" line 236) is right but understated. Use it as a chapter conclusion.

### One concrete suggestion
This chapter is already very good. The main suggestion is to leave it alone. If anything, expand the conclusion. The chapter currently ends with "this could be the key that opens up other functions to multiple adjectives" (line 248), which is too tentative. The chapter has earned a more confident ending: pre-nominal multi-adjective constructions are possible in English because of a specific chain of historical accidents (the OE compound-noun loophole, the loss of declensions, the rise of fixed word order, the growing flexibility of the determiner-modifier slot). Predicative multi-adjective constructions never had a similar opening. The cross-linguistic survey shows this isn't a universal cognitive constraint. So the asymmetry is a real pattern but a contingent one. That's the HPC argument in miniature: a homeostatic property cluster of grammatical English-as-of-2026 that could have been otherwise and may yet become otherwise. End the chapter on that.

### Why this chapter is the book's best
It does what every chapter in the book aspires to do: take a small, vivid example (a Hollies lyric, an L2 student's laughter), pose a real question, walk the reader through an empirical and historical answer, end with a cross-linguistic check, and arrive at a payoff that supports the book's larger argument. Other chapters either skip the empirical work, or pad with LLM-fill, or fail to land the payoff. This one earns every step.

If the rest of the book were as confident, structured, and patient as this chapter, it would be a remarkable trade book. The other chapters can learn from this one.

### Rhetoric that works
"There's never any reaction after the first, but laughter inevitably follows the second in a way it never does with other grammatical infelicities. These students just know that it's wrong." (line 26). Brett-as-classroom-teacher is the book's most credible voice. He has watched fifty international students laugh at the same construction. He has tested the example in the wild. The chapter earns the abstract conclusion because the empirical anchor is the linguist's classroom.

### Rhetoric that doesn't
"In other words, we're not programmed to prefer one kind of duplication over the other. We're socialized to do so." (line 236). This is true but understated. The chapter has been doing harder, more interesting work; this one-liner could be expanded into a paragraph that says clearly that grammar is shaped by history, not by evolution-installed constraints. As written, the punchline lands but doesn't ring.
