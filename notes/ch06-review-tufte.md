## Chapter 06: Prescriptivism on the right and on the left

### Strengths
The German contrast is the chapter's best move: in English the left wants gender-neutral nouns, in German the left wants gender-specific nouns. That single observation reframes the politics of language as locally contingent. The Nunberg paraphrase ("racists don't use slurs because they're derogative; slurs are derogative because they're the words that racists use") is sharp and quotable.

The disability-language paragraph is sharper than most of the chapter: the named institutions (Canadian Federation of the Blind, CNIB), the syntactic gloss (fusion of functions in nominal), and the Nunberg-paralleling closing line are doing real work in a small space.

### Major concerns
This is one of the shortest chapters in the book (eight kilobytes, ~40 paragraphs) and it shows. It is more sketch than chapter. There is no figure, no table, no diagram, no example list, and no quantitative grounding for any of its claims.

The opening four paragraphs cite Lakoff 1973, Miller 1976, Spender 1980, Cameron 1998, Maggio 1998, Vervecken 2012, Diewald 2018, Neiman 2023, eight references in roughly 200 words, with no quotation, statistic, or specific claim attached to any of them. This is reference-padding, not argument. The English-vs-German irony is the chapter's whole load-bearing claim and it is delivered as one paragraph that names no concrete forms.

The Schiller / Beaver / Stanley section at the end (lines 29-41) is verbatim copy from a chat or email thread: "you propose," "your paper's section," "citing them might enrich your argument" reads as if a reviewer's notes were pasted into the chapter and never integrated.

There is no figure, no example list, and the chapter never lays out the parallel structure (English left = neutralise; German left = mark; English left = police slurs; English right = police pronouns; English right = police "they" usage) it is gesturing at.

### Priority fixes
1. Build the four-quadrant table that this chapter's argument is begging for (see suggestion).
2. Cut the Beaver/Stanley/Schiller paragraphs (lines 29-41). They are review notes pasted whole. If the points are worth keeping, integrate them into prose.
3. Replace the citation-stuffed opening with a single concrete example: a *Stylebook* entry from 1970 vs the same entry in 2020, side by side, showing the change. Or a German style-guide note about *-In* endings shown next to an APA Style note about gender-neutral.
4. The chapter is currently about half its needed length to make its argument. Either expand or fold it into Chapter 4 (Morality), which already covers prescriptivism from a left/right angle.

### One concrete suggestion
Build a single 2x2 table titled "Who polices what."

Columns: English | German
Rows: Left-leaning prescriptive impulse | Right-leaning prescriptive impulse

Cell (Eng, Left): Eliminate gendered nouns ("waitress" -> "server"). Eliminate ableist metaphor ("blind to," "tone-deaf"). Police slurs.
Cell (Eng, Right): Resist singular *they* with named referents. Resist new pronouns. Defend traditional grammar.
Cell (Ger, Left): *Add* gendered noun forms to make women visible (Studierende, Lehrer*innen). Police slurs.
Cell (Ger, Right): Resist gender-asterisk and Binnen-I forms. Defend traditional grammar.

Below the table, a single diagonal arrow from (Eng, Left) to (Ger, Left), labelled: "Both call this anti-sexist."

The reader sees in one glance the chapter's central paradox: same political position, opposite linguistic moves, depending on the language's morphology. The table is the chapter's argument. Everything else is commentary.

A second small grid alongside, showing four word categories (slurs, gender, ability, race) by two register dimensions (use vs. mention), would let the chapter say what it currently asserts only loosely about the use/mention distinction.

For a chapter this short, two well-built tables would constitute roughly half its content and would carry roughly all of its argument. Right now the chapter has none.
