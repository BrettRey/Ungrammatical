## Chapter 19: Communicative efficiency

### Strengths
This is one of the chapter's most disciplined chapters in the book. It engages a real, current literature (Futrell, Gildea, et al. on dependency-locality and efficiency) and walks the reader through the empirical claims with concrete examples and counted dependency distances. The chapter's central move — that conventional word orders evolve to minimize dependency distance, and that "ungrammatical" word orders are usually the ones that violate convention without an efficiency motivation — is an argument the chapter actually makes, supported by a real metric.

The Highway 427 left-side exit analogy at line 40 is excellent. It does the analogy work the book has been promising at various points: a real-world illustration of why a system might generally prefer one configuration but accommodate the opposite when complexity demands it. That parallel — driving on the right vs. heavy NP shift — gives the reader a clean intuition for the linguistic argument without overreaching.

The Heavy NP Shift example (lines 53-66) is genuinely good. Counting dependency distances (9, 20, 10) lets the reader see why the shifted version is preferred in some cases. The progressive trash-out series (a) through (g) at lines 86-93 is teaching at its best: the reader feels the resolution from (f) to (g) and then is told why.

The CNN/passive example at line 76 ("a convoluted neural network architecture is formed by…") is a precise empirical case for when the prescriptively-disliked passive is the better choice. That neutralizes the Orwellian anti-passive prejudice without making a polemical point about it. Becker would approve: the chapter doesn't attack the prescriptive tradition, it just shows the data.

The Gildea 2007 alternating-dependent analysis is a good citation and the closing observation that English is close to the alternating ideal is a real theoretical claim.

### Major concerns
The chapter is short and ends abruptly at line 132. There's no section close, no transition to the next chapter, no return to the book's broader argument. The reader exits the chapter informed but unsure where they've landed.

The bigger structural concern: this chapter's argument is the most generative-friendly argument in the book. Futrell's efficiency program is widely cited by formal linguists; dependency-distance minimization is a serious quantitative finding; the chapter's framing of word-order regularities as efficiency-driven is broadly compatible with most contemporary syntactic theory. A generative reader will read this chapter and find very little to disagree with. That's not a criticism — it's a strength — but the chapter doesn't quite say what it's arguing for or against. It just presents the efficiency story as a fact.

A reader who has just been told in chapter 17 that the book is skeptical of strong UG claims might wonder how this chapter relates. Efficiency-driven word-order regularities are not UG, but they are real constraints on what humans find easy to produce and parse. The chapter could connect this to its larger argument: ungrammaticality is partly an efficiency phenomenon, and the constraints on what's "possible" in human language might come from production/parsing pressures rather than innate principles. That move would tie this chapter to chapters 7 (generative) and 17 (impossible languages) and give the reader a coherent picture of the book's overall position.

The footnote at line 11 ("This chapter draws heavily on Futrell 2020") is an honest signal but the footnote ends up doing more work than it should. The chapter is heavily indebted to a single paper, and it shows. Some independent thought, or extension of Futrell's argument to one of the book's other concerns (gradience, satiation, dialects), would make the chapter feel more like Brett's own.

The Japanese example at lines 32-35 is given in unglossed Japanese script. Most readers will not be able to parse this. Add a gloss line.

### Priority fixes
- Add a closing section that connects efficiency to the book's larger argument. One paragraph: efficiency pressures generate apparent grammatical constraints; these constraints are real but extragrammatical; this is part of why "ungrammatical" picks out a cluster of overlapping mechanisms rather than a single property.
- Gloss the Japanese examples.
- The chapter currently has only one citation (Futrell 2020) doing most of the work. Add Hawkins (one of the founding figures of efficiency-based syntactic typology) and Gibson (whose dependency-locality theory is the conceptual ancestor of Futrell's program).
- The prediction-is-grammar sketch from chapter 5 (predictive processing) and the efficiency story here are siblings: both treat human production/parsing constraints as the source of apparent grammatical regularities. Cross-reference.
- Source-ground the dependency-distance counts. Verify the 9, 20, 10 figures are accurate.

### One concrete suggestion
Add a two-paragraph closing section that uses the chapter's findings to advance the book's central HPC argument. The argument: dependency-distance minimization is a real, measurable, cross-linguistically-attested constraint on what humans easily produce. It's not a syntactic rule in any classical sense — there's no UG principle that says "minimize dependency distance" — but it predicts and explains a great deal of cross-linguistic word-order variation. This is exactly the kind of soft constraint that an HPC view of grammaticality predicts: pressures from production/parsing/social-coordination produce statistical regularities that we then experience as feelings of grammaticality. So when a reader feels that "I gave to my best friend for Christmas a book" is awkward, they're not consulting an internal grammar rule; they're tracking a violation of efficiency.

That move ties the chapter back to the predictive-processing story in chapter 5 and to the impossible-languages discussion in chapter 17, and it gives a generative reader a clear picture of where the book stands. It also makes the chapter feel like Brett's chapter rather than Futrell's. Right now it's an honest, careful summary of someone else's program; with the closing argument, it becomes part of an overall position. A generative reader will respect both, but only one closes the chapter on the book's own thesis.
