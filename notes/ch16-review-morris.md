## Chapter 16: Unravelling babel (codeswitching)

### Strengths

The toddler scenes are gold. Reynolds and Yoko move from Tokyo to Toronto in 2003, in the middle of the SARS outbreak. Yoko speaks Japanese with their son Tomo. Reynolds also speaks Japanese with him, except when reading. Reynolds's mother speaks only English. Six months in, in the kitchen, Tomo turns to his grandmother and translates for her what his parents have been saying. He's three or so, and he's already mapped not only "Japanese vs. English" but "who understands which." That is the chapter's argument as a kitchen scene. Reynolds is clearly moved retelling it ("I was amazed"). The reader is too.

Heike Wiese's daughter Inya is the chapter's twin scene. Three years old, refusing to speak English to her British in-laws because for Inya, English is "the Daddy register," not a separate language. The realization that "language" for a child can be parsed as "register-of-particular-person" rather than as language-in-the-Linnaean-sense is the chapter's hidden thesis. Wiese's coinage *com-sits* (communicative situations) is not as catchy as it could be, but the example earns it.

The chapter's working argument (that "language" is just a particularly broad com-sit, and that switching between Japanese and English is grammatical inside a bilingual com-sit but ungrammatical outside it) is real and important. It connects the codeswitching domain to swearing, register, dialect, and politics.

### Major concerns

The chapter is a stub of 47 lines, and the first 15 of those are a literal bullet-list of "Items to make in this chapter." That bullet list reads like a planning document, not a chapter draft. Then a duplicate quote: the long Wiese quote about Inya appears once at line 13 (inside a bullet) and again at lines 38-40 (as a block quote). One should be cut. The Smith 2019 quote also appears in two places (lines 7 and 32), nearly verbatim.

The Tomo-in-the-kitchen scene is the chapter's gold. It's just two short paragraphs (24-30). It deserves five times the space, with specific detail: what was Reynolds's mother making? what time of day was it? what was the Japanese sentence Tomo translated and what was the English version he gave? The reader needs the specifics that turn an anecdote into a documentary scene.

There is no engagement here with the canonical codeswitching literature: Carol Myers-Scotton's Matrix Language Frame, Penelope Gardner-Chloros's *Code-switching* (both flagged on the advisory board list), Shana Poplack's classic data. A trade-book chapter on codeswitching can use a light touch, but it should at least name and engage one canonical framework before walking past it. Right now the chapter relies entirely on Wiese 2023 and Grieve 2023.

The Grieve quote at the end is unmotivated and untranslated; it sits at the bottom of the file like a research note. "Situational diversity" and "situational complexity" need to be unpacked or cut.

The Pirahã item from the bullet list is dropped without development. So is the Babel framing: "Now the whole earth had one language and the same words" promises a chapter on multilingualism in human history, but the chapter delivers a paragraph on toddlers.

On the protagonists question: this chapter has its protagonists almost right. Tomo and Inya are real children, both translating between languages and people in ways that adults find startling. Use them. They are the chapter. The animal communication paragraph (vervets and green monkeys having one-to-one signs) is a nice contrast and earns its keep. Carol Myers-Scotton, Shana Poplack, and Lily Wong Fillmore are missing protagonists who could anchor the chapter's adult-codeswitching half.

### Priority fixes

1. Delete the bullet-list opening.
2. Delete one of the two Wiese quotations and one of the two Smith quotations.
3. Build out the Tomo scene. It deserves real space.
4. Engage at least one canonical codeswitching framework (Matrix Language Frame or its descendants).
5. Develop or cut the Grieve framing.
6. Mark stub status in STATUS.md.

### One concrete suggestion

Open inside the kitchen in 2003. Toronto. SARS. Reynolds, Yoko, his mother, three-year-old Tomo. Make the scene specific. Then, the moment when Tomo turns to his grandmother and translates. Hold there. From that scene, ask: what does this toddler know that Carol Myers-Scotton's Matrix Language Frame is trying to formalize? Can she capture what Tomo did? Then move to Inya as the parallel scene from another country, another linguist's family. The chapter has its scaffolding right; what it needs is to lean on the toddlers and let the academic machinery follow them.
