## Chapter 03: Being grammatical isn't always enough

### Strengths
The opening "I have 30 years" / "presser 2" pair is gold: same problem from both sides of the language pair. The text-message anecdote ("I still at the gym") is the kind of intimate evidence that wins trust from a non-linguist reader. The Pullum-via-Osman example (Henk and Trouble) is a real find: a literate, witty, mass-market novel disproving a 1970s syntactic rule. That's exactly the kind of evidence that convinces a non-linguist that grammaticality is more interesting than they thought. The Riau Indonesian and Pirahã pair makes the abstract point that languages differ in what they allow embedded clauses to do, while still letting humans communicate.

### Major concerns
This is the chapter most visibly mid-construction. Section 1 is titled "ideas" and contains literal notes-to-self. Section 4 (Conventions) tees up Anne Carson and then drops her. Section 5 (Reasons) contains placeholders ("[additional examples needed]"). Section 6 (Pragmatics) is a single quote with no surrounding argument. Section 5.1.2 (passives and dangling modifiers) contains the bracketed query "[is this true]?" still in the text. The "Russian dolls" section title doesn't connect to the content (the dolls metaphor for clausal embedding never lands). Multiple section breaks ("-- --") suggest unfinished prose.

The 1970s passage ("Back in the 1970s, when bell-bottoms were groovy and disco was king...") is the worst kind of register break. It's also factually weird: "they didn't know much about how context affects language use" insults a generation of pragmaticists who very much did know. Halliday was writing about information structure; Prince's Yale work was the 70s. The strawman undermines an otherwise good point.

The "darling, you are loved by me" example, while charming, leans on a very obvious gag (b-grade romance novel) where the chapter would benefit from a more delicate example. The chapter is also doing a lot of mind-reading of writing teachers who hate the passive without yet making the argument that information structure governs the choice.

### Priority fixes
- Cut "ideas" section header and integrate or kill the notes (Haspelmath, Zwicky & Pullum) into actual prose.
- Decide what "Russian dolls" means and either name the section better or build the metaphor (matryoshka opening to reveal the next clause).
- Cut placeholder bracketed queries and "[additional examples needed]" before any reader sees them.
- Rewrite the "1970s" passage. Drop the bell-bottoms framing. Linguists in the 70s did know about context; what they hadn't yet done was integrate it into formal accounts of acceptability. That's the real story.
- Anne Carson is teased and not delivered. Either give her a paragraph (and an example) or cut.
- Section 6 (Pragmatics) is currently a placeholder. Either write it or cut.

### One concrete suggestion
Reorganize around three claims, in order: (1) Grammatical sentences can fail because of convention (I have 30 years; presser 2). (2) Grammatical sentences can fail because of information structure (Henk and Trouble; the by-phrase passives). (3) Grammatical sentences can be socially policed even though they are correct (passives, danglers). Each claim gets a section. Each section opens with one example, then walks through what the rule actually is, then shows where the prescriptive tradition got it wrong. The Riau Indonesian and Pirahã material can move to a different chapter (perhaps 13, where cross-language variation is the topic). This chapter should be tight, four to six pages, with three sharp claims.

### Rhetoric that works
"\textit{I still at the gym}." This one line, dropped into a chapter about syntax, is the book's voice. A real human, telling on himself, making a joke that lands because he and his wife share enough English to know which form is the joke and which form is the question.

### Rhetoric that doesn't
"In those days, linguists would slap an asterisk on that second sentence faster than you could say 'Stayin' Alive.'" This kind of forced period-set whimsy is what an LLM produces when it tries to be folksy. The actual joke is in your wife's text message, not in disco. Strip the sitcom dressing and trust the example.
