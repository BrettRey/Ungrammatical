## Chapter 11: Seeing grammar in the brain

### Strengths

The chapter title is one of the best in the book. "Seeing grammar in the brain" is exactly the promise a trade reader wants from a neurolinguistics chapter, and it's a Zimmer-class title.

The two findings that are present - Just 1993 on pupillary dilation as a measure of processing load, and Divjak 2024 on heart rate variability dropping when listeners hear article errors, with larger drops for L1-accent errors - are exactly the kind of physiological-correlate-of-grammar evidence the chapter wants. "When our minds work, our pupils swell" is the chapter's only sentence and it's the right kind of sentence.

### Major concerns

The chapter is a stub. Literally eight lines, two findings, three citations.

This is one of three chapters explicitly flagged as needing to be built out (per the task brief). What follows are notes on what should fill it.

### Priority fixes

The chapter as it stands cannot be reviewed for content. It can be sketched for what it should contain.

### One concrete suggestion (and a sketch of what belongs)

The neurolinguistic evidence on grammaticality is some of the most reader-vivid science in the field. A working chapter should have:

1. **The N400 and P600 ERP waveforms.** Kutas and Hillyard (1980) showed the N400 dipping when *I take coffee with cream and SOCKS* hits the brain. Osterhout and Holcomb (1992) found the P600 spike when subject-verb agreement fails. Both are real, replicable, and have a visual signature on a strip chart. Walk the reader through one experiment, slowly. The cap of electrodes, the gel, the participant clicking through sentences, the timer triggering a stimulus, the deflection in the trace 400 or 600 ms later. That deflection is the closest neuroscience has come to seeing a feeling of ungrammaticality. (Some of this work is gestured at in chapter 5; this chapter should host it properly.)

2. **Pupillometry.** Just 1993 is cited; the chapter should describe what the pupil actually does. Eye-tracker, infrared light, real-time millimeter measurement of the pupil dilating about 0.2 mm when working memory is taxed. Ben Zubek, others, have run this on garden-path sentences.

3. **Aphasia case studies.** Broca's patients can produce content words but lose grammatical morphology; Wernicke's patients produce fluent but grammatically anomalous speech. Two patients, two scenes, one stroke each, what they could and couldn't say. This is where the brain's regional commitments to grammar become physical.

4. **Imaging.** fMRI work by Friederici and colleagues on the anatomy of syntactic processing in real time.

5. **Single-neuron recordings.** Chapter 5 alludes to the Nature 2024 hippocampal-sequence study; pull that here as a centerpiece, fully developed, since the chapter is supposed to be about seeing grammar in the brain.

Most importantly, find one patient. Aphasia stories - a stroke survivor who can sing fluently but cannot form a question, or who can name objects but cannot put two together with the right inflection - are some of the most affecting material in cognitive science. Find one in Howard Kushner's *Stuck on Words* or in Caplan's case histories. A reader who watches a real person struggle with grammar after a stroke will understand what the brain is doing for them better than any number of N400 graphs alone.
