## Chapter 12: Becoming (un)grammatical

### Strengths

The *very* / *much* story is the chapter's gift to the book. A late-13th-century French loanword arrives, sniffs around for work, and over the next two centuries muscles *much* out of its territory in front of plain adjectives, then specializes complementary. The 1481 Caxton passage with its Middle English original ("a moche fair chirche and ryche") is the kind of text-archaeology that does the chapter's argument better than any abstract claim could. A reader can feel the language change between the two paragraphs.

The John Payne discovery is also a genuine moment. Reynolds is writing a paper on *much*, sends it to John Payne (one of the CGEL authors), and Payne notices that *much* is an NPI in some constructions but not in adjective phrases like *much bigger*. That's a co-authored discovery story between two specific people. The chapter then traces *much*'s NPI history through the EEBO corpus and finds the polarity restriction crystallizing in the early 1800s. Hard work, real numbers, real corpus, real names.

The Jespersen's-cycle account of French *ne\dots pas* is well-told. The detail that *pas* originally meant "step," *point* meant "point," *personne* meant "person," and that *Je ne marche pas* once meant *I won't walk a step*: that's the kind of etymology that travels.

### Major concerns

There are two chapters glued together. Line 179 begins "\chapter{The onward march of \textit{do}-support}". This is supposed to be a separate chapter (probably 12 or 13), but it's currently inside file 12. Either it should be moved to its own file, or the new \chapter{} should be downgraded to \section{}.

The do-support sub-chapter (lines 179-222) is also where the chapter's prose collapses. The whole stretch from line 187 onward reads as LLM essay-mode: "These constructions, now so integral to the fabric of Modern English," "It paved the way for the complex system of auxiliaries," "It is a testament to the resilience and adaptability of the English language, which has weathered countless transformations over the centuries and yet remains a vital, expressive medium for communication." That last sentence is pure ChatGPT outro. So is "we are all just witnesses to the never-ending dance of language, participants in a global conversation that stretches back millennia." Cut every word of that closing run.

The "Words" section at line 155 is a single sentence and an unanswered question. The "Assertion and presupposition" section at line 159 is two paragraphs and a Cohn-Gordon quotation. Both look like notes-to-self, not chapter prose.

On the protagonists question: this chapter has its named humans in the right places. Otto Jespersen, William Caxton, John Payne are present. Israel (the linguist) is cited and quoted. Reynolds himself is in the room writing a paper, sending drafts to Payne, getting feedback. That model (Reynolds the working linguist sending email to a colleague who finds something neither had seen) is the book's best mode and it should be used more often.

But the do-support section has no people. Where are the Early Modern speakers experimenting with auxiliary *do*? Where is Shakespeare reaching for *I do not doubt* against the older *I doubt not*? Where is the modern teenager who said *I wonder what did he say* and got corrected by an older relative? The argument is good. The chapter just keeps reaching for "linguists have suggested\dots" instead of putting any specific person in the room.

### Priority fixes

1. Decide whether the do-support material is its own chapter. If so, split it into a separate file. If not, downgrade the \chapter{} to \section{}.
2. Strip the LLM-flavored closing of the do-support material (lines 212 onward).
3. Build out the "Words" section or cut it.
4. Build out the "Assertion and presupposition" section or move to NOTES.md.
5. The McWhorter quotation about language as a wiki is fine, but the paragraphs around it (213-216) are the chapter's most generic prose. Replace with one specific Shakespeare-or-other example of *do*-support emerging in a single text.

### One concrete suggestion

Open with Caxton at his press in 1481. The man imported the printing press to England. He needed to choose how to spell things, how to construct sentences, what to keep from older translations and what to update. Show him at the case, choosing whether to print "moche fair" or "very fair." That single moment of typesetting decided more about the future of English than any grammarian. Then trace *very* down through the next century and a half. By the 1600s, *moche* has become *much*, *much* has retreated, *very* dominates the territory. Then move the do-support story under that umbrella as a parallel case: another Early Modern restructuring, watched from inside one or two specific writers' workshops. The chapter as a whole has a real argument (grammar changes from inside specific historical moments by specific writers under specific pressures); it needs a Caxton-shaped scene to anchor it.
