## Chapter 07: The Generation Gap

### Strengths
This is the chapter where the book finally engages generative grammar directly, and the opening is reasonable. You start with the bare-bones idea — discrete combination, a lexicon, recursive structure — and you take a few pages to walk a non-specialist reader through what generative grammarians actually meant by "grammatical." That's the right move. The dust-storm/cosmic example is concrete enough to ground the abstract claim, and the demonstration that order of combination matters (so left-to-right won't work) shows the reader why constituent structure was an actual discovery, not a fashion.

The Culicover example is also well-chosen. *Robin is likely / probable to be elected* is exactly the kind of pair that breaks naive rule-listing accounts, and presenting it before pivoting to the generative-semantics revolt is good rhetorical staging. A generative reader sees that you understand the empirical problems the field was trying to solve.

The Jim McCawley quote is an excellent piece of evidence. McCawley/Postal/Lakoff publicly declaring the grammaticality concept incoherent is a useful reminder that the doubts in this book aren't external to the field. They're internal. That's a much stronger position to argue from than "outside critic complains."

The Boeckx island-repair quote at the end is the chapter's strongest argumentative move. It catches generative grammar in a clear internal contradiction: if the grammar generates only grammatical strings, what is being repaired? That's a sharp point. Keep it.

### Major concerns
The chapter is 42 lines. It currently sketches the position you want to engage but doesn't engage it. The Boeckx quote arrives, lands, and the chapter ends. A generative reader will close the chapter agreeing with you that generative semantics had a point in 1972, but with no reason to think this matters today. Modern minimalism, derivational vs. representational debates, contemporary work on islands (Phillips, Sprouse, Hofmeister, etc.) — none of that appears.

This matters because chapter 7 is the chapter where the book is most exposed to a hostile reading. A generative reader will give you the rest of the book if this chapter takes their tradition seriously. They will not if this chapter says "Chomsky was wrong, McCawley was right, and we've moved on." Right now, that's roughly what the chapter says.

There's a smaller concern: the parenthetical "Let's set aside, for the moment, the circularity of these claims" at line 2 is exactly the kind of casual flag that signals to a generativist "I'm about to dismiss this." Either follow up on the circularity claim seriously or drop it.

The closing paragraph "the way the language evolves is towards systematicity, while at the same time allowing new irregularities into the system in a constant yin and yang of order and disorder" is too pretty for the work it's doing. It restates the chapter's claim as a slogan. It's also "yin and yang," which is the kind of phrase that signals you've stopped arguing.

The Boeckx critique is real but it's older than it sounds in your prose. By 2012, multiple responses had been offered (e.g., representational accounts that don't have the "repair" problem in the same way). A reader who knows the literature will think you've cherrypicked. Better: state the response and then say why it doesn't satisfy you.

### Priority fixes
- The chapter needs at least double its length to do the work the book needs from it. This is the central engagement with the position the book most needs to take seriously.
- Add a section on contemporary generative responses to gradient-acceptability data. Sprouse and Almeida's work is the obvious citation; if you haven't engaged it, you'll be accused of arguing with a 50-year-old version of the position.
- Cut "Let's set aside the circularity of these claims" or develop the circularity argument.
- Develop the Boeckx point. It's good, but as a single quote it does less work than it could.
- The chapter title "The Generation Gap" is cute but not quite earning its keep. The pun is on the generation-of-strings sense and the generational sense. Consider whether the title prepares the reader for what the chapter argues.

### One concrete suggestion
Bring forward the strongest version of the modern generative position before you criticize it. The reader currently meets late-1960s generativism, then meets its 1972 critics, then meets 2012 Boeckx. There's a 40-year gap. In that gap, generativism didn't sit still. It absorbed gradient-acceptability concerns, it engaged satiation effects, it developed sophisticated experimental methods, it produced work like Sprouse and Hornstein on islands, work that takes corpus and judgement data far more seriously than 1965 *Aspects* did. If you skip this work, your generative reader will believe (correctly) that you've argued with the version of their tradition that was easiest to refute. Spend two pages on the strongest contemporary version, then make your move. The Boeckx critique will land much harder if the reader has just seen the modern generativist's most sophisticated reply, and watched that reply fall short. The Becker rule again: persuade by understanding first.
