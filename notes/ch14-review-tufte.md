## Chapter 14: Grammar and glamour

### Strengths
This is one of the better-written chapters in the book. The Doctorow *even it's never approved* anecdote is sharply chosen: a known proficient writer using a construction that the chapter's author corrects in his own students. The personal-correspondence detail ("Yup. It's colloquial, but expressive, unambiguous, and colorful") gives it documentary weight.

The tracksuit analogy lands cleanly: an unfashionable item becomes fashion via context and intention, just as an "ungrammatical" form becomes deliberately stylish via context and intention. This is one of the book's clearest analogical frames.

The *enshittification* morphological breakdown (lines 42-58) is genuinely fun and informative: shit > shittify > shittific > shittificate > shittification > enshittification, with each suffix annotated for its source and function. This is the kind of small-step decomposition that earns its space in prose because it's already structurally a stack.

### Major concerns
The *enshittification* derivation cries out to be a stack diagram and is presented entirely in narrative. Each suffix attaches in order; the word grows; each step has linguistic origin and semantic function. This is structural data that the reader should see assembled.

The chapter has no figures whatsoever. For a chapter explicitly arguing that grammar works like fashion, the absence of any visual is striking. A grammar/fashion-parallel diagram would carry the chapter's central analogy.

Section "Tracksuits" makes the fashion-grammar analogy and ends without circling back to specific grammatical examples in the way the *even* and *enshittification* sections do. The *a pair glasses* aside in a footnote (line 28) is doing partitive-determinative work that should be in the body, not relegated to a note.

The chapter is short (10 KB, ~3500 words) but dense and good. It is one of the most clearly drafted chapters in the book; it deserves the visual investment.

### Priority fixes
1. Build a stack diagram for *enshittification* (see suggestion).
2. Build a small two-column grid showing the grammar-fashion parallel: an "ungrammatical" form vs. an "unfashionable" item, each in three contexts (correction, tolerance, status-marking).
3. Move the *pair glasses* / *dozen* footnote into the body. The partitive observation is too good for a footnote.
4. Decide on subsection numbering: chapter 14 currently uses \subsection rather than \section, which conflicts with the (unwritten) chapter scaffolding.

### One concrete suggestion
Build a single stacked-decoration diagram for *enshittification*.

A vertical stack, ground up:
- Brick 1 (base): *shit* (n. ME *scitte*, OE *scite*, PG *skit-*; "feces / metaphor for low-quality")
- Brick 2: *shit-ify* (verb-forming Lat. *-ficare*; "to make X")
- Brick 3: *shittific* (adj-forming Lat. *-icus*; "having property of")
- Brick 4: *shittificate* (verb-forming *-ate*; re-verbing)
- Brick 5: *shittification* (noun-forming *-tion*; nominalising)
- Brick 6: *en-shittification* (verb-forming *en-*; "making into a state of")

Each brick coloured by language of origin (English / Old English / Latin / Anglo-Norman). To the right of each brick, a one-line gloss of what each suffix does. To the left of each brick, the resulting form.

At the very top of the stack, a small rosette labelled "American Dialect Society Word of the Year, 2023." The diagram literally shows a derivational ornament.

A small thumbnail beside it: *embiggen* shown as a much shorter stack (just *en-* + *big* + *-en*), labelled "1996 The Simpsons; OED 2017; M-W 2018." The reader sees that *enshittification* succeeds in part because *embiggen* paved the way.

This chapter is doing the rare thing right (it is making a clear analogical argument with concrete examples) and would benefit enormously from one strong visual to anchor it. The chapter currently asks the reader to build the stack mentally; a single small diagram would do the work and free up another paragraph for the more interesting analytical points.
