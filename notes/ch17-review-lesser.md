## Chapter 17: Impossible languages

### Strengths

The chapter has a strong premise: as a way to clarify what natural-language grammar is, linguists have invented rules that no human language seems to use, and watched what happens. The four-line example of an English sentence under three different "impossible" perturbations (Even-Odd shuffle, Partial reverse, Tense hop) is the chapter's pedagogical center. The reader feels the strangeness directly, and the side-by-side display works.

The Bishop John Wilkins reference (1668's "Real Character and Philosophical Language") with the Okrent quote ("when you speak in concepts, it's too damn hard to say anything") is a small archival treasure that should anchor the chapter, not appear in a bullet list.

The Sampson 1975 quotes on prime-length languages and even-occurrence languages are exactly the right kind of provocation: are these "impossible" languages mathematically definable? If they are definable but never instantiated, what work is the impossibility doing?

The Moro citation ("An impossible language is a non-recursive one") is a substantive theoretical claim that deserves engagement.

The Kallini (2024) figure showing a continuum of perturbations from possible to impossible is the right kind of empirical anchor for a chapter on this topic.

The colorless-green-ideas reprise with the Pereira bigram-model finding is well-placed here as well as in chapter 15. (Though the duplication is a problem too.)

### Major concerns

The chapter is in two parts. Part one (lines 1-34) is an outline: a "Timeline" header, a list of historical positions and figures (Wilkins, Saussure, Chomsky hierarchy, islands, OVS, post-structuralism), a few extracted quotes. Part two (lines 35 onward) is the actual chapter, headed "Impossible languages."

Part one needs to either become prose or be deleted. As an outline embedded in the chapter file, it's confusing for a reader. Several of the items in the timeline ("Cultural-historical approaches," "Functionalism: language as a tool for communication," "Formalism: language as a mathematical-semiotic system") are placeholders that don't match the rest of the book's voice or scope.

The Sampson and Moro quotes are stacked without development. The Sampson quote runs to roughly 200 words and the chapter does not engage with it.

The chapter doesn't address what's at stake. If "impossible languages" really are impossible, the book has a strong claim (against Sampson). If they're merely never-attested but mathematically possible, the book has a different and weaker claim. A trade reader needs to be told which.

The chapter doesn't engage with the recent neural-network-and-impossible-languages literature (Mitchell & Bowers; Kallini et al. 2024 is cited but the work is not summarized), which is exactly the kind of contemporary content that would lift this chapter.

The "What could this be for?" line on line 49 is a fragment.

There is no "ingrammatical" or "anitgrammatical" in standard English. The opening sentence ("crafting systems that are ungrammatical -- ingrammatical, anitgrammatical -- by their very nature") plays at neologism but lands awkwardly because both invented words are not quite right.

### Priority fixes

1. Cut the outline (lines 1-34). The chapter starts at "\chapter{Impossible languages}" on line 35.
2. Decide what the chapter argues. Are impossible languages truly impossible, never-attested, or distinguishable-by-LLMs? Each answer suggests a different chapter.
3. Engage with Kallini 2024 substantively. The figure is reproduced; the empirical finding (LLMs distinguish impossible from possible languages, possibly indicating a real architectural fact) deserves a paragraph.
4. Develop the Wilkins / Borges / Tlön material. Imagined languages from literature are a great trade-book hook, currently relegated to a bullet.
5. Drop the "ingrammatical, anitgrammatical" attempt at coinage.

### One concrete suggestion

Worst sentence: "These linguistic constructs stretch the definition of language, inhabiting a space that is scarcely linguistic at all." This is the AI-explainer voice ("linguistic constructs," "stretch the definition," "inhabiting a space," "scarcely linguistic at all"), all gesture and no content. Cut. Replace with a sentence that says what the next paragraph is about to demonstrate.

Best sentence: "Sometimes, the best way to be clear about what something is is to imagine what it is not." Plain, direct, true, and exactly the right framing for the chapter. Reynolds in his book voice, setting up the impossible-languages move. Build the chapter from this sentence outward.
