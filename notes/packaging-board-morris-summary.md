# Packaging board summary: Errol Morris

## How the book handles its protagonists

The book's antagonists are abstractions, and that is the book's central storytelling problem. "Linguists in the 1970s" wear bell-bottoms in a comic mass; "Chomsky" enters as a quotation-bearer with no body, no office, no MIT chair, no human cost. "Hankamer and Postal" arrive as senior syntacticians who got something wrong in 1973, but the chapter they're in (5) does treat them as full people and that's where the book's writing comes alive. By contrast, "the prescriptivists" of chapter 6 and "the generativists" of chapter 7 never become individuals.

When a real human walks into a chapter, the prose changes. Herbie Hancock at the piano with Miles Davis. Reynolds's argument with the captain off Komodo. Eleven-month-old Ethan saying "ball." The Turkish woman calling Pope John Paul "very very good." Father Putnam and Sister O'Hern with a tape recorder in a Washington alley in 1953. Bert Bell mandating injury reports in 1947. Tomo translating Japanese for his English-only grandmother in 2003. Cory Doctorow emailing back "Yup, it's colloquial, but expressive, unambiguous, and colorful." Each of these scenes carries the chapter's argument better than the surrounding prose can. The book has the human texture; it just keeps surrounding it with abstraction.

## Where the texture is rich vs. flat

Rich: chapters 1 (Hancock), 5 (Hankamer/Postal/Mia), 9 (Ethan), 13 (Putnam/O'Hern), 14 (Doctorow), 15 (Pullum/Reynolds on the *Rubymar*), 16 (Tomo), 21 (Hollies, Turkish woman, students laughing).

Flat: chapters 2 (Sampson is a foil with no face), 4 (Bentham/Parfit/Scanlon are theory-vending machines), 6 (no named prescriptivist on either side), 7 (Chomsky as faceless ideologue), 8 (Horner gets one sentence), 11 (stub), 17 (Moro is a citation), 18 (stub, no protagonist at all), 19 (Futrell in a footnote), 20 (stub).

The pattern: when Reynolds is the witness to a moment (a friend, a child, an email, a classroom), the prose is good. When the chapter aspires to academic-register summary of a position, the prose collapses into LLM-tic. The duplicate sections, the apology lines, the "Certainly. I'll build out\dots" residue, the "It's a testament to the resilience and adaptability of the English language" closers all live in those flat chapters.

## One storytelling repair

Decide once, and signal to the reader, that the linguists who got things wrong (Chomsky 1957 on *colorless green ideas*, Hankamer/Postal 1973 on *whose*, the 1970s passive-by-phrase rule, the long Latin-influenced prescriptive-grammar tradition) were not villains and not fools but reasonable people who made the mistake anyone would make with the data and tools available. Then write every chapter with that decision in force. Treat each named opponent as someone working in good faith inside their moment, the way Reynolds already treats Hurford in chapter 10 and Pullum in chapter 15.

That single ethical commitment would do more than any individual edit. It would let the chapters with abstract antagonists (4, 6, 7, 17, 19) become chapters with named honest mistake-makers, each of whom has a face and a year and a reason for believing what they believed. It would also relax the moments where the book's prose tightens defensively. Once the antagonists are fellow travellers rather than fools, the chapter can afford to be specific. And once the chapter is specific, the human story finds itself.
