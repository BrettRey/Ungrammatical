## Chapter 13: Across language boundaries

### Strengths
The four-way distinction at lines 16-22 is exactly the right way to introduce dialect-relative grammaticality: one Standard English form, one stigmatized non-Standard form, and two genuinely impossible forms. A reader sees that "non-standard" is not the same as "ungrammatical." The Singlish examples, paired with the Wikipedia citation, do similar work for a different dialect.

The BIN/been distinction (lines 39-45) is the chapter's small masterpiece. *They've BIN living in Chicago* (long time, AAE) is camouflaged as *They've been living in Chicago* (any duration, Standard). A standard-English speaker hears the second; a competent AAE listener hears the first. The chapter says exactly the right thing: for something to be judged ungrammatical, it must first be noticed.

The Spears quotation about Black students policing each other's vernacular (line 47-50) is brave and important. It punctures the simple narrative that vernacular language is suppressed only by outside power.

The Putnam-O'Hern alley study at the chapter's end is a real find. The fact that listeners could rank speakers by status from dialect alone, even without exposure to those particular speakers, makes a sharp argument for distributed linguistic knowledge. The Labov caveat in the footnote does the right honest work.

### Strengths-of-architecture
The chapter is doing something hard: showing that grammar is a relational thing, not an absolute thing. Within Singlish, *this guy Singlish damn good leh* is grammatical; outside it, the same string isn't even visibly an English sentence. This is the HPC point in a different costume. The chapter mostly delivers it.

### Major concerns
The opening (lines 3-11) is a register catalogue: academic, legal, literary, film, song, poetry, advertising, sports, Scots, Singlish, pidgins, creoles. Eleven varieties listed in eight paragraphs, none developed. This is taxonomy without payoff. A non-linguist reader will skim. The Bill Withers line ("ain't no sunshine when she's gone") is a great example, but it's buried in a list.

The "Just how different is it?" section is good (four grammatical differences in *Ain't nobody*), but the chapter doesn't say what the reader should take from it. The point seems to be that even apparently small dialect differences are systematically deep, not just lexical. Make that explicit.

The "Grammar in bilingual speech" section (lines 79-92) is one example (German-Italian adjective placement) that ends with the author refusing to generalize ("I won't try to generalize from these two examples. The data seems inconsistent"). This is honest but unhelpful. Either give us three examples and a tentative generalization, or cut.

The "One language, multiple grammars" section is a stub: a one-word example (Balinese) and a six-item bullet list that has no surrounding prose. This will not survive into a published book.

The "Gendered language" section ends on a question ("is the competence mismatch somehow special?") and does not answer it. The list of eight gender-coded examples is fun but undeveloped.

The Microdialects section is in the wrong place. It's about social meaning extracted from linguistic features, which is the chapter's argument. But it's labeled "microdialects," and the Putnam-O'Hern study isn't really about micro-dialects; it's about how listeners decode social class from speech. Re-anchor or rename.

There is also a stray ". " at line 37 (a paragraph break with just a period) and the broken Italian gloss at lines 61-66 (the "to help-us" should probably be "to help.1pl" or similar; the format is inconsistent with the chapter's earlier glosses).

### Priority fixes
- Cut the eleven-variety opener to three: literary English (Withers as the example), Scots (or Singlish), and AAE. The chapter's argument doesn't need a taxonomy.
- Develop or cut the bilingual speech section. One example is not enough; the section trails off.
- Cut the "One language, multiple grammars" section unless it's going to be written. Right now it's a placeholder.
- Develop or cut the "Gendered language" section. The Furfey-1944 anchor is interesting; the question at the end deserves an answer.
- Move or rename the Microdialects section. It's the chapter's strongest empirical material; don't bury it under a confusing heading.
- Repair the empty paragraph at line 37 and tighten the Italian gloss formatting.

### One concrete suggestion
Build the chapter around the BIN/been distinction. Open with it. State the chapter's claim: grammaticality is variety-specific, and what's invisible to outsiders can be a working contrast for insiders. Then three case studies in increasing distance: (1) AAE within American English (BIN, *ain't nobody*, habitual *be*); (2) Singlish (recognizable as English from outside but with its own grammar); (3) Bilingual code-switching (where two grammars coexist in a speaker's head). End with the Putnam-O'Hern study to show how listeners decode community membership from grammatical-phonetic micro-cues. The chapter's payoff: there is no single grammar of English, but there are systematic grammars of Englishes, and speakers move between them with subtle competence. This is exactly what HPC predicts and what monolithic generativism cannot easily accommodate.

### Rhetoric that works
"For something to be judged ungrammatical, it's needs to be noticed." (Typo aside.) This is a sharp observation packaged in a sentence. The whole chapter could organize around it.

### Rhetoric that doesn't
"Even as far back as 1944, Furfey observed that men and woman had characteristically different ways of speaking. He cited expression like *Oh, dear!* and *How perfectly sweet!* as being feminine coded." The throat-clear "even as far back as" and the unfinished plural "expression" are the kind of phrasing the chapter doesn't need. This whole section is a few sentences from being a real argument; tighten or cut.
