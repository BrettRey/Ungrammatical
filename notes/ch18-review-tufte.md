## Chapter 18: Swearing

### Strengths
The framing question on line 10 is good and concrete: "Is swearing a norm violation because it is obscene, like a couple lustily copulating in a McDonalds booth or a pile of shit on the floor of an elevator, or because it is ungrammatical?" This is exactly the right question for the chapter to start from, and the visceral comparison cases set the stakes.

The single insight at lines 12-16 - that swearing operates as a "secret language" with its own grammar of contextually-licensed expletives, that fluent swearers know when a curse earns laughter and when it lands as an attack - is a real claim that could anchor a chapter.

### Major concerns
This chapter is a stub. Two epigraph-style quotations (lines 5-9) lacking attribution, then four substantive paragraphs. Total: 16 lines.

There is no figure, no table, no example list, no quantitative grounding. There are no specific swearing examples (curse words appearing in their textual contexts, slurs in different com-sits, expletive-infixation as a real grammatical phenomenon, taboo masking, semantic bleaching of *fucking* as an intensifier).

There is no chapter-heading \chapter{Swearing} command in the file. Either the include in main.tex is generating a heading from the filename or the file is missing structure.

The unattributed epigraph quotations on lines 5-9 are recognisable: they are from Margaret Atwood's *The Handmaid's Tale*. Without attribution, they look like AI-pasted text.

The chapter promises to argue that swearing is a sub-grammar with its own well-formedness rules, and says so once at line 12. The argument is asserted, never developed, never illustrated.

There is no engagement with the substantial linguistics literature on swearing: Bergen 2016 *What the F*, Allan & Burridge on euphemism/dysphemism, Pinker on the neurology of taboo language, Hoeksema on *the hell* class as NPIs, infixation patterns in *fan-fucking-tastic*, expletive *do* and emphatic *do*.

### Priority fixes
1. Add the \chapter{Swearing} heading.
2. Attribute or remove the Atwood epigraphs.
3. Write the chapter. It needs:
   - Concrete swearing examples in their com-sits.
   - The well-known restriction that English expletive infixation only attaches at certain prosodic boundaries (*fan-fucking-tastic* ok, *fant-fucking-astic* not).
   - Cross-linguistic data: not all languages have expletive infixation; not all swear-vocabularies pattern the same.
   - The literature engagement noted above.
4. Connect to chapter 16 (codeswitching / com-sits): the chapter's "secret language" thesis is the same claim as ch16's com-sit claim, just for swearing-licensing contexts. Cross-reference.

### One concrete suggestion
Build a small two-part figure for the chapter as it gets written: "When *fucking* lands."

Top panel: a horizontal axis showing degree-of-formality (informal pub <-> formal lecture). Several speech-event icons placed on the axis, each labelled with a context (locker room, office meeting, eulogy, tweet, courtroom, kindergarten, drag bar, hockey commentary, parents' visit). Each context shaded by acceptability of *fucking* as an intensifier in a sentence like *that's fucking amazing*: white = fine, black = transgressive, grey = ambiguous.

Bottom panel: a single tree showing the prosodic-foot structure of *fan-tas-tic*, with arrows indicating where *fucking* can be infixed (between *fan* and *tas-tic*) and where it cannot (between *tas* and *tic*). Annotated: "swearing has its own grammar, and the grammar is different in each com-sit."

The figure compresses the chapter's main thesis - that swearing has com-sit-specific licensing AND its own internal grammatical constraints - into a single display.

For now: the chapter is unwritten. The above is what should be drafted, and the figure is what would let it be drafted in roughly 4000 words instead of 12000.
