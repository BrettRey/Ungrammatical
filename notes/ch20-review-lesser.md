## Chapter 20: Getting grammaticality wrong

### Note on the chapter's stub status

This is a stub. Twenty-five lines, one section ("Relative \textit{whose}") with three Old/Middle English examples, no other content. The review must therefore be partly prospective: what should belong here?

### Strengths of what's here

The historical material on relative \textit{whose} is well-chosen. Old English used \textit{þe} as a general subordinator; the relative \textit{who} of "doȝter wo miȝte is eir be" emerges only around 1325, in Middle English. The OE construction "þe his tohopa" (literally "that his hope") for what becomes Modern English "whose hope" is a small but striking demonstration of how the modern relative \textit{whose} arose from a paraphrastic construction.

Three glossed examples in twenty-five lines is decent density for archival material.

### What belongs in the chapter that isn't yet

The chapter's title "Getting grammaticality wrong" is a generous one and could go in several directions. Based on the prior chapters, the most plausible content is:

1. Cases where major linguists have wrongly judged something ungrammatical (Hankamer and Postal on independent relative whose, Hurford on probable-to-leave). The book has already laid this groundwork in chapters 5, 10, and 15. Here is the place to consolidate the thesis: linguists' grammaticality intuitions are sometimes wrong, and we should expect this. A short historical survey of well-documented cases would do real work.

2. Cases where speakers' grammaticality intuitions diverge from corpus reality. The book has touched on the ISIS construction (chapter 5) and the singular-they reaction (chapter 5). A consolidation chapter could lay out the typology: introspective judgments versus naturalistic-data judgments, what reflective equilibrium looks like in practice, when corpus data should override intuition and when it shouldn't.

3. Methodological lessons. The book's central methodological move (text the friends, ask the LLMs, check the OED, then revise the Wikipedia entry) is a method as much as it is an anecdote. A short chapter explaining how a careful linguist tests her own intuitions before publishing them would be useful.

4. The specifically pedagogical wrong: prescriptivism's confidence about ungrammaticality (split infinitives, ending sentences with prepositions, "less" versus "fewer," the "who/whom" rule). Reynolds has the Lowth biography and the Goold Brown survey already in chapter 1; he could do real work here showing how the prescriptivists got grammaticality wrong on point after point.

### Priority fixes

1. Write the chapter. As a stub it can't be reviewed.
2. Choose one of the four directions above. The book is already long; this is not the place to do all four.
3. Cross-reference the relevant earlier chapters explicitly. The book's argument benefits from the recursion (whose appears in chapters 5, 15, and now here), but the recursions need to be doing work, not just appearing.
4. Decide whether the existing relative-\textit{whose} historical content belongs here at all, or in chapter 5, or in chapter 15. As it stands, the chapter's content is a fourth pass through the same material.

### One concrete suggestion

There is no chapter to evaluate at the sentence level. The single sentence worth quoting from the existing material is the gloss line: "He had no one but a daughter who could be his heir." This is a reasonable Modern English rendering of the 1325 quotation. The Modern English itself is plain and clear, and that plainness is the right register for the chapter to adopt as it walks the reader through historical examples.

Suggestion: open the chapter with a list of cases where linguists got grammaticality wrong. Hankamer and Postal on whose. Hurford on probable. The 1970s syntacticians on the Henk-stroking-Trouble passive. Then ask: what should we learn from these mistakes? The answer is the chapter's argument.

The chapter title is "Getting grammaticality wrong." A trade reader will pick it up wanting humility from a linguist. Deliver it.
