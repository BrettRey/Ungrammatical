## Chapter 14: Grammar and glamour

### Strengths
This is one of the most charming chapters in the book. The Doctorow *even it's never approved* anecdote is a small masterpiece: Brett spots an oddity in a journalist's prose, emails the author, and gets a one-word reply ("Yup") with a justification ("colloquial, but expressive, unambiguous, and colorful"). That two-line exchange is worth a chapter. It establishes that some grammatical innovations are intentional and that Doctorow knows the rule he's breaking. The closing section on *enshittification* extends the same logic.

The fashion analogy (sweatpants worn with sneakers vs. with a trench coat and a nice handbag) is the book's strongest extended metaphor for grammaticality as choice rather than rule. The Friedman quote is perfectly placed and lands. The connection between fashion and grammar (an item can be unfashionable, but worn with purpose can be glamorous) is the chapter's core claim. It's the right metaphor at the right time.

The morphological breakdown of *enshittification* is genuinely satisfying. The reader gets to see how the word is built (*shit + ify + ic + ate + tion + en*) and to feel why each affix adds something. The Embiggen/Simpsons reference rounds it off with cultural context. This whole sequence works.

### Major concerns
The chapter is short (one section and three subsections). It could afford to be longer because the argument is good and the examples are vivid.

The chapter's structural choice (subsections without a section header) makes it look like a fragment of a larger chapter. Lines 4 begins with `\subsection{Even it's never approved}` but there's no preceding `\section{}` or transition. This is a LaTeX layout problem, but it also reveals that the chapter has no introduction or framing.

The argument that "a grammatical innovation will only work if it achieves some kind of semantic or pragmatic goal" is the chapter's central claim, stated at line 14. But the chapter doesn't develop the converse: what does an innovation that *fails* look like? The chapter could use one example of a failed innovation to throw the successful ones into relief.

The footnote on *dozen* and partitive *of* (line 28) is a sharp observation, but it's the kind of detail that belongs in a linguist's chapter on quantifiers, not in a trade-book chapter on glamour. Either move it or trim it.

The "I have a pair glasses" example (line 28) is invented to illustrate the irony of the *pair*-construction; but unlike the Doctorow example, it has no real-world attestation. A reader might wonder if this is a real thing or a hypothetical. Either find an attested example or flag the hypothetical clearly.

The Doctorow quote "Three suffixes is grand, but I can do more" attributed at line 52 with "(with apologies to Dr. Seuss)" appears to be **invented**. There's no indication Doctorow ever said this. If it's a paraphrase, mark it. If it's invented, cut it. Putting fake quotes in a non-fiction trade book is a credibility-destroyer, especially when the chapter is otherwise built around real quotes from the same person. **Source-grounding violation, fix before any reader notices.**

### Priority fixes
- Add a chapter introduction (one paragraph) that frames the analogy: grammaticality as fashion, not as rule. The argument is strong but currently buried.
- Promote subsections to sections, or add a section header. As-is, the chapter has no first-level structure.
- **Verify or cut the Dr. Seuss-styled Doctorow quote.** If it's invented, this damages the entire chapter's credibility.
- Either find an attested *a pair glasses* or flag the hypothetical.
- Trim the *dozen* footnote or move it.
- Consider adding one example of a failed innovation. Some have a clear shape: *snuck* succeeded; many invented gender-neutral pronouns (*ze*, *xe*) have not. The contrast would sharpen the argument.

### One concrete suggestion
Promote *enshittification* to the chapter's organizing example and use *even it's never approved* as the second case. Open with Doctorow blogging in 2023; describe the word's morphological extravagance in one paragraph. Then make the argument: this is what grammatical innovation looks like when it works. It has to be intentional, it has to do work the existing form doesn't do, it has to anchor in familiar materials, and it has to be deployed by someone with the standing to deploy it. Then *even it's never approved* as a parallel case in syntax: same author, same kind of confident innovation, with a clear semantic-pragmatic goal. Close with the fashion analogy: sweatpants can be glamorous when worn with the right confidence and the right context, and so can grammatical violations. End with a contrasting case that fails (an L2 learner's error; a misfired innovation that didn't catch on). The chapter is currently 2,400 words; aim for 4,000 with this structure.

### Rhetoric that works
"It's not just the use of familiar affixes but the particular affixes used that have resonance with other successful neologisms. *Embiggen* appeared on 'The Simpsons' in 1996." This is the kind of cultural and linguistic detail a trade-book reader can hold onto. The connection between *enshittification* and *embiggen* via the prefix *en-/em-* is exactly the right level of analysis: visible enough to be felt, technical enough to feel earned.

### Rhetoric that doesn't
"Doctorow uses the familiar to produce the sublime." (Line 60.) "Sublime" is too big a word. The chapter has been carefully showing how an everyday writer used everyday morphology to make a word that worked. "Sublime" turns it into philosophical statement. Trade prose: replace with something concrete: "Doctorow uses the familiar to produce the memorable" or just "the chapter ends with the joy of seeing how this works."
