## 1. Wendy Lesser (Threepenny Review)

**Summary.** The chapter uses the asymmetry between *a long, long road* and **the road is long long* to argue that what we call grammatical is the residue of historical accident, not a universal property of mind, with case studies from Old English, Middle English, Jamaican Creole, and Ambae.

**Strengths.**
- The opening earns the chapter. Two stanzas of the same Hollies song, one allowing reduplication, the other not, is exactly the kind of small noticing that pulls a general reader in. *"And what's fascinating is that we all intuitively grasp the difference between these two superficially similar uses of repetition."* That sentence is doing real work; protect it from anyone tempted to "tighten" it.
- The classroom anecdote is gold and must be kept. *"There's never any reaction after the first, but laughter inevitably follows the second in a way it never does with other grammatical infelicities."* This is the moment a reader thinks: oh, the author actually knows something firsthand.
- The ending lands. *"In other words, we're not programmed to prefer one kind of duplication over the other. We're socialised to do so."* Eleven words. Earned.

**Weaknesses.**
- The grey "Breaking it down" box stalls the chapter cold. The reader has just been told something delightful about laughter and a Turkish woman and the pope, and is then handed *"More specifically, \data{long} is an attributive modifier, a modifier that precedes the head noun, assigning it a particular property, in this case, the attribute of length."* This is journal prose. Either fold the necessary terms into the running prose or push the box to a later chapter.
- The Old English section drowns the argument. The reader has to fight through *burggeat*, *micel*, *fæst eald geat*, *blæc*, two DALL·E illustrations, and a forest of italicised Old English. *"This structure may have gradually eroded the restriction on multiple adjective modifiers"* is the load-bearing sentence and it's buried. Cut at least a third.
- The declension table is dead weight. The point — speakers came to rely on word order — can be made in a sentence.

**Key question.** Why does this chapter open the book? The thesis you actually deliver, *grammaticality is historically contingent*, is a destination, not an invitation. Should the Hollies stay but the Old English section move to a later chapter where the reader has already bought in?

**Verdict: Revise.**

## 3. John McPhee (master of structure)

**Structural shape.** Hub-and-spokes: a hook (Hollies → puzzle) feeds a typology spoke (Japanese, Russian), then a long diachronic spoke (Old English → Middle English), then a counter-evidence spoke (Jamaican Creole, NE Ambae), and tries to braid back at the end with the Turkish-students vignette and modern compounding reduplication.

**Strengths.**
- The opening is genuinely earned. Two stanzas of the same Hollies song that diverge in grammaticality (L4–7 vs L13–16) — one source, one word, two outcomes. It does what the first paragraph of a McPhee piece is supposed to do: names the puzzle without announcing it.
- The "Breaking it down" tcolorbox (L28–37) is good infrastructure. It does the technical work the trade reader needs without freezing the prose around it. A reader who already knows attributive vs. predicative skips it; a reader who doesn't gets a clean ledge to stand on.
- The Jamaican Creole / NE Ambae section (L225–239) earns the title's honesty. A lesser draft would have stopped at Japanese and Russian, declared a universal, and run.

**Weaknesses.**
- The Old English / Middle English spine is doing too much and too little at once. The diachronic argument narrated (L105–134) is **compound-noun reanalysis** (*burggeat* → *micel burg geat*), which gets you adjective-adjective stacking in attributive position. That's not the same thing as reduplicative intensification. The chapter then jumps (L210) — "All of this laid the groundwork" — to a 1579 *small little* citation as if the case has been made. It hasn't.
- The Turkish-students close (L245–253) is furniture pretending to be a frame. It's set up early (L26) as the puzzle the chapter will solve, but the eventual answer (L247–249) is a one-paragraph speculation. That's a hypothesis, not a payoff.
- The 1970s clarificatory-reduplication coda (L253) is a different chapter. *"right*-right" is fascinating but it arrives in two sentences after the chapter has already closed twice (the \bigskip on L243 and again on L251). Three endings is no ending.

**Key question.** The chapter's title-level argument is *grammaticality is historically contingent*. But the diachronic spine actually argues for **adjective stacking**, not **reduplicative intensification** — and the leap from one to the other (L210) is taken on faith. Which mechanism is doing the work, and can the chapter pick one?

**Verdict: Revise.**

## 4. Steven Pinker (Sense of Style)

**Summary.** The chapter argues that the asymmetry between attributive *a long long road* and predicative **the road is long long* isn't a deep universal of mind but a local English settlement reached through a chain of historical accidents in Old and Middle English, with cross-linguistic counter-examples to keep the universalist honest.

**Strengths.**
- The opener earns its keep. The Hollies stanza, then the same word in the predicative slot, is a small experiment the reader can run on themselves.
- The core claim is genuinely arresting. That **old old* is "the historical default" and that pre-nominal stacking opened up only after a cascade of unrelated changes is the kind of finding a general reader will remember at dinner.
- Honest commitment to the reader. The Jamaican Creole and NE Ambae examples actually break a tempting universalist conclusion.

**Weaknesses.**
- The terminology dump in the "Breaking it down" box is where you lose the reader who came for The Hollies. Inside one box, a non-linguist meets *attributive modifier*, *predicative complement*, *head noun*, *clause*, *reduplicative intensification*. The line *"It's not complement in the clause, not a modifier"* is also literally garbled (a missing "a", and the contrast doesn't parse).
- "Calquing" and "declension" are dropped in without ceremony. The Old English declension table appears before the reader has been told what work it does in the argument; a non-linguist sees four cases × two numbers and politely turns the page.
- Cross-linguistic examples without setup feel like footnotes promoted to body text. Japanese and Russian arrive with glossing conventions you have not yet taught a trade reader to read. The Jamaican Creole and NE Ambae examples have the same problem in reverse: they're the most important data in the chapter (they falsify a universal) but they're tucked at the end.

**Key question.** You stake the chapter on a historical-accident story for English, then show the asymmetry recurring in Japanese and Russian. If it's just an accident in English, why does an unrelated language land in the same place?

**Verdict: Revise.**

## 7. Detailed editorial pass

The chapter has a strong governing idea: *the road is long long* feels impossible not because reduplication is universally impossible in predicative position, but because English happened to develop one constructional pathway for adjective repetition and not another. That is a very good chapter-sized problem. It is concrete, memorable, and directly connected to the book's larger concern with what a star actually means. The best version of the chapter is not "here is a curiosity about reduplication," but "here is a case where an apparently obvious ungrammaticality turns out to be historically contingent, construction-specific, and learnable from distributional evidence."

The main editorial issue is that the chapter's strongest argument is currently partly hidden. The opening asks a sharp question. The ending gives an interesting answer. But the middle sometimes reads as a history of multiple adjectives rather than a history of the conditions that made *long long* possible in one syntactic position and not another. The revision should make that causal path more explicit.

### At the structural level

The chapter's spine should be something like this:

1. English allows intensificatory adjective repetition in attributive position: *a long long road*.
2. English does not allow the same intensificatory repetition in predicative complement position: *the road is long long*.
3. A tempting explanation is that this reflects a universal constraint on the human mind, or a semantic incompatibility in predicative position.
4. The history of English suggests something more contingent: adjective stacking became available on the left side of the noun phrase through a series of constructional developments, while the predicative position did not undergo the same development.
5. Other languages show that predicative/post-head intensificatory reduplication is possible.
6. So the star on *the road is long long* marks a fact about English, not a fact about possible grammar.

That structure is already present, but it needs to be made more visible. The paragraph at L99–100 is the obvious place to state it. At the moment, it presents three hypotheses — cognitive universal, semantic incompatibility, historical accident — but then the chapter moves straight into Old English without explicitly telling the reader which hypothesis is being pursued. I would make that paragraph the chapter's prospectus.

A possible revision of the transition would be:

> That gives us three possible explanations. The restriction might be a universal fact about how human minds interpret repeated adjectives. It might follow from the meaning of intensificatory repetition itself. Or it might be an accident of the history of English: adjective repetition became possible in one grammatical position because that position had already developed room for adjacent modifiers, while the predicative position had not. The rest of this chapter argues for that third answer.

That would make the Old English and Middle English sections feel necessary rather than digressive.

The second structural issue is the Pullum/Turkish material at L20–24. It is interesting, but it enters before the chapter has fully established its central contrast. The example is also not the same construction: *very very good* involves reduplication of a degree modifier, not an adjective in attributive or predicative complement function. That does not make it irrelevant, but it means it should be framed as a related phenomenon rather than as evidence for the *long long* contrast.

I would either move the Pullum paragraph later, into the "In other languages" section, or compress it early and explicitly say it is adjacent to, not identical with, the problem. The classroom observation at L26 is actually more directly useful for the opening because it tests the contrast between *a long long road* and *the road is long long*. I would consider moving that paragraph before Pullum:

> But I wonder whether something more local is going on. I teach English to international students studying in Canada, some of them beginners, and I have given them examples like *a long long road* and *the road is long long*. The first usually passes without comment. The second produces laughter. Even students with limited English experience often respond as if they have encountered a boundary.

Then Pullum can become part of the question: are they transferring a reduplicative pattern from another language, learning a distributional fact about English, or both?

The third structural issue is the historical middle. The Old English and Middle English sections need a clearer distinction between evidence and hypothesis. L105–134 propose a plausible pathway: compounds may have helped make adjacent pre-head modifiers more tolerable, and that may have contributed to the later availability of multiple attributive adjectives. But the passage sometimes sounds as though the pathway has been demonstrated. The verbs are doing a lot of work: "may have provided," "may have interpreted," "would have put," "eventually making it possible." Those are fine, but the section needs a sentence that tells the reader what status the story has.

Something like this would help before or after L127:

> This is not meant as a single documented moment of reanalysis. It is a way of seeing why the left edge of the noun phrase offered English speakers more opportunities for adjacent modifier sequences than the predicative position did.

That would protect the argument from seeming over-precise. You do not need to prove that some particular speaker reanalyzed *burggeat* in exactly this way. You need to show that the pre-head modifier zone had constructional affordances that the predicative complement zone lacked.

The fourth structural issue is the Middle English section. L152–194 spend a lot of space on case erosion and declension. That material is accurate-looking and pedagogically clear, but it does not yet pay its rent. The reader needs to know why the death of declension matters for adjective reduplication. At present, the section says: case endings eroded, word order mattered more, adjectives became less distinct from other categories, redundant adjectives appeared. The movement from word order to adjective sequences needs another bridge.

The declension tables may also be too heavy for the chapter's purpose. They teach something real, but they risk shifting the chapter from "why *long long* works here but not there" to "here is a general thumbnail history of English morphology." You could probably replace both tables with one compact example and keep the prose moving. The load-bearing point is not the full paradigm of *se hring*; it is that Middle English increasingly relied on position, and the pre-nominal zone became a stable place for modifier material.

The fifth structural issue is the ending. L222–253 are promising but abrupt. The Jamaican Creole and North-East Ambae examples are exactly what the chapter needs: they show that the English restriction is not a universal ban. But after L253, the chapter stops just as a conclusion is needed. The final paragraph on *right-right* introduces a new construction, then ends with "This could be the key that opens up other functions to multiple adjectives." That is intriguing, but too speculative as the chapter's final sentence.

I would add a short closing paragraph that returns to the original starred sentence. For example:

> So the star in *the road is long long* does not mark an impossible form of thought, or even an impossible form of grammar. It marks the limits of one English construction. English learned to tolerate doubled adjectives on the left side of the noun phrase: *a long long road*, *an old old tale*, *small little red flowers*. It did not generalize the same pattern to predicative complements. Other languages did. And newer English reduplications such as *right-right* show that even this boundary is not fixed once and for all.

That would make the chapter close on the book's central concern: the star is not self-interpreting.

### At the paragraph level

The opening works well. The Hollies example is efficient because the lyric gives you both forms: *It's a long, long road* and *The road is long*. You are not importing a made-up contrast; you are altering a real one. L1–18 are therefore a good opening sequence.

The first paragraph could be a little less explanatory. L2 says repeating *long* "amplif[ies] its meaning" and "essentially doubl[es] down on it." Those are doing the same work. I would make the prose cleaner and let the example carry more of the force:

> Consider an adjective like *long*. Repeat it, and its meaning intensifies.

L9 is also slightly overemphatic. "Would not come across at all" is too absolute: *It's a long road* still conveys length; it just lacks the emotional and rhythmic intensification of *long, long*. I would use "would not come across in the same way."

The contrast at L18 is excellent, but "Nobody says *the road is long long*" may be too absolute. People can utter almost anything in play, in child-directed speech, in echoic contexts, or in metalinguistic discussion. The point is not literal utterability; it is the absence of this form as ordinary intensificatory English. A safer version:

> As an ordinary English intensifier, *the road is long long* is ridiculous.

That also aligns better with the later discussion of *right-right*, where predicative reduplication does become possible in a different construction.

The Pullum paragraph at L20–24 should be split or moved. It currently contains too many jobs: it expands the claim to learners, introduces a famous anecdote, evaluates Pullum's interpretation, gives a frequency claim, introduces calquing, and gives Turkish. It also contains one of the chapter's load-bearing but unverified empirical claims: the frequency of *very very*. Since the file already marks that claim for verification, I would treat it as structurally risky until sourced. If the number stays, it needs a corpus name and a date or access note.

The paragraph at L26 is strong, and I would give it more prominence. It is your own classroom evidence, and it gets directly to the acceptability contrast. I would soften "These students just know that it's wrong," though. The chapter's own conclusion is that they may have extrapolated from distributional evidence in English. So perhaps:

> These students respond as if they have already learned the boundary.

That keeps the force of the observation without making the knowledge sound innate.

The "Breaking it down" box at L28–37 is useful, but it contains one sentence that needs immediate correction. L33 says, "It's not complement in the clause, not a modifier." That should be something like:

> It is not a modifier at all, but a complement in the clause.

The box might also benefit from a title more tightly connected to the chapter's problem. "Breaking it down" is serviceable, but "Two positions for adjectives" or "Modifier versus complement" would make the point sharper.

The rule at L39–42 overgenerates. As your commented-out paragraph at L97 already recognizes, not every adjective can be doubled in this way. *A Cambrian Cambrian fossil* is not just as good as *a long long road*. You need a live paragraph about gradability or scalarity before stating the rule, or you need to build that qualification into the rule.

A revised version of the rule might be:

> In the intensificatory construction under discussion, a gradable adjective in attributive modifier function may be repeated to strengthen its meaning. The same pattern is not generally available when the adjective functions as predicative complement.

That avoids three problems: it does not claim all adjectives participate; it marks the construction as intensificatory; and it leaves room for later contrastive-focus cases such as *right-right*.

The Japanese and Russian examples at L44–70 are a good move, but the framing should be cautious. Two languages do not establish a universal; they establish a temptation to universalize. Something like "The temptation to see this as a general fact about grammar gets stronger when we find similar patterns elsewhere" would be more accurate than "the same statement often seems to hold," unless you plan to give a larger typological base.

The paragraph at L100 is structurally crucial but too long. It should be split into three hypotheses and their predictions. The current version has the right content, but the reader has to extract the structure from a single dense paragraph. I would separate it into something like:

> Why should this restriction hold?
>
> One answer is universalist: something about the human mind might make intensificatory adjective repetition impossible in predicative function. That answer predicts that no language should allow the construction there.
>
> A second answer is semantic: perhaps *long long* means something that works only when it modifies a noun. That answer predicts that the restriction should follow from the meaning of the repeated adjective itself.
>
> A third answer is historical: English may simply have developed a construction for repeated adjectives in one position and not another. That answer predicts that other languages may do things differently.

Then the Old English section has a clear job.

The Old English opening at L105 is lively, especially "You just had to sneak one inside the noun." That sentence is good popular exposition. But "Intensificatory reduplication didn't exist in Old English" is a very strong absence claim. Unless you have robust support, I would soften it:

> The relevant construction does not seem to have been available in Old English.

Similarly, "an Old-English speaker couldn't even put two adjectives back to back for the most part" needs either a citation or a hedge. This section is too load-bearing to rest on unsourced categorical claims.

The blackbird paragraph at L107 is good, but it slightly overstates what makes compounds "single words." It is not simply that "their meaning changes if you take them apart"; many compounds are semantically transparent, and many phrases have meanings that shift by context. The useful point is syntactic opacity: *really* can modify *black* in *a really black bird*, but not inside *blackbird*. That is a strong explanation, and you can foreground it.

The two figures at L109–123 may be too much for a short chapter. The blackbird/black bird image might help non-specialists. The *burggeat* image probably adds less, and the caption "Folks near a burggeat" risks making the historical argument feel whimsical at precisely the point where you need evidential confidence. I would keep at most one image in this stretch.

L127 is conceptually useful but under-supported. "The odd burgher" is funny, but it makes a speculative historical reanalysis sound like a joke. In this paragraph, I would trade charm for clarity:

> Some speakers may have begun to treat compounds of this kind less as opaque words and more as sequences of modifier plus noun. If so, a form like *micel burg geat* could be heard as containing two adjacent pre-head modifiers.

That keeps the hypothesis without overcommitting.

The summary paragraph at L134 is doing necessary work, but it repeats what the preceding paragraphs have already explained. I would shorten it and make the contrast with predicative complements the payoff:

> The point is not that Old English already had phrases like *a great black gate*. It is that the left side of the noun phrase offered places where modifier-like material could accumulate. Predicative complements had no comparable source of adjacent adjectives. That asymmetry matters: it gave one position a path toward *old old tales*, while leaving *the tale is old old* as the historical default.

That is probably the key paragraph of the historical section.

The Middle English section needs heavier compression. L154–194 are clear, but they risk answering a question the reader has not asked. The case paradigm is less directly relevant than the rise of stable pre-head modifier sequences. I would either cut the tables or explicitly subordinate them:

> One of the larger Middle English changes was the erosion of case endings. As those endings weakened, word order took on more grammatical work. For present purposes, the relevant point is not case loss by itself, but the growing stability of positions within the noun phrase.

Then move quickly to the adjective-sequence evidence.

L210 is a crucial transition but needs rephrasing. "Redundant adjectives" may sound like an error label rather than a constructional development. Since *small little red flowers* is not reduplication but near-synonymous stacking, call it that:

> All of this laid the groundwork for a new kind of intensification: near-synonymous adjective stacking, as in...

Then explain that this is not *long long* yet, but it prepares the same syntactic space.

L216–218 are among the chapter's most important historical claims. They need at least one example with source support, not just a report that such examples "begin to show up reliably." If *long* is one of the earliest and most common participants, that is interesting enough to deserve a concrete dated example. Otherwise, the chapter asks the reader to accept the historical payoff without seeing the evidence.

L220 is a strong summary. "A long long chain of historical accidents" is perhaps a little cute, but it fits the chapter's title and topic. I would only change the example. *A tale which is old old* introduces a relative clause and distracts from the original contrast. Use the simpler form:

> In short, the ungrammaticality of *the tale is old old* is more or less the historical default.

The "In other languages" section is well placed. It tests the universalist hypothesis after the English historical account. But L241 overstates the conclusion: "we're not programmed... We're socialised." I would avoid the opposition between programming and socialization. The chapter's point is not anti-biology; it is constructional and language-specific. A better version:

> In other words, there is nothing impossible about predicative or post-head intensificatory reduplication. Speakers learn which positions their language has made available for it.

That is more precise and less rhetorically binary.

The final student paragraph at L245–249 is good, but "you simply don't find more than one adjective after *is*" is not true as stated. We do find coordinated adjective sequences after *is*: *She is kind, funny, and generous*; *He is tall, dark, and handsome*. The problem is with bare, uncoordinated adjective sequences in predicative complement function. So L249 should say something like:

> They have extrapolated from the evidence they have seen in English that bare, uncoordinated adjective sequences do not normally appear as predicative complements.

That also lets *She's nice good* do the right work.

The final paragraph at L253 introduces contrastive focus reduplication, but it should not be allowed to blur the earlier distinction. *Right-right* is not simply a late exception to the rule; it is a different construction with a different meaning. The paragraph should explicitly say that:

> This is not the same construction as intensificatory *long long*. It is contrastive: *right-right* means something like 'right in the relevant, strict, or intended sense'. But its existence matters because it shows that English can open predicative position to reduplication when a different construction supplies a different meaning.

Then add the concluding paragraph suggested above.

### At the sentence level

A few local sentences are doing more work than they need to.

- **L1**: the chapter title is *A long long road*, while the file name is "A long long way." That may not matter, but it is worth settling. If the title alludes directly to the lyric, *A long, long road* with the comma is closer to the quoted line. If the title names the construction, *A long long road* without the comma is defensible, but then you may want a note early on that ordinary punctuation varies while the linguistic example is written without the comma for consistency.
- **L2**: "Simply by repeating it, we manage to amplify its meaning, essentially doubling down on it" is slightly padded. Try: "Repeat it, and its meaning intensifies."
- **L9**: "in a way that would not come across at all" overstates. Try: "in a way that the more prosaic *it's a long road* would not."
- **L18**: "Far from adding emotional depth, it would just be ridiculous" is good, but "Nobody says" is too absolute. Try: "As ordinary English, *the road is long long* is ridiculous."
- **L21**: "people who have only the most limited grasp of English" and "so limited" may sound harsher than intended. "limited command of English" would be cleaner. The parenthetical "though far better than my grasp of Turkish" is charming but slightly distracts from the argument.

### Overall diagnosis

This chapter's best material is the relation between a tiny judgment and a large historical explanation. The opening star on *the road is long long* should become less obvious as the chapter proceeds. At first it looks like a universal fact: of course English works that way. Then Japanese and Russian make the universal temptation stronger. Then the history of English reframes the pattern as a constructional accident. Then Jamaican Creole and North-East Ambae show that the starred English sentence is not impossible in human language. Finally, *right-right* shows that even English can let reduplication into predicative position when the construction is different.

That is a strong arc. The revision should make it explicit, reduce the amount of general historical machinery that does not directly serve it, and make the speculative parts sound like carefully bounded hypotheses rather than recovered facts. The most urgent changes are: qualify the rule at L41, split and strengthen the hypothesis paragraph at L100, compress the Middle English declension material, clarify the distinction between intensificatory reduplication and contrastive *right-right*, and add a closing paragraph that returns to what the star in *the road is long long* is really claiming.

### New points from the detailed editorial pass
- **The rule at L41 overgenerates.** *A Cambrian Cambrian fossil* is not parallel to *a long long road*; the rule must specify *gradable* adjectives in *intensificatory* function. Brett's own commented-out paragraph at L97 already recognizes this. None of the first six reviewers caught it.
- **Pullum's *very very good* is not the same construction.** It's a degree-modifier reduplication, not adjective reduplication in attributive vs. predicative position. The chapter currently uses it as if it were direct evidence for the *long long* contrast. Either reframe as adjacent phenomenon, or move into the "In other languages" section.
- ***Right-right* is contrastive focus, not intensificatory reduplication.** The closing paragraph treats it as if it's the same construction reopening. It isn't. The point about *right-right* matters — it shows the predicative position can host reduplication when a different construction supplies a different meaning — but it must be framed as a different construction, not a late exception.
- **L241's "we're socialised, not programmed" sets up a false dichotomy** that the chapter doesn't actually need. Replace with something like "there is nothing impossible about predicative or post-head intensificatory reduplication. Speakers learn which positions their language has made available for it."
- **L249's "you simply don't find more than one adjective after *is*" is false** as stated — coordinated sequences (*kind, funny, and generous*) are fine after *is*. The constraint is on bare, uncoordinated sequences.
- **L191's gloss has two errors:** stray comma after *woman*, and *give* should be *gave*.
- **L33 garbled sentence** ("It's not complement in the clause, not a modifier") needs correction — Pinker also caught this. Should be: "It is not a modifier but a complement in the clause."
- **Multiple absolute claims need hedging:** "Nobody says…" (L18), "Intensificatory reduplication didn't exist in Old English" (L105), "an Old-English speaker couldn't even put two adjectives back to back" (L107).

### Contradictions
1. **The tcolorbox.** McPhee defends it as good structural infrastructure — a ledge a non-linguist can stand on. Lesser, Pinker, Radiolab, and the detailed pass say it kills the lean-in (and the L33 sentence is broken on top of that). Even McPhee allows that the rule following it could be sharper. The detailed pass's specific reframe ("Modifier versus complement" as a tighter title) is a middle path.
2. **The mechanism's identity.** McPhee notices the diachronic spine argues for **adjective stacking** but the puzzle is **reduplicative intensification**. Pinker independently notices Japanese and Russian show the same asymmetry without English's history. The detailed pass agrees the leap at L210 is unsupported but proposes a fix: relabel L210's *small little* as **near-synonymous adjective stacking** (a precursor that prepares the syntactic space) rather than as the same construction. That gives the chapter a way to keep the historical machinery without claiming it directly explains *long long*.
3. **Where do Jamaican Creole and NE Ambae go?** McPhee and Mendelson treat them as the resolving counter-evidence near the end. Radiolab and the detailed pass argue for putting them earlier so the universalist hypothesis is killed on screen before the historical account begins.
4. **The status of the historical narrative.** Multiple reviewers want the Old English/Middle English material cut. The detailed pass agrees but adds an alternative: keep the structure, but explicitly mark it as a *way of seeing* the asymmetry rather than a documented chain of events. A single sentence near L127 acknowledging that "this is not meant as a single documented moment of reanalysis" would protect the section from over-precision.

### Prioritized revision list
1. **Make the chapter's spine explicit at L99–102.** Split into three hypotheses (universal / semantic / historical), state each prediction, and announce that the rest of the chapter pursues the third. Six of seven reviewers point at this paragraph as the chapter's hinge.
2. **Cut the Old English section by at least half, and reframe what remains.** Drop the declension table; cut burggeat to a paragraph (or per Zimmer, ground in one real attestation). Replace L127's "odd burgher" with neutral phrasing. Mark the historical narrative as a *way of seeing* rather than a recovered chain of events. At L210, relabel as "near-synonymous adjective stacking" rather than reduplication.
3. **Resolve the construction mismatch.** Make explicit what counts as the *same* construction across the chapter. Pullum's *very very good* and the *right-right* coda are both *related* but *different* — say so. Qualify the rule at L41 to gradable adjectives in the intensificatory construction.
4. **Restructure the reveal sequence.** Move Jamaican Creole / NE Ambae before the Old English section (Radiolab + detailed pass) so the universalist hypothesis is dispatched on screen before history takes the stage.
5. **Pick one ending and add a closing paragraph that returns to the star.** The 1970s coda either becomes its own short structural argument (different construction; same predicative position; reduplication isn't permanently closed there) or moves to a later chapter. Add a closing paragraph that directly answers what the star in **the road is long long* is claiming. The detailed pass offers a draft.
6. **Sentence-level corrections.** L33 garbled sentence; L191 OE gloss errors (*woman, gave*); L241 false dichotomy; L249 false claim about adjectives after *is*; L41 overgeneration; missing question mark at L44; multiple absolute claims that need hedging (L18, L105, L107).
7. **Source-ground the TODO-flagged claims.** L23 "very very" 2.5/million (corpus + access date), L212 Bullein 1579 (bib entry), L224 Pullum p.c. on Jamaican Creole (date + form of citation).
