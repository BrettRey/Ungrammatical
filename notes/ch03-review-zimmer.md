## Chapter 03: Being grammatical isn't always enough

### Strengths

The opening gambit - *I have 30 years* is grammatical English but not how you state your age - is exactly the right kind of problem. It's also the chapter's clearest illustration of the gap between "well-formed" and "what speakers will accept." The contrast with French *J'ai 30 ans* and the converse French *faites le 2* for "press 2" is concrete and even funny.

The wife-text exchange - *you still at the gym? / I still at the gym* - is a real piece of household linguistics, with a real joke in it, and the analysis (the second relies on shared awareness that the casual *be*-drop is itself constrained to certain registers) is sharp. The set of *Is X / X?* parallels that follow is genuinely useful as a probe of which copula-drop questions still ring true.

The Russian-dolls explanation of unbounded dependency is patient and physical: keys dropped, picked up, the *it* that disappears, the chain extending to "I found the key that I thought she said you heard I had dropped." A reader can feel the depth.

David Gil on Riau Indonesian and Dan Everett on Pirahã are exactly the kind of cross-linguistic anchors a trade reader needs. The *touch this and you die* parallel for English - same kind of clause-stacking - is a small gem.

The Henk-and-Trouble passive (Pullum's example from Osman's novel) is brilliantly chosen and the discussion of information-packaging beats any abstract account of the by-phrase constraint.

### Major concerns

The chapter is a quilt. It declares "ideas" up top, opens with copula-drop, drifts into Russian dolls and Riau Indonesian, leaps to "Conventions" with Shakespeare and hip-hop and the Simpsons (without quite using any of them), trails off into a stub on Anne Carson, jumps to information structure and the 1970s with bell-bottoms and disco, then to verbs becoming intransitive in subcommunities, then to the passive-as-moral-failing and the Jeff Brown / Nietzsche-in-translation anecdote, then dangling modifiers, then a single quoted Israel sentence on pragmatics and stops. The pieces are good; the chapter is not yet a chapter.

The Anne Carson intro has no Anne Carson. We are told she's "contemporary Canadian poet, essayist, and classicist" and then... nothing. Either give us a Carson sentence and what it does, or cut.

The "[etc]" and "[is that really why?]" markers and the trailing block-quote-without-context show this is mid-draft.

The "1970s linguists with hard-and-fast syntactic rules" framing is a strawman. The point about information structure trumping the supposed-syntactic constraint is good, but the disco/bell-bottoms voice (probably LLM-generated tonal mimicry) doesn't sit well next to the Pullum example.

### Priority fixes

1. Pick a spine. The unifying theme is "grammatical-but-not-accepted" or "grammatical-but-marked," and each section should be a different way that can happen: idiomatic specialization, register, group membership, information packaging, moral coloring. Right now the spine is missing.
2. Cut Anne Carson or commit to her with at least one quoted line and analysis.
3. The Shakespeare/hip-hop/Simpsons throwaway ("I won't bore you with...") is a meta-tic that does nothing. Delete.
4. The passive-hating Nietzsche supervisor anecdote is great but the section ends with "There's no evidence of anyone having had any problem with the passive until George Orwell wrote 'Politics and the English language.' [is this true]?" That needs an answer.

### One concrete suggestion

For the brewing/drinking subcommunity passage, a short scene at a homebrew shop or club meeting would land what's currently abstract. Someone says "I've stopped brewing" across a counter; a stranger thinks they mean coffee; a beer-club regular knows immediately. The chapter argues, but doesn't show, that group membership reshapes which verbs feel transitive. A 100-word vignette in a hobbyist's garage with carboys, hops, and the smell of the wort would let the reader feel the dialect inside the dialect.
