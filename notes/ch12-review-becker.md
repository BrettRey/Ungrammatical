## Chapter 12: Becoming (un)grammatical

### Strengths
The historical-grammar work in this chapter is the book at its strongest. The *very*/*much* story is a real diachronic argument with real corpus evidence: the Caxton 1481 quote with the Middle English original below it is exactly the kind of primary-source move that earns reader trust. Showing *moche fair*, *moche grete*, and *moche noble* in actual 15th-century use, side by side with their Modern equivalents, lets the reader feel the change. That's better than any abstract claim about "language evolves." Keep this section essentially as is.

The Jespersen's-cycle pivot to French negation is also good architecture. You move from one English construction to a cross-linguistic generalization without overclaiming, and the *I won't walk a step* / *Je ne marche pas* parallel is a beautiful piece of teaching. The link to negative polarity items, with the lovely list (*drink a drop, sleep a wink, lift a finger, give a damn*), brings what could be a dry typological observation into immediate everyday English.

The *much* NPI investigation, with the John Payne collaboration, is the chapter's most disciplined empirical move. You've shown that *much* is an NPI in some constructions but not others, that the corpus evidence for it being an NPI in *much time* contexts is much weaker than the textbook claim suggests, and that the pattern firmed up in the early 19th century. That is a publishable finding embedded in a book chapter. Generative readers will respect this work.

The euphemism-treadmill aside (Pinker via Taylor 1974) is a nice cross-linguistic-cycle parallel.

### Major concerns
There are two chapters in this file. *Becoming (un)grammatical* and *The onward march of do-support* both begin with *\chapter*. The second chapter has either been concatenated by mistake or is a stub for what should be chapter 13 of a different ordering. As written, the two will collide in the build.

The *do*-support chapter (lines 179-222) is heavily LLM-generated. The phrasing is unmistakable: "the curious case of do-support," "It paved the way for," "this brings us to the present day," "as linguist John McWhorter puts it," "in the grand scheme of things," "the never-ending dance of language," "the language's ongoing evolution." That last paragraph in particular is what generated text looks like in the wild: lyrical, expansive, content-thin. A generative reader who reaches the closing paragraph ("the never-ending dance of language") will close the book.

The empirical claim in this generated chapter is also miscalibrated. *Embedded inversion* (e.g., *I wonder what did you say*) is a real and well-studied phenomenon, but it's not "becoming increasingly common" in casual American or British English in the way the chapter suggests. It's strongly associated with Belfast/Hiberno-English and with some Southern Irish English varieties, where it has a long history. The chapter generalizes over varieties in a misleading way.

The McWhorter quote ("Language is not a protocol...") is famous and overused. If you want McWhorter, take him on a specific point, not as inspirational sign-off.

A smaller concern with the *very*/*much* chapter: the gap from *much great* to *very strange* is briefly noted, but the polarity-vs-comparative pattern (*very + plain adjective*, *much + comparative*) deserves a clearer paragraph. As written, the reader has to do interpretive work that the chapter could do for them.

### Priority fixes
- Separate the two chapters in the file, or remove the *do*-support content if it belongs elsewhere.
- Cut the closing paragraphs of the *do*-support chapter wholesale if you keep it. The "never-ending dance of language" prose has to come out.
- The *do*-support chapter, if kept, should be replaced with a tightly-written historical narrative (parallel to the *very*/*much* chapter) using actual EEBO/COHA frequency data. You've shown you can do that work; do it here.
- The "frith" example at line 157 is a fragment. Either develop it (Middle English vocabulary loss vs. grammatical loss) or remove.
- The Saussure quote at line 144 attributes "founder of modern linguistics" to him, which is contestable and unnecessary. Cut the throat-clearer.
- The closing paragraph claim about "people developed a sense that *much* was better in non-affirmative environments, without developing the feeling that it was ungrammatical there" is the chapter's punchline. Make it land.

### One concrete suggestion
Drop the *do*-support content. The *very*/*much* historical investigation, the NPI pivot, the John Payne collaboration, and the COHA findings on *much time* together make a complete and well-shaped chapter. Adding the *do*-support material weakens the chapter rather than strengthens it, because the *do*-support section is generated and overgeneral while the rest is original and disciplined. Better to have one tight chapter on diachronic NPI emergence than a chapter with two parts of unequal quality. If you want a *do*-support chapter elsewhere in the book, write it the way you wrote *very*/*much*: with corpus evidence, with attention to specific texts, with the question "what happened in the 1830s vs. the 1830s?" running through it. A generative reader who finishes the *very*/*much* investigation feeling persuaded will then read whatever you write next. A generative reader who finishes the *do*-support paean feeling lectured won't.
