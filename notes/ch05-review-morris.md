## Chapter 05: How grammar feels (intuitions)

### Strengths

This chapter contains the book's three best research stories. First, the *whose* investigation. Reynolds reads Hankamer and Postal's 1973 squib, asks GPT-4 and Gemini, gets a 6 out of 7 from each, then texts twelve friends and family, then digs into the OED, then finds web examples ("the most generous patron whose also is\dots"), then publishes a paper. The detail that Wikipedia required sources and "Brett's friends and family along with some LLMs doesn't cut it" is the kind of human-comic concession that makes the chapter alive. There is a real dramatic arc: a graduate student's bold claim from 1973 vs. a corpus check fifty years later. Reynolds is a genuine character in this story, not the ghost of his own argument.

Second, the daughter Mia's roommate Sasha and the *they*-pronoun problem. Reynolds confessing he gets blindsided by *I wish they would*. He cannot, for the life of him, hear *they* as Sasha-the-individual. He has tried. He calls his comprehension "ballistic, in the sense that it can't be stopped." That is honesty. That is the chapter's argument inside a parent's body. It also functions as a model of how to write about contested grammar without being a coward and without being a scold.

Third, the ISIS construction overheard on EconTalk. He tracks it to a friend in Tokyo around 1998. He's been watching this construction grow for 25 years. He knows it must be grammatical for Russ Roberts and Kevin Kelly. He still can't hear it without flinching.

The Geoff Pullum miniature is also affectionate and specific: dropout, bookstore clerk, touring pianist on hit albums of the 1960s, only later a linguist. Pullum becomes a person, not a citation.

### Major concerns

This is the longest and most chaotic chapter. There are at least three duplicate sections: two "What does (un)grammaticality feel like" sections (lines 400 and 440), two identical "Reinterpreting grammatical intuitions through a constructionist lens" subsections (lines 701 and 717), and a "Form-meaning mismatch" section that begins twice (line 808 and line 818). Line 818 includes the line "Certainly. I'll build out the two examples at the end" -- that is an LLM voice talking to Reynolds. Line 819 has the literal string "latexCopy". The chapter ends mid-thought at line 899 with a ChatGPT-generated treatise on emotional vectors that does not belong in a trade book.

There is also a stretch of orphaned literary epigraphs at lines 414-431: Cormac McCarthy, Faulkner, Beckett, Orwell, Shakespeare, Pascal, all listed without commentary. They look like a working-notes pile.

The Bayesian formula section is the chapter's weakest argumentative move. Stipulating $P(B|A_2) = 10^{-15}$ then "calculating" the result is theatre, not argument. A trade reader will see straight through it. Cut.

On the protagonists question: this chapter is the most successful in the book at humanizing its subjects. Hankamer and Postal arrive as fully fleshed academics: Postal directing his own thesis, his advisor refusing to read it for two years, the Linguistics Wars of the late '60s. Pullum gets a dropout-to-pianist-to-philosopher arc. Even Mia and Sasha and the friend-from-Tokyo are real. The book should look at this chapter and ask: why is the writing so much better when there are named people in the room?

Lisa Feldman Barrett and Kent Berridge, by contrast, arrive as theory-vending machines. Barrett's name appears, her theory is summarized, the chapter moves on. No image of who she is. Same for Berridge.

### Priority fixes

1. Delete one of the two "What does (un)grammaticality feel like" sections.
2. Delete the duplicate "Reinterpreting grammatical intuitions" subsection.
3. Delete the second "Form-meaning mismatch" with the "latexCopy" remnant and the "Certainly. I'll build out\dots" line.
4. Delete the ChatGPT-vector-space appendix at the end.
5. Cut the Bayesian formula or relegate it to a footnote.
6. Decide what to do with the orphaned literary epigraphs.

### One concrete suggestion

The Sasha-they passage and the *whose* investigation are the chapter's spine. Restructure around them. Open with Mia and Sasha. Reynolds tries to hear *they*. He fails. Then ask: how could a top syntactician's intuition not match a regular speaker's? Bring in Hankamer and Postal. Reynolds asks GPT-4. GPT-4 disagrees with Hankamer and Postal. So do twelve friends. So does the OED. The chapter has a structure already: the grammarian who is wrong about his own intuition. Reynolds has earned this story. Cut the rest of the chapter to a quarter of its length and let those two scenes carry it.
