## Chapter 18: Swearing

### Strengths
The chapter's framing question (line 10) is exactly the right question for an HPC-account of grammaticality: "Is swearing a norm violation because it is obscene, like a couple lustily copulating in a McDonalds booth or a pile of shit on the floor of an elevator, or because it is ungrammatical?" That single sentence formulates a question that could organize a whole chapter: are taboo violations grammatical violations, or are they something else?

The two epigraphs (Atwood, then a second unattributed quote on the inexpressibility of experience) gesture at the chapter's possible lyrical register. They aren't doing much work, but they're not damaging.

The chapter is currently a stub of about 250 words.

### Major concerns
This chapter doesn't exist yet. So the review is mostly about what the chapter should do.

A trade-book chapter on swearing has rich material to draw on. Here are the things this chapter must do (in order of priority):

1. **Distinguish use from mention from exemplification.** The chapter on morality already touched on this, but the swearing chapter must make it central. *Fucker* the word vs. calling someone a fucker vs. quoting someone calling someone a fucker. These are three different acts with three different grammaticality profiles, only one of which (the use) is a swear.

2. **Distinguish epithet from interjection from intensifier.** *Fuck* is grammatically different in *fuck you* (epithet/imperative), *Fuck!* (interjection), *that's fucking expensive* (intensifier). McCawley's classic "Cassidy F-ing Hayakawa" examples and Pinker's *fuck infix* analysis are exactly the right material.

3. **Engage Bergen's *What the F***, Pinker's *The Stuff of Thought*, and Mohr's *Holy Shit*. These are public-facing books on swearing that the chapter must converse with.

4. **Make the central argument explicit.** Probably: swearing has its own grammar (where can *fuckin'* be infixed? *abso-fuckin'-lutely* works; *fu-fuckin'-tball* doesn't). The grammaticality of swearing is itself a grammatical phenomenon, not a moral one. That's a real claim; build the chapter around it.

5. **Address the social-positioning argument that's currently the chapter's main theme.** Lines 12-16 sketch swearing as a register-marker for in-group identity. This is true but well-worn. The chapter should pair it with the more linguistically-specific point about expletive infixation and other syntactic-prosodic constraints.

The chapter also has the typical signs of LLM drafting in its current form: "casual precision," "twisted grammar," "wink of shared understanding," "subversive language that draws lines between those in the know." This is the Wikipedia register of swearing, the magazine-feature register. The chapter needs to be replaced with the actual phonology, morphology, and syntax of expletives.

### Priority fixes
- The chapter as written needs to be replaced wholesale.
- The opening question (line 10) is the right hook; preserve it.
- Build on McCawley's *fucking* infixation analysis and Pinker on *fuck-infixation*.
- Engage Bergen, Pinker, and Mohr on swearing as data, not on swearing as cultural attitude.
- Tie it to the book's larger argument: swearing is a different *kind* of grammaticality (register-and-context-bound) but is itself rule-governed.

### One concrete suggestion
Open with the abso-fuckin'-lutely example. Show that expletive infixation is highly constrained: it has to land before the stressed syllable, the stem has to be more than two syllables, etc. This is a clean piece of grammatical structure that nobody teaches you and yet that all native speakers obey. So swearing has a grammar. Then the central question: when *fuck you* is "wrong," is the wrongness grammatical or social? The chapter's answer is that it's social, but the wrongness rides on a grammatical scaffolding that it cannot escape. End with the in-group/out-group point you already have, but earned this time.

A useful sub-section: the distinction between *epithet* (a word used to insult), *expletive* (a swear in the linguistic sense), and *taboo word* (a word with strong cultural restrictions). These overlap but are not the same. *Slut* is an epithet that's also a taboo word but isn't typically used as an expletive. *Damn* is an expletive that's no longer much of a taboo word. *Fuck* is all three. The grammar tracks each.

### Rhetoric that works (in advance)
The opening framing question (line 10) is sharp and specific. The image of the couple in the McDonalds booth and the pile of shit in the elevator does honest work; it makes the abstract question (is the wrongness moral or grammatical?) concrete. Build forward from this.

### Rhetoric that doesn't
"Imagine swearing isn't about the shock value of certain words, but instead exists as a secret language hidden within our own. It has its own twisted grammar, a code of expletives and crude turns of phrase that flout everything your schoolteacher drilled into you." (line 12). This is the chapter trying to be philosophical when it should be empirical. "Twisted grammar," "code of expletives," "flout everything your schoolteacher drilled into you" are clichés. Replace with one example: *abso-fuckin'-lutely* but not *fu-fuckin'-tball*. Show the twisted grammar; don't tell about it.
