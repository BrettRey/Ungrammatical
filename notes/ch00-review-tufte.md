## Chapter 00: Introduction

### Strengths
The opening example list (six "wonderfully bizarre" starred sentences from CGEL) is the chapter's best piece of evidence-display: a small typographic table that does in seconds what paragraphs of explanation could not. The historical narrative about Schleicher and Sweet is well-paced. The Mill epigraph at the top earns its space. The autobiographical interludes are restful in the way Bringhurst would approve of: white space between bookish argument.

### Major concerns
This is a chapter about a typographic mark, the asterisk, and yet there is not a single image of one used in context, no facsimile of Schleicher's reconstructed *dent, no scan of Sweet's 1892 page, no side-by-side of "asterisk-as-reconstruction" vs. "asterisk-as-ungrammatical." The chapter argues that one symbol carries two distinct meanings and never lets the reader see them next to each other. That is a wasted opportunity.

Worse, the chapter ends by quoting Householder (1973) on the inadequacy of the binary asterisk, "what counts as ungrammatical varies," and the binary judgement is shown nowhere. If the book's thesis is that grammaticality is gradient and multidimensional, the introduction should plant a visual seed of that thesis, not assert it.

The historical material has the AI-tic of compounding: words such as "groundbreaking," "transformative," "captivating," "obsessive," "relentless," appear at high rate in the Schleicher passage, and the chapter would be tighter if those compounded with a few small images.

### Priority fixes
1. Add a single page-spread figure: the asterisk's two careers. Left panel: Schleicher's reconstruction tree from *dent down to tooth/Zahn/dens/odontos/danta. Right panel: Sweet's first ungrammatical asterisk in context. One mark, two meanings, on one page.
2. Add a small typographic plate showing the asterisk drawn at six different sizes/styles across IPA, Bible commentary, footnotes, comparative reconstruction, generative syntax. Show that the symbol travels.
3. Cut at least one of the LLM-padded biographical paragraphs about Schleicher; the prose is doing what an etymology table could do faster.

### One concrete suggestion
Build a two-panel figure on facing pages titled "One asterisk, two jobs."

- Left panel: a small Schleicher-style cognate fan. Centre node: *dent. Five spokes radiating to tooth (English), Zahn (German), tand (Dutch), dens (Latin), danta (Sanskrit). Each spoke labelled with the sound-shift rule (e.g., Grimm's Law step). The asterisk on *dent in 18-point. This shows: asterisk = unobserved-but-reconstructed.
- Right panel: a stripped-down rendering of Sweet's 1892 sentence pair. Top row: "These tall men" -> "These men are tall." (no star) Bottom row: "Some Englishmen" -> *"Englishmen are some." (with starred output). Set both in Sweet's period typography. This shows: asterisk = unobserved-because-impossible.

The reader leaves the introduction having seen, not only read, the polysemy of the mark. The rest of the book is then about which job the asterisk is doing in any given case, and the figure becomes a recurring reference point.
