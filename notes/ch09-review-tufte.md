## Chapter 09: Without Grammar

### Strengths
The Ethan transcript at the start (lines 9-20) is the chapter's best evidence: an actual recording of an 11-month-21-day-old child's one-word utterances. This is data, not summary. The reader sees what "ungrammatical" looks like at one extreme of the developmental gradient.

The Bloom 1973 example *Mommy sock* with its two glosses ("Mommy is putting a sock on me" / "Mommy's sock") is exactly the kind of single-form, two-meaning datum that benefits from minimal typographic display.

The Riau Indonesian dialogue (lines 141-146) is similarly good: four short turns showing what a low-grammar exchange looks like in practice.

### Major concerns
This chapter is in unfinished shape. Three trailing fragments:
- Line 122: "John McWhorter, who cut his teeth in linguistics working on creoles." (stops)
- Line 124: "But David Gil s" (stops mid-word)
- Line 175: "Now, returning to Riau Indonesian," (stops)

The Hurford block quote at line 178 is OCR-mangled: "condensing a mess variety," "sign languages give us a windedles," "presyntactic, pragmatic mode" -- this passage was scanned and never proofread. It is unreadable as it stands.

The Sumatra travelogue (lines 95-106) is two pages of geography and trade history before getting to a single Riau Indonesian fact. Most can be cut.

The chapter never builds the comparison it begs for: a small grid showing what grammatical scaffolding is present or absent across (a) child language, (b) pidgins, (c) creoles, (d) Riau Indonesian, (e) Standard English, (f) Japanese. The Yagua-vs-English-tense footnote and the banana-vs-bananas case are gestured at but never displayed side by side.

There is no figure. The chapter argues for a typological gradient in grammatical density and never plots it.

### One concrete suggestion
Build a single horizontal-band table titled "How much grammar is in your language?"

X-axis: rough scale from "least grammar" (Ethan at 11 months, single words) to "most grammar" (Yagua, with seven tenses).

Each row is a language or stage. Cells are coloured according to which grammatical machinery is present:

Columns: word order (rigid?), tense morphology, number morphology, person agreement, definiteness, predicate/attribute distinction, subordination, adpositions.

Rows (top to bottom):
- Ethan, 11 months
- Pidgin (e.g., Tok Pisin early)
- Creole (e.g., Hawaii Creole)
- Riau Indonesian
- Mandarin
- Modern English
- Latin / Russian
- Yagua (7 tenses)

Cells: white for "not grammaticalised," grey for "optional," black for "obligatory."

The pattern that emerges: Riau Indonesian sits leftward of English in nearly all columns, but it has the head-modifier ordering that Ethan and pure pidgins lack. The reader sees that "with grammar" is not binary, that languages vary continuously along independent grammatical dimensions, and that Riau and English are at adjacent points on most columns despite the chapter's framing.

A second small inset: the *Mommy sock* example with its two readings, plus the Riau Indonesian "*cantik gol*" example with its two English-forced readings. Each example annotated: "what English forces you to grammaticalise." The figure shows where languages are required to make distinctions and where they are free not to.

This figure would do for Chapter 9 what the *be*-trying frequency curve should do for Chapter 2: convert a paragraph of typological prose into a glance.
