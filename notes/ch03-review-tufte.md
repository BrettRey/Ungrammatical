## Chapter 03: Being grammatical isn't always enough

### Strengths
The seven minimal pairs at lines 17-51 are an excellent typographic comparison: the inversion-test (Is X Y?) vs. its truncated declarative form, all aligned on the question mark. This is a small-multiple done in pure text. It is the right move for showing degrees of acceptability without forcing the reader through prose paragraphs.

The Riau Indonesian / Pirahã passages give the chapter its anthropological weight. The Henk-and-Trouble passive sentence is a memorable single-example, well-chosen.

### Major concerns
This chapter is a stub in many places. Section 3.1 ("ideas") is two lines of bullet notes about Haspelmath and Zwicky/Pullum that have not been written up. Section 3.3.1 ("Syntactic Satiation") is a heading with no body. Section 3.4 ("Reasons") trails off mid-sentence at line 149 ("but membership in"). Section 3.5 ("Pragmatics") is one block quote and stops. Roughly half this chapter is scaffolding.

Where the chapter is written, much of it is in tonally-off LLM register: "back in the 1970s, when bell-bottoms were groovy and disco was king" (line 94) is the kind of prose that is doing nothing for the argument and is exactly what gets cut on a careful pass.

The seven inversion examples are visually flat: the reader cannot tell at a glance which factor (registers, semantics, length, embedding) drives the variation in starring. They are arranged as a sequence, not a 2D grid, so the explanatory dimensions are hidden.

The dangler / passive section says repeatedly that passives are everywhere and never quantifies. This is exactly the chapter to put a small frequency strip in.

### Priority fixes
1. Fill in the stubs or cut them: section 3.1, 3.3.1, the trailing line in 3.4, and the bare quote in 3.5. Either write them or remove the headings; right now the chapter looks unfinished.
2. Convert the seven inversion examples into a 2 by 4 small-multiple table where rows are factors (clause length, presence of modal, register cue) and cells display the truncated form. Reader sees the variables.
3. Cut the disco-decade prose (94-105). The voice does not earn its space.
4. For the Orwell-and-passive claim (line 168, "There's no evidence of anyone having had any problem with the passive until..."), include a small chart: passive frequency in fiction, journalism, science writing 1900-2000. Even if the chapter just reproduces a known figure from someone else's corpus work, that is what the prose is gesturing at and failing to show.

### One concrete suggestion
Build a single bar/strip plot for "passives are everywhere, but writers think they shouldn't be."

X-axis: passive verbs per 1000 finite clauses (linear scale, 0 to about 200).
Y-axis (categorical, top to bottom): research articles, news reports, fiction, casual conversation, children's books, advertising, blog posts, advice columns about avoiding passives.

Each row is a horizontal strip showing the passive rate. The advice-column row should be shaded a different colour and labelled with a separate axis: number of advice columns telling writers to avoid passives, or the proportion of style guides flagging them. The visual contrast: the writers who advise against passives are themselves writing in genres that use passives at the same rate as everyone else.

Below the chart, a single block-quote: "Avoid passive voice and don't write short sentences. Look at Nietzsche." This pairs the visual evidence with the anecdote that opens the section. The reader sees: the prescription is contradicted by the data, and the data is in the genre doing the prescribing.

This kind of self-undermining-by-evidence display is what *Visual Explanations* praises in Cleveland's plots and Snow's cholera map. Right now the chapter argues by anecdote alone.
