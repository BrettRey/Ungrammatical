## Chapter 16: Unravelling babel

### Strengths
The Tomo and Inya stories — your son and Heike Wiese's daughter, both at three years old, both demonstrating sensitive control over which language goes with which interlocutor — are the chapter's strongest material. They are real, they are specific, they make a clear point about how children acquire not languages but communicative-situations-with-people-attached. That framing of language as "just a communicative situation" is the chapter's most interesting theoretical move and the personal anecdotes earn the right to make the argument.

The Wiese long quote is well-chosen. "Com-sits" is a useful term and Wiese's framing of language as community-oriented practice fits cleanly with the book's broader sociological argument. The Grieve quote on situational diversity is also a genuinely interesting framework for thinking about language complexity, and it could do real work in this chapter and elsewhere.

The vervet/green-monkey "leopard call" example at line 5 is good cross-species evidence that "language" in the human sense isn't just a system of signs.

### Major concerns
The chapter is a fragment. Lines 1-15 are bullet-point notes, then lines 17-22 sketch the chapter's eventual argument, then the actual prose starts at line 24 with the Toronto/SARS story. That story repeats the chapter's earlier note (line 13 already contains the Inya quote that line 39 quotes again), and the Smith and Smith 2019 quote appears verbatim at lines 7 and 32. As written, the file is a draft with both the planning notes and the early prose preserved.

More substantively: the chapter title is "Unravelling babel" but the chapter is about codeswitching, which is what the back-of-book description announces. There's a gap. Babel and codeswitching are connected but not the same. Currently the chapter sketches a theoretical argument (language = com-sit) and tells two anecdotes (Tomo, Inya) but doesn't get to codeswitching as a phenomenon at all. The MacSwan / Poplack / Myers-Scotton / Bhatt-and-Bolonyai literature on codeswitching constraints is missing.

This matters because chapter 16 is one of the chapters that has to do real work for the book's HPC architecture: codeswitching is a place where what counts as "ungrammatical" gets renegotiated by multilingual speakers in real time. There are well-attested constraints (e.g., the Free Morpheme Constraint, the Equivalence Constraint, the Matrix Language Frame) that some bilinguals follow and others don't. Whether or not those constraints are universal is debated, but the data is rich. A book on grammaticality that promises a chapter on codeswitching needs to engage that data. As written, the chapter doesn't.

There is also a Becker concern: the framing "language is just a communicative situation" is provocative and probably right in a sense, but it's also a position that will read to a generative reader as dismissive of decades of careful work showing that codeswitching has predictable structural constraints. If you want to push that framing, you have to engage the constraint literature charitably first.

The Genesis quote opening is fine for a popular book but the connection to the chapter's actual content is currently unclear.

### Priority fixes
- Decide what this chapter does. If it's about codeswitching, get to codeswitching. If it's about com-sits as the primary unit of grammatical knowledge, retitle it.
- Remove the duplicated Wiese quote and the duplicated Smith and Smith quote.
- Engage the codeswitching literature: MacSwan, Poplack, Myers-Scotton, Sankoff and Poplack, Bhatt and Bolonyai. At least one of the constraint frameworks deserves a paragraph.
- Develop the Tomo story into a working illustration of the chapter's claim, then bring in attested codeswitching data.
- Source-ground the vervet leopard-call claim. Cheney and Seyfarth is the canonical reference; check that Price 2014 actually says what you cite.
- Source-ground the Smith and Smith 2019 reference. The quoted passage doesn't sound like an academic paper; check the citation.
- The Grieve 2023 situational-diversity-vs-grammatical-complexity claim is a strong empirical claim. Either develop or remove.
- The "communicative situations are languages" line in the chapter introduction needs to be argued, not announced.

### One concrete suggestion
Build the chapter around a single attested codeswitching case study. The Spanglish literature (Lipski, Otheguy, MacSwan) has lots of carefully analysed examples. Pick one or two — say, *La amiga de Mary tiene que dejar de fumar* with mid-clause Spanish and English, where some bilinguals accept it and others don't — and walk the reader through what's grammatical for whom and why. That gives the chapter a concrete object to think about. Then bring in Tomo and Inya as supporting evidence that even children pick up community-specific switching norms. Then the theoretical claim about com-sits as the unit of grammatical knowledge, supported by the data rather than asserted.

Right now the chapter is theory looking for data. A generative reader will trust theory only after the data has been engaged. Codeswitching is a domain where there is a lot of data and a lot of careful theoretical work, and the chapter as it stands is missing both. A reader who knows the field will close the chapter feeling that the book hasn't yet earned its claims about multilingual grammar.
