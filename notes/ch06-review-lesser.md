## Chapter 06: Prescriptivism on the right and on the left

### Strengths

The chapter's central observation is genuinely sharp: the political left, ostensibly opposed to prescriptivism, is the most active prescriptivist force in contemporary English usage policing. And the German counterexample (where gender-marking is the progressive move, the opposite of the English direction) is a real, useful surprise.

Nunberg's epigraph ("racists don't use slurs because they're derogative; slurs are derogative because they're the words that racists use") is the chapter's argumentative spine, and Reynolds's adaptation of it ("bigots don't use these words because they're derogative; the words become derogative when they're the words that bigots use") is the move the chapter needs. That's a real argument and a real reversal.

The footnote on "the blind" as a fusion-of-functions construction (modifier doubling as head) is the kind of CGEL-savvy aside that distinguishes this book from a generic-trade book on grammar. It belongs.

The Jones quote on metaphorical extensions of "blind" is well-chosen, and Reynolds's pushback (the etymological fallacy in reverse, the prediction that deprecation will only narrow usage to bigoted speakers) is exactly the contrarian-but-careful note the chapter needs.

### Major concerns

The chapter is forty lines long. It's a stub plus a chunk of LLM workshopping notes, not a chapter.

The opening four paragraphs read like a Wikipedia summary with citations dutifully appended. "The feminist movement sought to address sexism in language by advocating for more inclusive and gender-neutral terms (Miller 1976). These efforts were aimed at dismantling traditional gender stereotypes..." This is the AI-explainer voice at full strength, and it concedes the whole register before the chapter has a chance to make its actual argument.

The closing section on Beaver & Stanley reads like notes from a chat: "You propose an interplay of formal constraints..."; "If your multi-factorial approach is to incorporate real-time heuristics..."; "Citing them might enrich your argument..." This is an LLM addressing the author, not prose. It must come out.

The German section is underdeveloped. Six sentences mentioning Neiman, Diewald, and Vervecken. A trade reader will want to know how the gender-specific solution actually works in practice (do feminine plurals encompass mixed groups? what happens when a profession has been historically male-dominated?). Right now the section asserts a fact and moves on.

The chapter doesn't deliver on its title. It promises an examination of left and right prescriptivism. It opens with feminist-led de-gendering, pivots to the left's word-level policing, mentions person-first vs identity-first language, then gives a partial discussion of metaphorical-extension banning. The right is barely on stage.

The two paragraphs on disability terminology stop mid-sentence: "the blind and deaf communities" — and then the section ends.

### Priority fixes

1. Remove the LLM-chat fragment at the end (lines 30-41) entirely. Replace with one paragraph in Reynolds's voice on the Beaver and Stanley point: arational influence in language change, how ideology can short-circuit grammaticality intuition.
2. Develop the right-prescriptivism case. Where are the contemporary right-wing language fights? Pronoun resistance, anti-LGBTQ-coded vocabulary, "states' rights" coding, "inner-city" coding. Without this, the chapter's title is a check the chapter doesn't cash.
3. Repair the truncated sentence at the end of the disability-terminology paragraph.
4. Cut the first four paragraphs of historical setup down to two. The reader knows the feminist movement happened; the citations don't add anything.
5. The Nunberg quote should be loaded earlier. It's the fulcrum.
6. Decide what the German example is meant to show. If it's that the relationship between marking-strategy and inclusivity is contingent on the language, say so plainly.

### One concrete suggestion

Worst sentence (a tie): "Empirical and Sociopolitical Ramifications" as a section heading inside running prose, with bullet points framed as "Finally, your paper's section on how grammaticality shifts over time or across communities might dovetail with..." That's not a sentence in a manuscript; it's an LLM coaching the author. Delete the entire trailing section.

Best sentence: "But just as there is no objectively correct way to form a subordinate interrogative in English, there is no objectively correct way to refer to individual racial groups." It compresses the chapter's whole argument into a single comparison, and the comparison itself does the work: if the left accepts the descriptive view of grammar, why does it refuse the descriptive view of vocabulary? That's the contradiction the chapter is built around. The sentence belongs at the start of section two.
