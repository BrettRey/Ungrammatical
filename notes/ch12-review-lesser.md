## Chapter 12: Becoming (un)grammatical

### Strengths

The much / very story is one of the strongest in the book. Reynolds tracks "verray" from late-13th-century borrowing through its 15th-century shift to intensifier, the displacement of "much fair" by "very fair" between the 1470s and 1530s, and the subsequent specialization of the two words. The reproduction of the Caxton 1481 passage in modernized form alongside the original Middle English is exactly the right pedagogical move: the reader sees "moche fair" and feels its loss directly. The frequency-over-time figure is well-placed.

The Jespersen's Cycle exposition for French negation is the book's most efficient piece of historical-linguistic explanation. Three stages, "Je ne marche pas" as "I won't walk a step," and the reader gets the idea. The euphemism-treadmill parallel ("stupid" --> "retarded" --> ...) does double duty: it explains the morphological mechanism and gestures at the social one.

The polarity-items section is cleverly organized: "I couldn't move a muscle" sets up the NPI concept through a familiar English idiom, and from there Reynolds extends to "drink a drop," "lift a finger," "give a damn." The list does work because each item is a small confirmation. And the Israel quote that closes it ("these are not the sorts of facts one is likely to notice about a language, but they are remarkable nonetheless") frames the chapter's whole strategy: pointing out the unnoticed.

The 2023 paper-on-much story and the John Payne contribution ("much" is an NPI in some constructions but not others) is the kind of working-with-collaborators detail trade readers love. The COCA-and-COHA percentages (8% in 1820s, 31% in the 1850s) make the diachronic claim concrete.

The Hemingway-on-bankruptcy reference ("slowly and then suddenly") leading into the S-curve graph is well-handled.

### Major concerns

The chapter doesn't end. The "Words" section is one sentence ("There dwelled Abram in wealth and in frith") and a question. The "Assertion and presupposition" section is partially developed. Then a quote from Cohn-Gordon (good quote), then an O'Connor quote on vagueness, then... a new chapter heading appears mid-file: \chapter{The onward march of \textit{do}-support}. Two chapters are sharing one .tex file.

The do-support chapter that follows is over-long and wears its LLM origin on its sleeve. Phrases like "These constructions, now so integral to the fabric of Modern English, were once entirely absent from the language" are the AI-explainer voice at full volume. Closing paragraphs include "we are all just witnesses to the never-ending dance of language, participants in a global conversation that stretches back millennia and will continue long after we're gone" and "the never-ending dance of language" and "this vast, unfolding story." This is the AI-aspirational closing genre.

The McWhorter quote at the end ("Language is not a protocol legislated by an authority but rather a wiki that pools the contributions of millions of writers and speakers") is fine but appears unsourced.

The Caxton passage has a small error (the modernized version says "Lde" but the original has "lde": consistency would help).

### Priority fixes

1. Separate the two chapters. The do-support material is properly chapter 12.5 or its own thing; right now it's appended to "Becoming ungrammatical."
2. Cut the closing two paragraphs of the do-support chapter ("In the end, the story of do-support and SAI is not just about a quirky grammatical rule..." through "the power and beauty of human language in all its ever-changing glory"). Those are AI-finale prose.
3. Develop or cut the "Words" stub. The "frith" question is tantalizing but undeveloped.
4. Source the McWhorter quote or paraphrase it.
5. Tighten the Israel-citing polarity-item discussion. Strong as it is, it's a detour from the much/very narrative.

### One concrete suggestion

Worst sentence: "In the grand scheme of things, we are all just witnesses to the never-ending dance of language, participants in a global conversation that stretches back millennia and will continue long after we're gone." Cut entirely. This is the AI-uplift coda the book must purge. The chapter has done its work; it should stop.

Best sentence: "Like a path in a forest that becomes more defined and specialized as more people walk it, \textit{much} in non-affirmative contexts became more specialized simply through the natural progression of language use." Excellent. The metaphor is exactly right (a worn path is gradient and emergent, not designed), the syntax is plain, and it lands the chapter's core claim about how grammar consolidates without anyone having to consolidate it. Reynolds in his real voice.
