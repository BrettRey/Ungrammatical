## Chapter 11: Seeing grammar in the brain (neurolinguistics)

### Strengths
The two empirical citations Brett has down (Just 1993 on pupillary dilation; Divjak 2024 on HRV drops in response to article errors) are exactly the right kind of physiological evidence for a chapter on the embodied side of grammaticality. The Divjak finding in particular, that HRV drops more when an L1 speaker produces an error than when an L2 speaker does, is fascinating: it suggests that the body's response to ungrammaticality is socially calibrated. That is the chapter's natural payoff.

The chapter is currently a stub: a section title and two sentences. So this review is mostly about what the chapter should do.

### Major concerns
The chapter doesn't exist yet. What's there is a placeholder. So the priority is not editorial but architectural: what does this chapter need to cover, and where in the book does it sit?

A neurolinguistics chapter in a trade book about grammaticality has a real job. It needs to address whether the *feeling* of ungrammaticality is detectable in brain or body, and what that detectability tells us about the nature of grammaticality. Chapter 5 (How grammar feels) already does some of this work via predictive processing, the N400, and the P600. The risk is that chapter 11 either repeats chapter 5 or fails to build on it.

Topics that probably belong here, ranked by priority:

1. **The N400 and the P600**: already mentioned in chapter 5, but they belong here in detail. Brain potentials that respond differently to semantic vs syntactic anomalies. A reader can come away from this chapter knowing that the brain treats "I like my coffee with cream and socks" differently from "The man bit dog the." Cite Kutas and Hillyard 1980 (N400) and Osterhout and Holcomb 1992 (P600). Both are public-facing and well-documented.
2. **Pupillary dilation and HRV (Just 1993; Divjak 2024)**: bodily responses, not just brain responses. The Divjak finding is the chapter's hook because it shows the response is shaped by who is talking.
3. **Aphasia and Broca's area**: the classic story of how brain damage affects different aspects of grammar. Public-facing readers will know the name "Broca." This is a place where the book can show that grammar has neural substrates without fetishizing them.
4. **fMRI and the Hagoort/Friederici tradition**: probably too much detail for trade. One paragraph if at all.
5. **The bird-brain comparison**: songbird syntax (Bengalese finches and others) provides a controlled comparator that helps explain how much of "syntactic" processing might be domain-general. Already gestured at in chapter 5 with the squirrel monkeys; that material may belong here instead.

### Priority fixes
- Decide whether this chapter or chapter 5 holds the predictive-processing/N400/P600 material. Doing both will be tedious.
- Get the L1/L2 asymmetry from Divjak as the chapter's emotional center: ungrammaticality is felt more strongly when produced by someone the listener takes to be a competent speaker. That is a deeply social fact about a putatively neural response, and it goes against any naive nativist account.
- Pull the squirrel monkey and rock hyrax material from chapter 5's section 6 here if it survives the cut. Cross-species comparators belong with the brain chapter.
- Decide on Broca: he's both a great character (the patient "Tan") and a cliche; the chapter should mention him only if it has something new to say.

### One concrete suggestion
Anchor the chapter with one image: a person hooked up to an electrode cap (or a heart monitor, or a pupillometer) hearing a sentence that contains an error. What does the brain or body do? Walk the reader through three findings in increasing complexity: (1) the body responds (pupillary dilation, HRV drops); (2) the brain produces signature waveforms (N400 for semantic anomaly, P600 for syntactic); (3) these responses are modulated by who's speaking (Divjak: L1 vs L2 accent), by what the listener expects, and by context. Close with the moral: the body knows what the grammar is, and what the body knows is socially calibrated. That is the HPC view in physiological costume. The brain doesn't store a list of grammar rules; it stores expectations, calibrated to communities, and registers surprise.

### One concrete word about absence
Trade books have a trick: when a chapter is short, that can be a feature. Don't pad this chapter to match the others. Six pages of well-organized neurolinguistics done in your voice will outperform fifteen pages of literature review. The chapter on neurolinguistics is the chapter where the temptation to LLM-fill is greatest because the topic invites encyclopedic prose. Resist.

### Rhetoric that will work (in advance)
"When our minds work, our pupils swell." This is a perfect opening sentence. Direct, concrete, physical. Build the chapter around it.

### Rhetoric to avoid (in advance)
The temptation will be to write "Recent advances in neurolinguistic research have shed unprecedented light on the neural substrates of language processing." Resist on sight. The chapter should be about findings, not about the field's self-congratulation.
