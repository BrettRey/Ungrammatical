## Overall shape: Tufte's take

### What the book is currently doing structurally

Twenty-one chapters, an introduction, and a long-running closing meditation on *long long*. The opening promises one journey: how do we know what's grammatical? The chapters then visit a domain at a time: the asterisk and Ross-style islands (00-01); gradience (02); pragmatic and conventional non-grammar (03); morality, intuitions, and politics (04-06); generative grammar and discrete combination (07-08); ungrammatical-but-communicative speech (09); real patterns and arbitrary distributions (10); the brain (11); historical change (12); dialect, code, and bilingual mixing (13, 16); fashion and innovation (14); a model statement (15); impossible languages (17); swearing (18); efficiency (19); a "where I went wrong" reprise on *whose* (20); and a single construction traced end to end (21).

Word counts tell their own story. Chapters 04, 05, 15 each run 600-900 lines. Chapters 11, 18, 20 are seven to twenty-five lines of pasted notes. Six chapters under 100 lines. The numbers themselves form a small-multiple chart of completion, and the chart is not a normal distribution. It's bimodal: a few overgrown drafts, many stubs, a thin middle.

### What's working at the level of shape

The hook works. The asterisk-as-shorthand-for-many-things sets up the thesis cleanly: one mark, many sources of badness. That's a real structural promise the book can pay off.

Chapter 21 works as the final exhibit. *Long long* is one construction inspected from every angle the book has by then introduced: cross-linguistic data, historical accident, attributive vs predicative function, learner intuition, social transmission. If the book is about disaggregating "ungrammatical," this is the right closing example because it's a single object that the reader can now see through every lens.

The framing chapters that establish the model (02 ends with the explicit "Model of Grammaticality" enumeration; 15 spells out form-meaning pairing) are doing real load-bearing work, even if 15 is too long.

### What's not working at the level of shape

The book's central problem is that the table of contents promises a survey of domains, but the prose architecture wants to be an argument. A reader scanning the TOC sees "morality, intuitions, politics, fashion, codeswitching, swearing, neurolinguistics" and reasonably expects either a tour (one chapter per domain, parallel structure) or a build (each chapter advances a thesis). The book delivers neither cleanly. Chapters 04 and 06 sprawl across morality, ethics, prescriptivism, and slurs without converging. Chapters 09, 13, 16 all touch reduced-grammar and code-mixing and overlap heavily. Chapters 03, 14, 15 all argue, in different vocabularies, that ungrammatical innovations need a payoff to take.

If the book were a single visual it would be a Venn diagram with HPC at the centre and each domain showing which cluster properties it activates. That diagram does not exist, and the prose is currently doing the diagram's job badly. One figure would replace several chapters of restatement.

The chapter count isn't earning its keep. Twenty-one is a number books reach by accumulation, not by design. The HPC-cluster argument has, generously, six or seven moves: (1) the asterisk hides many things, (2) grammaticality is gradient, (3) form-meaning convention is the engine, (4) communities and registers redraw the lines, (5) some constraints are architectural and don't soften, (6) innovation needs payoff, (7) here's a single construction with all of this on display. Twenty-one chapters spreads each move thin.

The middle sags. Once the reader is past 02-03 (gradience, conventionality), chapters 04-09 read as variations on "ungrammaticality reflects social and pragmatic norms," with each chapter rediscovering the point in a new domain. By chapter 10 the reader has been told repeatedly that grammar is conventional and form-meaning pairing matters; they need a turn, not a restatement.

Chapters 11, 18, 20 are stubs that the TOC pretends are chapters. Either commit to them or cut.

### What would change the shape for the better

Concrete moves, naming chapters by number:

**Cut or fold.** Chapter 11 (neurolinguistics, 7 lines) folds into chapter 05 (intuitions). Pupil dilation and HRV evidence belong with the discussion of what ungrammaticality feels like; they don't earn a chapter. Chapter 18 (swearing, 15 lines) folds into chapter 13 or 16; swearing is a register/community phenomenon, not a separate axis. Chapter 20 (relative *whose*, 25 lines) folds into chapter 21 as a sub-section, since it's the same move (a single construction reanalysed) and chapter 21 already does it better.

**Combine.** Chapters 04 (morality) and 06 (politics) are one chapter, not two. Both are about how ethical and political stances colonise grammaticality talk; splitting them buys nothing and the split forces redundant scene-setting. Chapters 09 (non-grammatical), 13 (across boundaries), and 16 (codeswitching) are arguments about the same thing: that "ungrammatical" presupposes a community boundary, and the boundaries are porous. Make this one chapter with three sections.

**Split.** Chapter 05 (900 lines) is two chapters or one chapter half its length. The "feeling of ungrammaticality" thread and the "*whose*" thread are doing different work. Chapter 15 (632 lines) is the model statement: it should be a tight chapter near the front (right after 02), not a sprawling near-end chapter that re-enters the model after the reader has been promised it for 200 pages.

**Reorder.** Move what is currently chapter 15 to position 03. The model needs to be on the table before the domain tour, not after. Move chapter 19 (efficiency) earlier, near 10, where it belongs with "real patterns" as part of the structural-pressures explanation.

**Add a figure.** A single chart showing which domain (linguistic, moral, political, fashion, swearing, codeswitching, neurolinguistic) activates which cluster property (form-meaning mismatch, community boundary, register, architectural constraint, processing cost, innovation without payoff). Right now the prose hides this structure across nine chapters. The figure would let chapters be shorter because they could just point to their cell in the matrix.

**Retitle.** "How grammatical can you get?" (02), "Help! Help! I'm being repressed" (08), "Without Grammar" (09), "Grammar and glamour" (14), "Unravelling babel" (16) are cute but don't carry their content. Plain titles help readers track the argument.

A retargeted shape: 12 chapters, not 21. (1) The asterisk hides many things. (2) Grammaticality is gradient. (3) The model: form-meaning pairings in communities. (4) Real patterns and arbitrary conventions. (5) Efficiency and architectural constraint. (6) Communities, registers, code. (7) Innovation needs payoff. (8) Becoming (un)grammatical: change. (9) Morality, politics, prescription. (10) The feeling of ungrammaticality (intuitions, neuro, *whose*). (11) Impossible languages. (12) *Long long*. That structure has a spine, and each chapter earns its place.

### The one structural move

Build the matrix figure first, then prune to it. One small-multiple page showing each domain (rows) against each property of grammaticality the book wants to claim is in the cluster (columns). Cells filled with the canonical example from that intersection. That figure will tell you which chapters are redundant (whole rows duplicating a column) and which are missing (empty cells worth filling). Right now the book is asking the reader to assemble that figure in their head over twenty-one chapters. Show it. Then write the half-as-many chapters needed to walk through it.
