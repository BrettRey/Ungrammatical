## Chapter 21: A long, long way

### Strengths

The Hollies opening is the chapter's keystone and it works on three levels: the lyric is recognizable, the difference between *long, long road* and *the road is long* (no reduplication possible) is felt by every reader as soon as they try it, and the song's title gives the chapter a built-in resonance. The reader is in a chapter about reduplication that itself reduplicates *long*. Trade-book writing of this caliber is what the rest of the manuscript should aspire to.

The Turkish woman's *John Paul very very good* is the book's strongest single example of how a learner of English can know more than they "should." Her grasp of English is so limited she omits *is*. She still knows reduplication intensifies. Pullum thinks she's repeating something she heard. Reynolds checks the corpus: *very very* appears 2.5 times per million words. The English exposure is implausibly thin. So the Turkish *çok çok iyi* must be calquing into her English. That tiny detail (corpus frequency, calque, real woman, real Pope, real 2006) is documentary-grade attention.

The international-students-laughing detail is gold. Reynolds gives them *a long long road* (no reaction) and *the road is long long* (immediate laughter). Every time. Beginners. Students who have minimal English. They still know. That moment in the classroom is the chapter's argument as a felt experience.

The Old English / Middle English / Modern English progression through the chapter (no reduplication possible in OE because adjectives couldn't even sit side by side; compounds open the door; reduplication arrives by the 1600s) is a real piece of historical linguistics, and the *small little red flowers* citation from Bulleins (1579) is the kind of quotation that makes a book live.

The cross-linguistic check (Jamaican Creole *blakblak*, North-East Ambae *qi-qilodo*) actually supports the book's thesis: the post-head pattern is rare but possible. We are socialized into one or the other, not programmed.

### Major concerns

The chapter is one of the manuscript's most coherent. The major concern is structural: it lives at the end of the book, and the prompt suggests it was copied from chapter 1. The "%copied from 01.tex" comment at line 1 confirms it. Some material has migrated to chapter 1 already. The duplication needs auditing: which version stays?

The "Old English" section is wonderful but dense. Three pages of Old English declensions, compounds, and the burgeons of the *blackbird* analogy. Trade readers will be lost without a clearer pedagogical hand. The DALL-E illustration of the *burggeat* is charming but maybe over-budgeted: a real medieval gate-house image would be more honest.

The closing material (the 1970s right-right reduplication) is interesting but sketched. The "this one can be predicative" claim is exactly the kind of late twist that earns a section of its own.

The footnoted commented-out passages about Tyrkkö's medical-text corpus and *pure transparent yellow amber* are sitting in the file. Either bring them out or delete them.

On the protagonists question: this chapter handles its protagonists with an enviable lightness. The Hollies are credited with a song. Geoff Pullum gets a parenthetical 2006 citation. The Turkish woman is dignified by being treated as someone who knows something nontrivial. The international students are shown as competent in spite of their incomplete English. The Old English speakers are not treated as primitives. Even Bulleins of 1579 gets to be a person whose phrasing the chapter mines for evidence. Nobody is a villain. Nobody is a fool. Everyone is a person with a relationship to a language. This is the book's best ethical handling of its material.

### Priority fixes

1. Audit overlap with chapter 1 ("copied from 01.tex"). Decide which material lives where.
2. Decide whether the chapter is the book's closer (currently chapter 21) or whether it might fit earlier as a vivid case study supporting an earlier theoretical chapter.
3. Either delete the commented-out Tyrkkö passages or bring them out.
4. Build out the 1970s right-right reduplication section. It deserves more.

### One concrete suggestion

If this is the closing chapter, lean into the closing function. The book has been arguing throughout that grammaticality is a long, long history of small accidents and small functional pressures and small social conventions. Reynolds could end the book by stepping back and saying so explicitly: that what we feel as the firm ground of grammar is the consolidation of centuries of *micel burg geat* and *small little red flowers* and *very very good*. The chapter's title is already doing this work; the closing prose could carry it home.

If this isn't the closer, keep this chapter and write an additional closer that ties the book back to its opening (Komodo island, asterisk, Mill, six oddballs from CGEL). The book opened with weird sentences; it should close with the realization that those sentences are weird because they were never socially walked-into-existence. *A long long way* is exactly the right metaphor for a closing chapter on a book about how grammaticality became what it is.
