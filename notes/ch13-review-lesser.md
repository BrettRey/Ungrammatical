## Chapter 13: Across language boundaries

### Strengths

The chapter contains the book's most rigorous treatment of dialect-internal grammatical structure. The Standard / non-Standard / ungrammatical trichotomy ("Nobody has come to help us" / "Ain't nobody come to help us" / "Isn't nobody come to help us") with the marked-only-as-non-Standard "!" diacritic is a quiet pedagogical triumph. Most trade books would either flatten the dialect distinction or romanticize it; Reynolds calmly distinguishes "nonstandard" from "ungrammatical" with notation and example.

The four-grammatical-differences-in-two-words analysis of "ain't nobody" is the chapter's argumentative core, and it lands because Reynolds takes a phrase that the reader thinks they've already analyzed (just nonstandard, just dialect) and shows that it's actually four simultaneous grammatical innovations: \textit{ain't} for \textit{have}, copular instead of perfect, subject-after-verb in a non-question, and double negation. That's careful linguistics put to careful trade-book use.

The Spears 2015 BIN-living example is a real find, and the camouflaging point ("for something to be judged ungrammatical, it's needs to be noticed") is one of the chapter's strongest claims. The Spears block-quote on student-driven language policing in all-Black schools is exactly the right counter-narrative to anyone who imagines dialect tolerance is a community default.

The Singlish examples are well-chosen and graded by accessibility.

The Putnam and O'Hern alley-residents study is a genuine archival pull. Reynolds even handles the methodological uncertainty (Labov on the data quality) honestly, which gives the trade reader a glimpse of how careful linguistic history works.

### Strong opening, weak middle

The opening five paragraphs are noticeably overwritten. "We speak in many ways. Some of us use academic language for scholarly articles and academic settings with specialized terminology and formal structures differing significantly from our everyday conversations." This is the AI-explainer surveying registers without actually adopting one. The "literary English showcases a broad spectrum," "Bill Withers moves us with the lamentation," "advertising and marketing English is persuasive and catchy" are filler.

Then the chapter pivots to the Bill Withers example and the contraction-avoidance-in-faux-archaic-English point, and from there it's mostly strong.

### Major concerns

The "One language, multiple grammars" section is a section heading followed by a single word ("Balinese") and a six-item bulleted list with no body text. That's an outline, not a chapter section.

The "Gendered language" section presents eight gender-coded utterances and asks the reader to sort them, then poses the comparison ("Is your feeling upon hearing a masculine person say \textit{Oh my gosh!} fundamentally the same as when you hear an Anglophone say \textit{I no like that}?"), but doesn't develop. This is a real question and worth developing, not abandoning.

The Cantone 2009 citation for German-Italian bilingual word order is fine, but the paragraph admits "the data seems inconsistent, and various positions have been staked out and defended" and then doesn't tell us anything more. The reader gets the gesture without the substance.

There's a stray "." line at line 37 that interrupts the paragraph.

### Priority fixes

1. Cut the first five paragraphs. Replace with one paragraph: "We don't speak one English. We move among registers, dialects, and styles, mostly without thinking. The question is what counts as ungrammatical when so much variation is built into competent speech."
2. Develop or remove "One language, multiple grammars." The Balinese register system would actually be a great example here; right now it's just a placeholder.
3. Develop "Gendered language" beyond the question. The competence-mismatch / gender-mismatch comparison deserves a paragraph.
4. Tighten the bilingual-word-order paragraph. Either give the reader a finding or skip.
5. Repair the stray "." on line 37.

### One concrete suggestion

Worst sentence: "In poetry, too, are dances we do not elsewhere do." This is throat-clearing dressed up as poetic register, and the inverted syntax makes it self-conscious. Cut. The chapter doesn't need it; the chapter needs the Putnam-and-O'Hern study to land harder, not introductory throat-clearing.

Best sentence: "This shows that, for something to be judged ungrammatical, it's needs to be noticed." Even with the typo ("it's" for "it"), the idea is sharp: ungrammaticality is partly a perceptual phenomenon. If you don't perceive a dialect feature as different, you don't perceive it as wrong. The BIN-living point is doing real argumentative work in the book's broader case.
