## Chapter 00: Introduction

### Strengths
The asterisk-as-organizing-symbol move is a genuine rhetorical coup. Beginning with a familiar typographic mark and excavating its strange double history (Schleicher's reconstructed forms, Sweet's ungrammaticality) gives a non-linguist a real handhold. The Mill epigraph is well-chosen: it sets up a position your book will partly dismantle, which is the right way to use authority. The six-example battery from CGEL hits the right note, weird sentences are funnier and sharper than dignified ones, and the reader is immediately curious. The Komodo island memoir passage does honest work: it earns the present-tense intellectual restlessness.

### Major concerns
Two voices fight here. One is yours, sailor and grammarian who blogs at *English, Jack*, lover of Kinokuniya. The other is an LLM doing encyclopedia-fill: "Sweet's rebellious spirit and unconventional approach to scholarship can be attributed, in part, to his unique background and educational journey." That sentence is wallpaper. Likewise the Schleicher-and-Hegel-and-Darwin patch, which reads like a Wikipedia gloss with adjectives sprinkled on ("an obsessive desire for clarity," "an unshakable belief"). Cut roughly half of paragraphs about Schleicher and Sweet without losing the argument. The Householder reference at line 89 is dropped in and abandoned: it deserves a paragraph or no mention at all. There is also a missing word ("had had been clear only") and a typo ("placed deep faith my myths"). The phrase "groundbreaking contributions to phonetics" sets off the AI-prose alarm; it appears in a thousand obituaries.

### Priority fixes
- Cut biographical filler about Schleicher and Sweet by half. Keep the asterisk story, lose the inspirational adjectives.
- Remove or rewrite "His background in philosophy had fuelled a desire to question linguistic assumptions and seek scientific rigour." That is the voice of a teacher's college brochure.
- Fix typos at lines 42 and 50.
- Decide whether Householder enters here or in chapter 2; right now he is a hostage.
- The closing "what grammar is, how we know it, and why it matters" is fine. It earns its summary because the chapter ended in the right emotional register (Kinokuniya, FANBOYS embarrassment).

### One concrete suggestion
Open the chapter with the Komodo memory, not Mill. Mill's epigraph is tonally heavy and arrives before the reader trusts you. Lead with "I have memories of hours, whole afternoons, spent on the sixth floor of the Kinokuniya book store in Shinjuku." The book is about how a curious working teacher came to ask "what is grammar?" Open in that voice, then bring in the asterisk by way of the books on those shelves. Mill can become an epigraph for chapter 7 or wherever you need to set up generativism. Let the introduction be Brett's, not Mill's.

### Rhetoric that works
"To give you a sense of what I mean, (...) presents six typical oddballs from *The Cambridge grammar of the English language*." Direct, plural, friendly. "Oddballs" does the work that ten paragraphs of jargon would.

### Rhetoric that doesn't
"It became a silent testament to a profound shift -- a moment when linguistics embraced its identity as a natural science, a relentless search for patterns in the seemingly chaotic evolution of human language." Every adjective is doing publicity work. "Silent testament," "profound shift," "relentless search," "seemingly chaotic." This is the prose of a TED talk teaser, and it tells the alert reader that the human author has stepped out of the room.
