## Chapter 21: A long, long way

### Strengths

The Hollies song lyric ("It's a long, long road / From which there is no return" vs. "The road is long / With many a winding turn") is a beautifully chosen anchor. Same word, same song, two grammatical positions, and the reduplication works in one and not the other. A reader can hear the difference immediately. That a working linguist could not improve on the songwriter's intuition is the kind of observation that earns trust.

The Geoff Pullum 2006 anecdote about a Turkish woman saying *John Paul very very good* in 2006 is great, and the *very very* / Turkish *çok çok* / English calque diagnosis is the right move. The COCA frequency datum (about 2.5 instances per million words, comparable to *levy* or *whereby*) makes the rarity tangible.

The international-students-laughing detail - they accept *a long long road* without comment but laugh at *the road is long long* - is the chapter's best human anchor. Beginners feel the constraint without being told. That observation deserves more weight than it gets.

The Old-English compound-noun story (*burggeat* `city gate', invisible to surrounding modifiers because of compounding, allowing *micel burggeat* to coexist with the no-double-modifier rule, which then erodes as some speakers reanalyze) is a patient, clever historical reconstruction. The DALL-E illustrations of *burggeat* and *blackbird vs. black bird* are good visual anchors.

The Jamaican Creole example (*im want wan we blakblak* / `he wants one that is very black') and the North-East Ambae example (*qi-qilodo* / `very short') are exactly the right kind of cross-linguistic counterweights to a "this is universal" reading. Reduplicative intensification can occur post-head in some languages; it just doesn't in English, Japanese, or Russian.

The 1579 *Bulleins bulwarke* citation with *small little red flowers* is a delightful early-modern English example of redundant adjectival doubling. It dates the construction precisely.

### Major concerns

The chapter is labeled "copied from 01.tex" at the top. That note plus the *\newpage* immediately after suggest the chapter is a transplant whose seam hasn't been sewn shut.

The middle section (the table of *se hring* declensions in Old English vs. Modern English) is an interesting historical detail but not directly tied to the reduplication story. Why does case-loss matter for *long long*?

The "1970s, a new kind of adjective reduplication appeared" closing - referring to the contrastive doubled-up *right-right* construction - is exactly the kind of contemporary closing the chapter wants, but it's two sentences. That deserves at least a paragraph with one or two real examples ("Are you OK or are you OK-OK?").

Several commented-out paragraphs (*īsern* `iron' having an adjective and noun form, possessives and quantifiers in Old English, the development of multiple pre-head adjectives) are clearly material the author was working with and pulled. Some of it might be worth restoring; without it, the historical chain feels truncated.

The chapter never fully delivers on "why" reduplicative intensification works pre-head and not post-head. Three explanations are floated (a property of the human mind; a meaning shift between attributive and predicative position; a historical accident) and only the third is developed. The author seems to favor the historical-accident view but doesn't say so cleanly.

The Russian example uses transliterated text without IPA, which is fine, but the intransitive judgment (*Yeyo glaza krasivye, krasivye* marked *) deserves a sentence of explanation - is it ungrammatical for the same reasons as the English and Japanese cases?

### Priority fixes

1. Remove the "copied from 01.tex" comment and the *\newpage* break. Sew the seam.
2. Either tie the case-system collapse table to the reduplication story or remove it. Right now it sits as a parallel topic.
3. Develop the contrastive *right-right* / *good-good* construction at the end with at least one real example and a one-sentence claim about whether this might be the route through which post-head reduplication could eventually grammaticalize.
4. Take a position. Of the three explanations floated for why reduplication only works pre-head, which does the author actually believe?
5. Restore one or two of the commented-out paragraphs (the *īsern* iron example is particularly good for showing how adjectives and nouns blurred categorially).

### One concrete suggestion

The students-laughing observation is the chapter's quietest gem and deserves to come earlier. A teacher in a Toronto classroom, ESL students from Korea and Brazil and Iran, the author writes *a long long road* on the board (no reaction) and *the road is long long* (laughter). That's a moment of cross-linguistic intuition coming through people who barely speak the language yet. It's the kind of small classroom scene Zimmer would open a *Times* column with. Right now it's a sentence. Make it a paragraph. Then let the chapter be about why those students could feel the difference even when they didn't have the rule to articulate it. That's the chapter's emotional and intellectual center.
