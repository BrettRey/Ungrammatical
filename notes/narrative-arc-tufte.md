## Proposed narrative arc: Tufte's take

### The arc in one sentence

A single mark, the asterisk, hides at least seven different judgements; the book teaches the reader to see those judgements as overlapping bands on one axis, and ends by watching all the bands light up at once on a single phrase.

### The opening

Chapter 1's job is to put one diagram on the page and earn it. Not the asterisk-as-anecdote, but the asterisk-as-problem.

Open with a wall of starred sentences, twelve to fifteen of them, set as a typographic small-multiple. *They saw what?* *How many people was there?* *Colorless green ideas sleep furiously.* *I ain't done nothing.* *He goed home.* *Long time no see.* *Whom did you see?* *Me and her went.* *What did she doubt the idea that they had seen?* Each marked with its asterisk. Each obviously bad in some way. The reader's eye runs down the column and the question forms itself: these can't all be bad in the same way.

Then the chapter delivers the canonical model-of-grammaticality diagram (the one called for in the visual review). A horizontal axis: fully grammatical, context-coercible, satiable rare type, architecturally blocked. Coloured bands beneath: form-meaning convention, community boundary, register, processing cost, structural impossibility. Each of the fifteen opening sentences gets a tick mark at its appropriate spot. The asterisk shatters into a spectrum.

That single page is the book's mental map. Every later chapter points back to it.

### The development

Three movements, each a deliberate descent into one strip of the axis.

**Movement I: The architecture (chapters 2-4).** The reader's first hypothesis, after seeing the spectrum, is that grammaticality must therefore be hopelessly fuzzy. The book pushes back. Some constraints are sharp. Combinatoriality is real (chapter on discrete combination). Some sentences are blocked by the architecture of the system itself (chapter 17's impossible languages, brought forward). Gradience exists, but it lives between hard walls. The figures here earn their place: a tree, an impossible-language gradient, the coordination paradigm.

**Movement II: The convention (chapters 5-7).** Inside the walls, what fills the space is form-meaning convention sustained by communities. This is where the book does most of its work. One chapter on the model itself (currently 15, brought forward and halved). One chapter on real patterns and arbitrary distributions. One chapter that fuses the current 9, 13, 16 into a single argument: communities draw boundaries, the boundaries are porous, and codeswitching, register-switching, and dialect contact are not exceptions to grammaticality but its medium.

**Movement III: The verdict (chapters 8-10).** Now ungrammaticality as judgement. What does the verdict cost, who issues it, on what grounds? Morality and politics fuse into one chapter (currently 4 and 6 are obviously the same chapter wearing two coats). Intuitions, neurolinguistics, and the *whose* case study fuse into one chapter on what ungrammaticality feels like and where the feeling comes from. Innovation and fashion fuse: an innovation needs payoff to take, whether the payoff is communicative, social, or aesthetic.

### The turn

Chapter 11. *Becoming ungrammatical*: change.

The book has been showing the spectrum as if it were stable. It isn't. The bands move. *Much* drifts toward NPI status across centuries. *Whose* expands. The Jespersen cycle runs to completion. The same diagram from chapter 1 reappears, but now with arrows: tick marks migrating across decades. The reader sees that the spectrum isn't a fixed map but a snapshot of a moving system.

This is the turn the current draft is missing. Chapter 12 of the current order has the materials but not the rhetorical position.

### The resolution

Chapter 12. *A long long way*.

One construction. The book's whole apparatus brought to bear on it. Cross-linguistic data, historical accident, attributive vs predicative function, processing payoff, social transmission, learner intuition. The reader, who has been holding the diagram in their head for eleven chapters, watches every band light up at once on this one small phrase.

The closing image is the same diagram from chapter 1, with *long long* plotted on it. The reader sees the whole book on a single tick mark.

### Why this arc serves the HPC argument

HPC's claim is that grammaticality is a cluster of co-occurring properties held together by mechanisms, not a unified essence. A book that argues this by listing domains in parallel (chapter on morality, chapter on fashion, chapter on swearing) accidentally suggests a list-of-essences view: grammaticality has a moral version, a fashion version, a swearing version. The proposed arc says the opposite. There is one diagram. Each domain activates a different combination of bands on it. The book's shape is the cluster.

The opening puts the cluster on the page. The three movements walk through its components (architecture, convention, verdict). The turn shows the cluster is dynamic. The resolution shows the cluster compressed into one phrase. The reader's path through the book is literally the path their finger traces on the diagram.

### Chapters in proposed order

1. **The asterisk hides many things.** Open with the wall of starred sentences. Build the canonical diagram. Plot the openers on it.
2. **Gradient grammaticality.** Why fuzzy edges don't mean no edges.
3. **Combination and constraint.** Discrete combinatoriality, coordination, the architectural walls.
4. **Impossible languages.** The far end of the axis. What can't be a language, and why.
5. **The model: form-meaning pairing in communities.** Tight chapter, half the length of current 15.
6. **Real patterns, arbitrary conventions.** Why some distributions look like rules and aren't.
7. **Communities, codes, registers.** Fuses current 9, 13, 16. Boundaries and how they're crossed.
8. **Morality and prescription.** Fuses current 4 and 6. Verdicts as social acts.
9. **The feeling of ungrammaticality.** Fuses current 5, 11, 20. Intuitions, neuro, *whose* as case study.
10. **Innovation needs payoff.** Fuses current 3, 14, partly 19. Why some new forms stick.
11. **Becoming ungrammatical.** Change. The spectrum moves.
12. ***A long long way*.** The whole book on one phrase.

Twelve chapters. Each earns its space on the diagram. Bulk halved. Argument visible.
