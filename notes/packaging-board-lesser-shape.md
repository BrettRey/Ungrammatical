## Overall shape: Lesser's take

### What the book is currently doing structurally

The book opens with a small, beautifully chosen object (the asterisk) and a promise: trace what people mean when they call something ungrammatical, and connect the diversity to a homeostatic-property-cluster account. The introduction sets up grammaticality as the puzzle. Chapter 01 plants the linguistic flag (Ross's islands, the Tristan chord, a long enumerated model of grammaticality, an emergence coda). Chapters 02 and 03 work the linguistic core (gradience, conventional preferences, intransitives, passives, danglers).

Then the book pivots outward: morality (04), intuitions and feelings (05), politics (06), generative grammar (07), coordination/conformity (08), language without grammar (09), real patterns (10), neurolinguistics (11), historical change (12), language boundaries (13), fashion (14), what's ungrammatical (15), codeswitching (16), impossible languages (17), swearing (18), efficiency (19), getting it wrong (20), and a long-form case study on reduplication (21).

Twenty-one chapters. Wildly uneven lengths: 900 lines (intuitions), 724 (morality), 632 (what's ungrammatical) sit beside 7-line and 15-line stubs. Several chapters are scaffolds with bullet-list ideas and unincorporated quotes. Chapters 04 and 05 in particular contain stretches of raw ChatGPT prose. The whole-book table of contents reads like a syllabus, not an argument.

### What's working at the level of shape

The asterisk doorway works. The HPC promise is genuinely the right backbone for this material: the diverse, overlapping things people mean by "ungrammatical" really are a homeostatic cluster, and that's what gives the book a thesis instead of a tour. Chapter 01's "Stories from syntactic islands" is a strong second beat, a clean pivot from history to phenomenon. Chapter 21 ("A long, long way") is the single best-shaped chapter in the manuscript: it picks a small construction, follows it across languages, back through Old and Middle English, and shows what the HPC view actually buys you. That is the argument-as-narrative the book keeps promising and only sometimes delivers.

The personal threading (Tokyo bookstores, Humber FANBOYS, Toronto SARS, the bilingual son) has potential. When it lands, it's the connective tissue the book needs.

### What's not working at the level of shape

This is two books. Maybe three.

Book A is the linguist's book on grammaticality as an HPC kind: islands, gradience, generative grammar, real patterns, becoming ungrammatical, impossible languages, efficiency, the long-long-way reduplication case study, getting whose wrong. That's an excellent trade book waiting in there.

Book B is a sociology of judgment: morality, politics, intuitions, conformity, fashion, dialect, codeswitching, swearing. Some of this is essential to the HPC argument (boundary phenomena, the "grammaticality is more than syntax" claim), and some of it is a different book about how people use language to police each other.

The reader feels the seam most acutely between chapters 03 and 09. Chapters 04, 05, 06, 08 are almost entirely scaffolding. Chapter 04 (morality) at 724 lines is the worst offender: a Jahangir Khan biography, ethical frameworks summarized, contractualism applied to AAVE habitual *be*, and almost no traction on whether morality genuinely illuminates grammaticality. Chapter 05 at 900 lines is the same problem at greater length, ending in pages of unaltered ChatGPT vector-space prose.

Then there's a structural muddle in the middle. Chapter 07 (Generation Gap) repeats material the introduction and Chapter 01 already cover. Chapter 11 (neurolinguistics) is seven lines. Chapter 18 (swearing) is fifteen. Chapter 20 (getting *whose* wrong) is twenty-five lines but the *whose* material has already been done at length in Chapter 15. The book ends, structurally, in mid-air: Chapter 21 is the strongest chapter but the manuscript provides no closing argument to hold it open.

The thesis the introduction promises (HPC, the cluster of meanings) shows up in the model in Chapter 01 (the long enumerated list, sections 7.5–7.13), then mostly disappears as a structuring force. By Chapter 13 the reader has lost the thread.

### What would change the shape for the better

Compress to fifteen chapters, reordered into three movements.

**Movement I: The puzzle (intro through cluster).**
- 00 Introduction. Cut the personal section, push to a brief preface or absorb into Movement III. End with the HPC promise stated plainly.
- 01 Islands and the model. Keep the islands opener. Move the long enumerated model of grammaticality (currently 01 §7) out of this chapter into a free-standing Chapter 02 titled "What grammaticality is": this is your thesis chapter and currently it's buried.
- 02 What grammaticality is (new). The cluster, the model, emergence. This is where the HPC backbone lives. State it once, here, clearly.
- 03 Gradient grammaticality (current 02). Tighten.

**Movement II: The cluster in action (the linguistic core).**
- 04 Real patterns + Becoming ungrammatical (merge current 10 + 12). Both chapters trace conventional patterning over time; they are doing the same work and belong together.
- 05 Generative grammar and its limits (current 07, expanded with the "is grammatical" debate now scattered across 02, 07, 17). Cut the redundancy with 00 and 01.
- 06 Without grammar (current 09). Tighten the over-italic prose.
- 07 Across language boundaries (current 13). Absorb the relevant parts of current 16 (codeswitching) here. They're one chapter pretending to be two.
- 08 Efficiency (current 19). Good chapter.
- 09 Impossible languages (current 17). Pair with 08 thematically.
- 10 A long, long way (current 21). The case study. This belongs in the heart of the book, not the end, because it's where the HPC argument finally pays off.

**Movement III: The boundary phenomena (where the cluster fades).**
- 11 How grammar feels (current 05, cut to a third its current length, drop the ChatGPT vector-space material entirely).
- 12 Fashion, swearing, and the social edge (merge current 14 + 18 + the relevant pieces of 03 §Reasons and 06). Single chapter on style, in-group marking, expressive deviation. This is one of the most distinctive things the book has to say. Right now it's in pieces.
- 13 Politics and prescriptivism (cut current 04 to its useful core, merge with 06). Drop the contractualist exposition. Keep the AAVE habitual *be* discussion. Land the "left and right both prescribe" point in two pages, not eighty.
- 14 Getting it wrong (the *whose* material from 15 + 20). One chapter on how speakers and linguists alike misjudge grammaticality.
- 15 The asterisk and what comes after. A real conclusion. Currently the book has none.

Cut entirely: Chapter 08 (Coordination), Chapter 11 (neurolinguistics stub), the duplicated *whose* material in 20, the impossible-languages opening notes file. Chapter 11 is seven lines that say "pupils dilate, HRV drops" — fold those two facts into Chapter 11 (the new one, on feelings) and be done.

### The one structural move

Move the model of grammaticality out of the islands chapter and make it Chapter 02. This is the single highest-leverage change. Right now the book's central thesis is buried as section 7 of a chapter named "Stories from syntactic islands," after Wagner, Herbie Hancock, Goold Brown, Lowth, Ann Fisher, plagiarism, and tearful farmers. By the time the reader reaches the enumerated cluster model, they have lost the question it answers. Pull the model forward, name the chapter for what it is, and every subsequent chapter becomes a test or extension of one explicit claim instead of a new topic introduced cold. The book stops being a tour and starts being an argument.

Do that, cut roughly a third of the manuscript, and what's left is a real book.
