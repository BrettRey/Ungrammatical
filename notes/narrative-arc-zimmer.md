## Proposed narrative arc: Zimmer's take

### The arc in one sentence

A linguist hears a song on the radio, notices something strange about it, and spends the rest of the book chasing what "wrong" actually means, until "ungrammatical" comes apart in his hands and reassembles as a cluster of overlapping things people have been calling by one name.

### The opening

Open in a Toronto classroom. The author is teaching an English class. ESL students from Korea, Brazil, Iran. He writes on the board: *a long long road*. No reaction. He writes: *the road is long long*. The room laughs. Beginners who don't yet have the rule can feel the rule. That's the opening scene, two paragraphs, no theory.

Then the second beat: he is in a car listening to the Hollies. *It's a long, long road / from which there is no return.* And then: *The road is long, with many a winding turn.* Same word, same song, two grammatical positions, and the songwriter knew which one took the doubling. The linguist did not improve on the songwriter's intuition. That is the puzzle.

Then a small confession: I am the linguist who could not improve on the Hollies. And I have been chasing this kind of "wrong" for decades.

The asterisk-genealogy (Schleicher, Sweet, Householder) becomes chapter one, not the opening. It earns its place once the reader cares about the symbol; it cannot earn its place cold. Schleicher dying at 47, Sweet writing *it is me* in the preface: these are scenes, but they're scenes about a tool. Let the reader meet the puzzle the tool was built to mark before they meet the tool.

### The development

The middle is built around the working linguist on a lead. Each chapter is a question and a chase. The reader follows him to a corpus, an email exchange, a kitchen, an alley in 1955 Washington, a 1481 Caxton folio. The shape is *and then I went to.*

Movement one (chapters 2 to 5): **what does the asterisk hide?** Reduplication, islands, gradients, the *probable to play* discovery. Each chapter ends having complicated the asterisk a little more. The reader leaves this movement believing that "ungrammatical" is not one thing.

Rest stop: the *whose* investigation. A long, lived chapter about chasing a single construction across emails, family, friends, Wikipedia. This is the book's first proof-of-concept that a linguist's question can be a science-writing chase.

Movement two (chapters 7 to 10): **how does grammar feel?** The metacognitive-feeling argument, the brain chapter, the efficiency story. The abstraction load is highest here, so this is where the rest stops have to be most physical. Pupil dilation. Hankamer and Postal. A specific subject in a specific lab with a specific sentence.

Rest stop: Caxton 1481, *moche fair*. Six hundred years of *very* eating *much*. A scene the author can write because he has actually held the page.

Movement three (chapters 12 to 15): **whose grammar?** The combined domain chapter (what was Politics, Fashion, Codeswitching, Swearing, plus the moral case from the cut-down chapter 4). One chapter, five sections, one thesis: the same form is licit in one community and illicit in another, and the licensing involves identity, register, and stance. The Cory Doctorow exchange. Tomo in the kitchen. The Putnam-and-O'Hern alleys. The Pullum *Rubymar* email.

### The turn

Chapter 16: **the cluster snaps into focus.** This is the synthesis chapter the book currently does not have. After the reader has watched "ungrammatical" mean a dozen different things across a dozen vivid scenes, the author names the pattern. The diversity is not noise. The cluster is the kind. There is no single property that makes a sentence ungrammatical. There is a homeostatic cluster of co-occurring properties, and the cluster is what we have been pointing at all along.

This chapter has to feel like a hinge. The reader should feel the floor tilt. Everything before reframes; everything after is consequence.

### The resolution

Chapter 17: **getting it wrong.** What we lose when we treat grammaticality as a single thing. Prescriptivism, AAVE judgments, ESL instruction, AI evaluation. The cost of the asterisk taken too literally.

The book closes back where it began: the road, the students laughing, the songwriter who knew. The reader's takeaway in one sentence: *grammaticality is not what you thought it was, and that is a relief, not a problem.*

### Why this arc serves the HPC argument

The HPC account is structurally hard to sell to a trade reader: it asks them to accept that a familiar concept is a cluster rather than a thing. That's a move you cannot make by definition. You can only make it by accumulation. The reader has to feel, scene by scene, that "ungrammatical" keeps meaning something a little different, until the cluster is the only way to make the diversity stop feeling chaotic. The arc above is engineered to deliver that accumulation. The synthesis chapter cannot land before the reader has felt the diversity in their hands.

### Chapters in proposed order

1. **The road is long long** (was 21). Hook scene, classroom and Hollies, no theory.
2. **The asterisk** (was 00). Schleicher, Sweet, Householder, the symbol's prior life.
3. **Islands** (was 01). Ross 1966, the first technical complication.
4. **Gradients** (was 02). Toy grammars, fuzzy edges. Cut by a third.
5. **Not enough** (was 03). Riau, relative clauses, the question of what counts as evidence.
6. **The generation gap** (was 07). What generative grammar promised. Cut Chomsky's biography.
7. **Real patterns** (was 10). The *probable to play* corpus chase. A model chapter.
8. **The whose investigation** (extracted from 05 and 15, combined). Lived investigation.
9. **How grammar feels** (was 05, cut by two-thirds). Hankamer-Postal, Knobe, no ChatGPT vector spaces.
10. **Grammar in the brain** (merged 11 and 19). Pupil dilation, dependency locality.
11. **Becoming ungrammatical** (was 12). *Very* eating *much*, Caxton.
12. **Across language boundaries** (was 13). Putnam-and-O'Hern, AAVE.
13. **Without grammar** (was 09). Holophrastic, telegraphic, aphasic.
14. **Whose grammar?** (merged 06, 14, 16, 18, plus moral case from cut-down 04). The domain chapter.
15. **Conformism** (was 08). Coordination as social.
16. **What grammaticality is** (synthesis, new or rebuilt 15). The cluster, named.
17. **Getting it wrong** (was 20, expanded). The cost of the asterisk taken too literally.
