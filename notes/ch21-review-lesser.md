## Chapter 21: A long, long way

### Strengths

The chapter is built around one of the book's best small puzzles. Why is "a long long road" perfectly grammatical and "the road is long long" laughable? Two superficially identical reduplications, two opposite verdicts. Reynolds frames the puzzle clearly with The Hollies' "long, long road" and the second-verse "road is long," and the reader feels the contrast immediately.

The Pullum / Turkish-woman / Pope anecdote ("John Paul very very good") is an exceptional small fact: a non-native speaker who has internalized so much English that she drops "is" and yet preserves the modifier-reduplication pattern. The calque from Turkish "çok çok iyi" plus Reynolds's empirical follow-up (very-very appears half as often as "levy" or "whereby" in COCA) is the chapter's empirical core, and it lands with conviction.

The cross-linguistic tour (Japanese "nagai nagai michi" works, "michi ga nagai nagai" doesn't; Russian "krasivye krasivye glaza" works, "glaza krasivye krasivye" doesn't) is well-handled: the same pattern holds across structurally different languages.

The Old English material (the no-two-adjectives-in-a-row constraint, the workaround through compound nouns like "burggeat") is the chapter's most striking piece of historical analysis. The DALL-E illustration of "folks near a burggeat" is a charming visual touch even if the figure caption is slight. The argument that compound-noun analysis allowed listeners to gradually reanalyze "micel burg geat" as "micel blæc geat" with two contiguous adjectives is genuinely elegant historical-linguistic reasoning.

The Jamaican Creole "blakblak" and North-East Ambae "qi-qilodo" predicative-position reduplications are a fine concluding move: the universal-grammar prediction is wrong; predicative reduplication does exist in human languages. "We're not programmed to prefer one kind of duplication over the other. We're socialized to do so."

The 1970s "compounding" reduplication for clarification ("right-right") at the end is a nice contemporary follow-up.

### Major concerns

The chapter is titled at line 1 with "%copied from 01.tex" as a comment. That's a working note that shouldn't survive into the final book. The chapter then has both a \chapter and \section line with similar names ("A long, long way" and "A long long way"), suggesting copy-paste residue.

The teaching anecdote about international students laughing at "she's good good" is reused twice, once at the chapter's opening (line 26) and once at the chapter's close (line 240). Pick one.

The Middle English declension section (lines 152-191) is dense and technical for the chapter's general argument. Six lines of declension tables before Reynolds gets back to the adjective question. A trade reader may need this background, but they need it more concisely.

The "footnote" on Shakespeare's "o mistress mine" appears in chapter 19 and should not be reused here. Cross-check.

The "Cambrian fossil" / "Cambrianicity" passage on line 97 is commented out; if it's there for a reason, decide whether to restore it or delete it.

The Old English material is brilliant but has a structural problem: the chapter introduces declension loss, adjective-noun blurring, and the re-analysis from compounds-to-multiple-adjectives all in quick succession. Some sequencing would help.

### Priority fixes

1. Remove the "%copied from 01.tex" comment.
2. Resolve the duplicated chapter / section heading.
3. Cut one of the two appearances of the international-students-laughing anecdote.
4. Decide whether the commented-out Cambrian-fossil passage belongs.
5. Consider trimming the declension table to one row plus a one-sentence summary; the full table is more detail than the chapter needs.
6. Cross-reference (or move) the heavy NP shift footnote that appears in chapter 19 ("I grant you wishes three" / "o mistress mine").
7. The chapter is positioned as a kind of capstone (it's chapter 21 of 21) but doesn't quite act like one. Consider adding a brief closing paragraph that ties the long-long argument back to the book's central HPC claim: that grammaticality is a cluster of historical accidents, conventions, and constraints that congeal into intuitions we can't introspect on but can reason about.

### One concrete suggestion

Worst sentence: "We might speculate that there is some property of the human mind makes this construction impossible in predicative functions." A small grammar slip ("makes" wants "that makes" or "to make"). Beyond the grammar, the sentence is itself the sort of speculation it's flagging; the chapter then declines to either endorse or refute the speculation and just moves on. Either commit to a hypothesis or omit.

Best sentence: "In other words, we're not programmed to prefer one kind of duplication over the other. We're socialized to do so." A two-sentence punch that closes the cross-linguistic tour and lands the chapter's core claim. Plain, declarative, and exactly the contrast the book has been building toward: grammaticality is conventional, not biological. Reynolds's voice is at its strongest in moments like this. The book should end on a note like this one.
