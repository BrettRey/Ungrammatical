## Chapter 13: Across language boundaries

### Strengths
The Englishes Venn diagram (figures/EnglishesVenn.png) at \ref{fig:enter-label} is a real attempt at structural display: the dotted vs. solid lines do useful work distinguishing permeability levels, and the nesting (Standard inside English, AmEng inside Standard, AAE inside AmEng, Singlish on the periphery) maps out the chapter's central claim. It is one of the more ambitious figures in the book.

The four-way example list (\ref{ex:nobody-has-come} through \ref{ex:hasn't-nobody}) is a clean small-multiple: the same proposition rendered in Standard, AAE, and two starred forms shows the asymmetry directly.

The Singlish examples (lines 33) ranging from "this person's Singlish is very good" through "this guy Singlish damn good leh" to "Wah lau! This guy Singlish si beh hiong sia" is a near-perfect three-step gradient illustrating the chapter's claim about non-Standard varieties.

The 1955 Putnam-O'Hern study at lines 126-136 is excellent historical anchoring: 74 alley residents, 12 speakers, 70 listeners, and a striking rank correlation between perceived social status and actual status from speech alone.

The Spears 2015 BIN/been camouflage example is sharp and well-explained.

### Major concerns
The Englishes Venn diagram has some legibility problems. The label "Singhish" (line 28 in figures/EnglishesVenn.png) appears to be a typo for "Singlish." The "American English" subset is plotted but no other regional Englishes are shown (British, Australian, Indian, etc.), which makes the choice of just American + AAE feel arbitrary and the diagram less universal than its claim. The dashed-line/solid-line distinction is not visually salient enough.

The Matt Shirley Venn diagram at \ref{fig:context-expressions} is a meme repurposed as a figure, but its three categories ("Starting a Fight," "Petting a Dog," "Consoling a Lost Child," "Seeing Someone Whose Name You Don't Remember") are not actually about language boundaries. It is a tonal interlude that does no analytical work in this chapter. Cut it.

The "Microdialects" section (lines 123-136) tells a great story about Putnam-O'Hern but doesn't show their data. There is no figure showing the rank correlation, no breakdown of which features tracked status. The 70-listener-12-speaker design is a 2-axis quantitative result and gets prose only.

The "Gendered language" section (105-121) is one example list and a question. The chapter raises gender mismatch and competence mismatch as two kinds of "ungrammaticality" and doesn't develop the comparison. Stub.

The "One language, multiple grammars" section (94-103) is a single bulleted list under "Balinese" with no body text. Stub.

### One concrete suggestion
Replace the current Englishes Venn diagram with a directed-graph display: "Mutual intelligibility, mutual judgement."

Each variety as a node: Standard British English, Standard American English, Australian, Singlish, Indian English, Jamaican English, AAE, Scots.

Between each pair, two arrows in opposite directions, weighted by:
- Arrow A -> B: percent of speakers of A who would judge a B-form sentence as "grammatical English." (or who can fully understand it)
- Arrow B -> A: same in reverse.

Standard varieties typically have thick outgoing arrows (their forms are accepted by most speakers of other varieties) and thinner incoming arrows (their speakers judge non-Standard forms as ungrammatical). This produces an asymmetric, not symmetric, Venn-style display: you cannot show this with overlapping circles, but you can show it with arrows.

Below this diagram, a single inset table showing the four AAE *Ain't nobody come to help us* features (ain't+have, post-aux subject, double negation, BIN) each with a checkmark column for which other Englishes share each feature.

The reader sees in one display: AAE shares some features with other Englishes (double negation with Italian, ain't with informal British) but bundles them in a way that triggers the cross-variety asymmetric judgement. The Venn cannot do this. The directed graph can.
