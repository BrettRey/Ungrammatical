## Chapter 20: Getting grammaticality wrong

This chapter is essentially a stub. Twenty-five lines, three Old English / Middle English examples on relative *whose* and *who*. There's no chapter to review, but there is a chapter to design. Given the book's architecture and what's promised by the title, here's what should be here.

### Strengths (of what's here)
The historical examples are well-chosen. The 1325 *Doȝter wo miȝte is eir be* (line 14) is a nice early attestation of relative *who*. The Old English *þe his tohopa bið to Drihtne* (line 22) showing the *that his* construction that historically preceded *whose* is exactly the right kind of evidence. These are useful primary-source data points.

The chapter title is also good. "Getting grammaticality wrong" is the right title for a chapter near the end of the book that returns to cases where the field, including the author, has misjudged what's grammatical.

### Major concerns
The chapter is an empty shell. The reader gets three Old/Middle English examples and then nothing. Whatever the chapter is supposed to argue is missing. This is the kind of chapter that needs to do significant retrospective work: cataloguing places where the field was wrong, where the author was wrong, where intuition led astray. With three examples and no argument, the chapter does none of that.

The chapter title promises a meta-reflection on the book's enterprise, and the book needs that meta-reflection. By chapter 20, the reader has been told that grammaticality is gradient, contextual, conventional, social, and constructed. They've seen multiple cases where what looked ungrammatical turned out to be grammatical (the missing *whose*, *probable to play*, the *Rubymar* sentence, syntactic-satiation cases). They've also seen cases where what looked grammatical to one community looked wrong to another (AAE *Ain't nobody*, Singlish). The chapter has the materials for a powerful retrospective: where, in this book, has Brett himself been wrong? Where has the field as a whole been wrong? Where are the persistent edge cases?

What the chapter could and should be doing:
- Walking through cases where the author's own grammaticality intuitions changed during the writing of the book (the *whose* investigation in chapter 5 is one such case; *probable to play* in chapter 10 is another).
- Walking through cases where canonical generative claims have turned out to be empirically wrong (the 1970s passive *by*-rule that Pullum's Osman example breaks, the various cases where corpus evidence overturned introspective judgement).
- Walking through cases of asymmetric error: where the field has been more confident in some directions than the data warrants. The Riau Indonesian case, the Pirahã controversy, the various claims about creole exceptionalism, the long history of treating non-Standard varieties as ungrammatical.
- Reflecting on the cognitive bias toward the universal: linguists habitually overgeneralize from the languages they speak. The chapter could productively cite Whorf, Boas, and the corrective sociolinguistics tradition (Labov, Wolfram, Eckert).

The chapter is doing work the book hasn't yet done elsewhere: looking back at its own argument and saying what it might have got wrong, what's still uncertain, what the reader should hold loosely. That's a Becker-style move (humility before the data), and it would do significant work for the book's reception.

### Priority fixes (chapter design)
- Decide what cases of "getting it wrong" the chapter will treat. A short list would be:
  - The missing *whose* (chapter 5)
  - *Probable to play* (chapter 10)
  - The *Rubymar* infinitival-perfect (chapter 15)
  - The 1970s passive *by*-rule (chapter 3)
  - Maybe one large field-level case: e.g., the linguistics-of-non-Standard-varieties story
- Each case should walk through what the field believed, what evidence emerged, and what we should conclude about how grammaticality judgement works.
- The chapter should close with a methodological recommendation: what working linguists should do when their intuitions conflict with data, with corpora, with non-linguist informants, with LLM judgements.

### One concrete suggestion
This is the chapter that would do the most work for a generative reader's reception of the book. A reader who has been pushed back on for nineteen chapters arrives at chapter 20 ready to be argued with again. If chapter 20 instead says "and here is where I, the author, have been wrong over the years; here is where I had to revise my own judgements; here is where the field, including me, has had to learn from data" — that's the moment the generative reader begins to trust the author. Becker's principle: never criticize an opponent until they feel understood, and never claim certainty for yourself you don't grant to them.

A model: the *whose* investigation in chapter 5 is exactly this. Brett the author had a particular position; new evidence forced revision; the position is now different. That's the chapter's narrative shape, applied to multiple cases. Walk through three or four such cases, showing the work, and the chapter does serious sociology-of-knowledge work for the book's central argument.

The current chapter has 25 lines. The right chapter would be 15-20 pages. The materials exist in the rest of the book; the chapter is the place to bring them together as a single retrospective argument. A generative reader who finishes a chapter where the author has been visibly humble about the limits of intuition will close the book with respect. A generative reader who finishes the current 25-line stub will conclude the book ran out of steam.
