## Chapter 12: Becoming (un)grammatical

### Strengths
The *very/much* opening is one of the book's most successful pieces of historical reconstruction. Caxton's 1481 passage in modernized English, then again in Middle English, is the right way to make the change visible to a non-linguist reader. The figure showing the trajectories of *much* and *very* in EEBO is the right kind of empirical anchor. The Hemingway echo ("the way one goes bankrupt, slowly and then suddenly") earns its place because the figure delivers the S-curve.

The Jespersen Cycle in French is a genuinely satisfying explanation of a famous phenomenon. The chain *ne > ne...pas > pas* with *pas* originally meaning 'step' is the kind of thing that makes a reader want to learn more linguistics. The aside about Italian *non vado mai* (where the single negative is ungrammatical) is the right corrective to the prescriptive line about double negatives in English.

The "discovery story" in Section 1 (the John Payne correspondence about *much* in adjective phrases vs. NPI environments) does what chapter 5's whose-gorilla story does: tells us that this is what working linguistics looks like. Two collaborators going back and forth, somebody noticing something nobody had noticed before.

### Major concerns
**The chapter contains an entire second chapter pasted in.** Lines 179-222 are a chapter titled "The onward march of *do*-support" with its own `\chapter{}` command. This will compile into a separate chapter in the book. The do-support material is interesting but it's been written by an LLM, and visibly so: every paragraph ends in a TED-talk closing ("It's a testament to the resilience and adaptability of the English language"; "we are all just witnesses to the never-ending dance of language"; "the power and beauty of human language in all its ever-changing glory"). The McWhorter quote at the end is real and well-chosen, but the prose around it is generated. **This entire passage needs to be cut, or excised to its own chapter and rewritten in your voice.**

The Cohn-Gordon quote at line 166 is a real find ("everyone acts as if the rules are already common knowledge, and then -- the strangest part! -- they gradually try to work out what this common knowledge is"). But the chapter has switched topics three times by the time it gets there: from *very/much* historical change, to *ne...pas* and Jespersen's cycle, to NPIs, to a section called "Words" that has only one orphan example, to "Assertion and presupposition" that gestures at common ground theory and then ends. Each of these is interesting. None completes itself.

The Israel quotation at line 92 is incomplete: "If something is much smaller than something else, it" - sentence terminates without verb.

The frith example at line 157 is a section opener with no chapter and no payoff: "How does it register with modern readers given that its use is not ungrammatical, but the word is entirely unfamiliar?" The question is good, but the chapter never answers it.

### Priority fixes
- **Cut the do-support chapter** at lines 179-222 entirely from this file. If the do-support story belongs in the book, it goes in its own file with its own carefully written prose, not LLM-generated padding. The McWhorter quote alone can be repurposed elsewhere.
- Finish the Israel sentence at line 92.
- Decide whether the chapter is about *much* (with NPIs as a side topic) or about diachronic change generally (with *much*, *ne...pas*, and *do*-support as instances). Either way, give the chapter a single spine.
- The Words section (line 155) and the Assertion section (line 159) are both unfinished. Either build them out or cut them.
- The closing Joseph quote on Saussure's centripetal/centrifugal forces is good, but the chapter doesn't say anything about it. Add one sentence that explains why this quote is here.

### One concrete suggestion
Build the chapter around three case studies of grammaticalization, each in three pages: (1) *much/very* (positional/semantic specialization through competition); (2) *ne...pas* in French (Jespersen's cycle, with *pas* developing from the most extreme example of taking a step); (3) *do*-support in English (the rise of an auxiliary that wasn't needed). Each case ends with the same observation: speakers don't decide to grammaticalize anything; the change accumulates from many small uses. This is the chapter's HPC argument: grammars are homeostatic property clusters precisely because they tolerate slow drift while remaining stable enough to be useful. The current chapter has all three cases, but the first is excellent, the second is good, and the third is LLM-padded; the structural unity is invisible.

### Rhetoric that works
"Like the poor child who makes good, or the team that comes from behind to win, *very* had a hard row to hoe." Half a sentence personifies a word doing exactly what your evidence has shown it doing. This is the book's voice when the historical detective work has earned the metaphor.

### Rhetoric that doesn't
"It's about the very nature of language as a human invention, constantly being shaped and reshaped by the collective creativity of its users. It's a testament to the resilience and adaptability of the English language..." (line 218). This entire closing paragraph of the do-support pseudo-chapter is the book's worst LLM contamination. The phrase "It's a testament" is a red flag in prose; "in all its ever-changing glory" is the kind of close that begs for an applause sign. Cut every word.

### A note on the source-grounding violation
The do-support chapter contains specific historical claims (a "Celtic origin" hypothesis; the timing of Shakespeare's usage; "embedded inversion" being "documented in various dialects") that no source is given for. Some of these are real findings, others may be confabulations. **All such claims need to be checked against source.** Do not let the LLM-generated do-support passage survive into the book without each claim being verified or cut.
