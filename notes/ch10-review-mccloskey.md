## Chapter 10: Real patterns

### Strengths
The Amelie/Basil/Clara opening is the cleanest piece of teaching in the book. Three names, one verb, four sentences with the same lexical material but different grammatical roles, and the reader sees how grammar carries information that the words alone don't. Then Azerbaijani and Hixkaryana to show that English's pattern is not the only possibility. This is what a trade-book chapter should look like: one example, several languages, one clear payoff.

The *probable to play* discovery is genuine and lovely. The story (1946 NFL bribery scandal, Bert Bell's injury reports, "probable" enters sports vocabulary as a status, *probable to play* emerges by the 1980s) is a perfect demonstration of how an arbitrary historical contingency can let a new construction in through a side door. The footnote about NHL elbows ("Matthews is out with an elbow") is gold; one sentence does enormous work for the chapter's claim.

The historical research on "November the fifth" (Early English Books Online, mid-17th century) is the kind of empirical patience that a chapter on real patterns deserves. Hurford's claim that this format imposes a semantically-unmotivated restriction is real, and your historical answer (bureaucracy, register, written-document brevity) is plausible.

### Major concerns
The chapter contains a major LLM dump at lines 101-114, "A functionalist approach to grammar emphasizes the role of language use..." This is six bullet points written in the style of a grad-school answer, complete with "1. Semantic distinction:", "2. Language use and conventionalization:", "3. Gradience and acceptability:", "4. Diachronic development:". The fifth point starts in non-numbered prose ("Frequency and Entrenchment:"), then a "In summary" paragraph closes it out. **This is unmistakably the output of an LLM asked "how would a functionalist respond to Hurford?" pasted into the manuscript verbatim.** Cut entirely.

The Chris Anderson long-tail digression (lines 93-95) is incomplete and dropped: "but" at the end of line 95 has no continuation. The chapter pulls in long-tail to argue that LLMs can learn micro-constructions, then never says it.

The "spray/load" section is one paragraph of setup followed by an honest admission: "Linguists haven't been able to figure out why there are these two groups of verbs." This is fine, but the chapter doesn't connect it to the chapter's argument. What does spray/load tell us about real patterns? Maybe: that real patterns can be irreducibly arbitrary in the verb-by-verb particulars but still systematic at the construction level. The chapter could make that move; right now it just describes the puzzle.

The chapter's title and its first section are pulling in different directions. "Real patterns" is the Dennett phrase, evoking patterns that are real because they project (predict) reliable behavior. The Amelie sentence shows this beautifully. But the chapter never invokes Dennett or makes the projectibility argument explicitly. The chapter is doing Dennett's argument without naming it.

The "[footnote about Mark Heisler]" needs context: who is Mark Heisler? The reader doesn't know if this is a sports columnist or a linguist. The earliest example matters; the discoverer doesn't.

There's a typo at line 9: "plus a mere quartet of words... plus ." The sentence ends with "plus ." showing an unfinished thought.

### Priority fixes
- **Cut the functionalist LLM dump** at lines 101-114. Replace with one paragraph in your voice: spray/load, *probable to play*, and *November the fifth* all show that grammar tolerates lexical-particular patterns alongside general principles. The HPC view predicts this; strict generative grammar struggles with it.
- Finish the Anderson long-tail thought or cut it. As written, it's an orphaned sentence.
- Fix the unfinished sentence at line 9.
- Tie spray/load explicitly to the chapter's argument. What does the unsolved puzzle of why *spray* alternates and *soak* doesn't show us about real patterns?
- Decide whether to invoke Dennett. The chapter's title and argument are his; saying so might help non-linguists place the argument.

### One concrete suggestion
Make the Amelie example the chapter's spine and use the rest as variations on its theme. Open as you do. Then introduce the term "real pattern" explicitly: a real pattern is one that lets us predict reliably, even when no general rule is given. Then three case studies of real patterns that don't fit clean rules: (1) Spray/load: arbitrary verb-by-verb membership in a construction class; (2) *Probable to play*: a construction that emerges from social-historical contingency, not semantics; (3) *November the fifth*: a frozen formula limited to month names. End with the moral: real patterns are not generated by neat rules. They are stabilized by use. They project. They are real because they predict, not because they obey. This is the HPC argument. Naming it here saves the book several chapters of repetition.

### Rhetoric that works
"Matthews is out with an elbow." One sentence in a footnote earns the chapter's argument. A reader gets that "elbow" has come to mean "elbow injury" through a peculiar sports-journalism convention, and they get it without explanation. This is the book's voice when it's working: spare, witty, evidentially specific.

### Rhetoric that doesn't
"This concept is particularly relevant in the context of digital goods and online markets, where the cost of inventory storage and distribution is significantly lower than in traditional retail settings." The Wired-magazine paraphrase is filler. The chapter does not need to define long tail; the chapter needs to use the long tail. Either show that grammar's micro-constructions are a long tail (which is what you mean), or skip the term.
