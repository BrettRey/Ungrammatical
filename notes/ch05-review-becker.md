## Chapter 05: How grammar feels

### Strengths
The phenomenological opening, "what does ungrammaticality feel like?", is a real entry point for a non-specialist reader. Asking the reader to introspect on metacognitive feelings, with a Proust epigraph as an anchor, is the kind of move trade books exist to make. A generative reader will find this strange terrain, but you've earned the right to lead them through it: the chapter's serious intellectual work is in showing that grammaticality judgments are feelings about cognition, not direct readings off a competence module. That's a contestable claim worth making.

The "Whose gorilla?" section is the chapter's centerpiece and it is genuinely good. Hankamer and Postal are taken seriously, Pullum is named with affection but not deference, the OED data is real source-grounded work, the LLM-and-friends survey is the right kind of citizen-science move, and the resolution (that the literature was wrong, the Wikipedia entry needed updating, the squib's data was incomplete) is exactly the model of disciplined humility you want a generativist reader to see in this book. The claim "is it mine whose was right" is also a charming sentence-level pun that emerges from the data itself. Keep this section.

The dangler discussion (Donaldson, Pullum) and the singular *they* discussion (Mia, Sasha, *This American Life*) are also strong. The singular-*they* honesty, that you yourself can't fully achieve reflective equilibrium with a named referent, is the right kind of vulnerability. A generative reader who privately agrees with you here will respect you for saying so out loud.

### Major concerns
The chapter is currently three or four chapters wearing one chapter's costume. There are two "What does (un)grammaticality feel like?" sections. There's a Proust block quoted twice. There's a section called "Reinterpreting grammatical intuitions through a constructionist lens" duplicated verbatim. The Bayesian section, the predictive-processing section, the Barrett constructed-emotion section, the Berridge valence section, the squirrel-monkey/hyrax exaptation section, and the "Grammar and the Sacred" section all overlap heavily and don't decide which framework is doing the work. The "vector space of emotions" section at the end is openly attributed to ChatGPT and reads like it.

For a generative reader, the cumulative effect is fatal: the chapter says, in different ways, that grammaticality judgments are constructed, predictive, social, sacred, exapted, and Bayesian, and never settles on a single account of how those frameworks relate. The reader is not being persuaded; they are being shown a list of frameworks the author hasn't yet finished negotiating with.

The "Grammar and the Sacred" section, with its Tower of Babel framing and its claim that grammars are sacred markers of social belonging, is striking but tonally at odds with the rest of the chapter. It also makes a strong sociological claim ("grammar is sacred") that, if you mean it, deserves a chapter of its own; if you don't mean it that strongly, the rhetoric overshoots.

The "Joshua Knobe (forthcoming from Ergo)" placeholder needs filling. The single source of "(forthcoming)" is also a source-grounding flag.

### Priority fixes
- Cut to one framework. My recommendation: predictive processing plus Bayesian update, since they actually fit the satiation and gradience data. Keep one section on the social-marker function (without the "sacred" frame) as a complement, not a parallel theory.
- Delete the duplicated section, the duplicated Proust quote, the "What does (un)grammaticality feel like 1?" / "What does ungrammaticality feel like 2?" pair (one section, please).
- Cut the ChatGPT vector-space passage at the end. It's unanchored and visibly machine-generated.
- The Bayesian numerical example with 10^-25 probabilities is overcalibrated for a trade book. The math should illustrate, not pretend to compute.
- "Grammar and the Sacred" — either commit to it as a serious sociological argument with citations to Durkheim/Goffman/etc., or rewrite it as one short subsection.

### One concrete suggestion
The "Whose gorilla?" investigation is the chapter's argument. Let it be the chapter. Open with the squib, walk the reader through your actual investigation (LLMs, family, OED, web examples), and let the reader draw the methodological lesson: linguists' intuitions are themselves data points among others, and reflective equilibrium is what you do when the data points conflict. Then bring in danglers and singular *they* as parallel cases. End with a brief paragraph nodding at predictive processing as a possible mechanism, but don't try to do the cognitive science here. You'll have other chapters for that. The chapter is currently reaching for a unified theory; it should be telling one tightly built story. A generativist reader will be much more impressed by the *whose* investigation than by a six-framework synthesis. The investigation respects the field's evidential standards. The synthesis looks like overreach.
