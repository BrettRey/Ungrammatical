## Chapter 11: Seeing grammar in the brain

### Strengths

The two facts the chapter contains are real and useful. Just (1993) on pupillary dilation as a working-memory measure is the right kind of citation for a trade book: a small, vivid, replicable physiological signal that grammar errors do produce measurable effects. Divjak (2024) on heart-rate-variability drops in response to article errors, with the surprising twist that the drops are larger when L1 (native) speakers commit the error and smaller for L2 speakers, is a genuinely interesting wrinkle that the chapter could exploit.

That asymmetry is the seed of the chapter: we are physiologically more troubled by ungrammaticality from people we expect to be fluent than from people we know to be learners. That ties directly to the chapter on intuitions earlier in the book, and to the social-recognition argument about grammar boundaries.

### Major concerns

The chapter is a stub. Seven lines, two sentences. As it stands it cannot survive into the book.

A trade book treatment of neurolinguistic evidence would, at minimum, want:

- N400 and P600 as event-related-potential signatures (ERPs are already mentioned in the predictive-processing section of Chapter 5; perhaps that material belongs here instead).
- Aphasia evidence: Broca's vs. Wernicke's, and what each tells us about grammatical processing in the brain. The book would benefit from a real patient story (Phineas Gage of language work; or one of Caplan's patients).
- The hippocampus / entorhinal-cortex sequence-learning study mentioned in chapter 5 (the 2024 Nature paper). That would fit better here.
- Pupillometry, HRV, and eye-tracking as a converging-evidence trio.
- Some coverage of the squirrel-monkey and rock-hyrax pattern-detection work (currently in chapter 5).

### Priority fixes

1. Write the chapter. As a stub it can't be reviewed.
2. Move the predictive-processing / N400 / P600 / squirrel-monkey / hippocampus material from chapter 5 here, where it belongs. Chapter 5 is about feelings; this chapter should be about brains.
3. Find one patient case study to anchor the chapter. The book trades on real-people stories (Schleicher, Sweet, Hankamer, Postal, Pullum, Doctorow); a neurolinguistic patient with selective grammatical impairment would carry that voice.
4. Develop the L1/L2 asymmetry. It's a small fact with a large implication for the book's argument about grammar as community signal.

### One concrete suggestion

There is no worst sentence to flag because there is no chapter to read. The single sentence that is here is fine: "Heart rate variability is another measure. \citet{Divjak2024a} found the HRV drops when listeners are exposed to article errors. Drops were larger when L1 accent made the errors and smaller when L2 accent did."

The chapter's best opportunity is exactly this fact. Build the chapter around it. The L1/L2 asymmetry says something the book has been hinting at: grammaticality functions as in-group monitoring, and our nervous systems treat L1 errors with more alarm because they signal something deviant in someone we expect to share our system. Open the chapter with the Divjak study, work out from there.
