## Chapter 04: Morality and grammaticality

### Strengths

The chapter contains, at its core, a real idea: grammaticality judgments often masquerade as descriptive when they're moral, and this conflation matters. The Pirahã and Bentham passages have promise, and the double-modals section, with Steven Coats's billion-word YouTube corpus and his "would might" hunt, is the chapter's actual gold. That story has everything: a real linguist, a real method, a real surprise (54 hits), a real comparison (rarer than "interstitial"). It belongs in the book.

The Parfit framing, his climbing-the-same-mountain metaphor for unifying ethical theories, is a real idea worth exploring as a structural conceit for the chapter. The Parfit-in-residence-at-All-Souls detail is the kind of biographical hook a trade reader will remember.

Otherwise, this chapter has serious problems.

### Major concerns

The chapter is broken in a way the other chapters aren't. There are three substantially overlapping versions of the same material:

1. "Ethical Perspectives on Grammaticality" (lines 78-105)
2. "Ethical Perspectives on Grammaticality" again (lines 90-104, also pragmatism through social justice)
3. "Ethical Perspectives on Grammaticality: Climbing the Linguistic Mountain" (lines 361-460)
4. "Climbing the same mountain from different sides" (lines 463-715)

Each pass is slightly differently worded but covers the same seven ethical frameworks. This isn't editing; it's an LLM having generated three drafts and the author having forgotten to delete two of them. As a chapter, it's roughly 12,000 words of content that should be 4,000.

Even within the kept version, the prose is a long, formulaic exercise in: "Approach X says Y. Consider this example. Some implications include [bulleted list]. However, this approach faces challenges: [bulleted list]." Repeated seven times. This is the most LLM-coded prose in the manuscript so far. No reader will get through it.

The Jahangir Khan section is a particularly bad case. Six paragraphs about a squash player who showcases "resilience, determination, and humility" (precisely the AI-tic register the project rules call out) just to land the point that cooperation isn't always optimal. Khan doesn't earn the space. Cut him; the Gricean cooperative principle can do its own work.

Two stub sections ("Utilitarianism" with body "Utilitarianism is..."; "Laws and morality" with body "Moral philosophers will sometimes point out a distinction between laws and moral") suggest the chapter is more outline than draft.

There is even a fragment: "You're right. I apologize for missing that formatting detail and for not addressing the mirroring aspect of these two perspectives." That's leftover chat from the drafting LLM.

### Priority fixes

1. Delete two of the three duplicate passes through the seven ethical frameworks. Keep one, and rewrite from scratch in the book's voice.
2. Cut Jahangir Khan to a single sentence, or remove him.
3. Remove the "You're right. I apologize..." fragment immediately. It's the kind of artifact that would embarrass the author if it shipped.
4. Decide whether the Parfit conceit is worth the airtime. If yes, the chapter should organize around it from the start. If no, find another organizing idea.
5. Keep the double-modals passage and Coats's corpus work. That's the chapter's surviving asset.
6. Rebuild around a single, clearer thesis: when do native speakers' grammaticality judgments slide into moral judgments, and what happens when they do?

### One concrete suggestion

Worst sentence (a tie of two): the chat-fragment apology line, which is simply not editable, only deletable. Beyond that: "Yet, the principle of cooperation isn't universally applicable. Just as Khan found success in non-cooperation on the squash court, there are contexts in life where competition is crucial: sports, business, arguing legal cases, science." Cut the entire Khan section. Replace with: "Communication is mostly cooperative; deviation can be effective. The Gricean maxims describe the cooperative case."

Best sentence: "I can't ever recall hearing a single instance of a double modal in my daily life, but Steven Coats, of University of Oulu in Finland, got tired of waiting and went looking." It works because it has a real person doing a real thing for a real reason, and because "got tired of waiting" gives Coats a personality, and because the sentence promises a hunt the next paragraph delivers.
