## Proposed narrative arc: Lesser's take

### The arc in one sentence

A book that begins with a single typographical mark, walks the reader through what speakers and linguists actually do when they reach for it, builds a working model of grammaticality as a homeostatic cluster, tests that model against the cases that ought to break it, and ends by showing the model paying off on a small construction nobody had quite seen before.

### The opening

Chapter 01's job is to make the reader curious about a mark on a page. Start with the asterisk: where it came from (Aristophanes, Schleicher), what working linguists do with it now, what readers do with it now (Twitter pile-ons, copy-editor margins, the AAVE habitual *be* arguments). End the chapter with the question the rest of the book is going to answer: when six different people put an asterisk in front of the same sentence, are they doing the same thing? The hook is the sociology of the mark, not the theory behind it. The theory comes next, and the reader will arrive at it wanting it.

### The development

Three movements, each doing distinct work.

**Movement I — The model (Ch. 02–04).** This is where the HPC backbone is stated, plainly, once, in its own chapter. Then two short, lean chapters work the model through familiar linguistic ground: gradience and the conventional patterns that hold a language together over time.

**Movement II — The cluster in action (Ch. 05–10).** The diachronic and cross-linguistic case studies. These are the chapters that already work in the manuscript. They show the model doing, not telling. *Much*-as-NPI, *probable to play*, the do-support story, the passé composé, codeswitching at the syntactic boundary, efficiency, what an impossible language would even look like. Each case is a small archival victory; together they make the cluster visible.

**Movement III — The boundary phenomena (Ch. 11–14).** Where the cluster fades into something else. Feelings, fashion and swearing, the politics of prescription, the moments where speakers and linguists alike get it wrong. This is the part of the book where the HPC frame stops solving the puzzle and starts telling you why the puzzle has the edges it has.

### The turn

The turn comes between Movement II and Movement III. Through Chapter 10 the reader has been collecting wins: the model explains gradience, conventionalisation, change, language contact, efficiency, impossibility. Then the book turns: here are cases where calling something ungrammatical has nothing to do with syntax in any narrow sense, and yet speakers reach for the same word. The book's argument is that this isn't a bug. It's the cluster's edge. That turn is what takes the book from explaining grammaticality to explaining why the concept does the social work it does.

### The resolution

Close on the *long, long way* chapter. Currently buried at position 21, it is the strongest piece of writing in the manuscript and the clearest demonstration of what the HPC view actually buys you. After the boundary phenomena, return the reader to a small construction, follow it across languages and back through Old and Middle English, and let the model finally cash out on something nobody else has framed quite this way. Then a short final chapter, three or four pages, on the asterisk again — what we now know it's doing, what it can't do, and why the diversity is the point.

The reader's takeaway, in one sentence: the word *ungrammatical* names a real cluster of overlapping properties, not a single property, and the cluster's shape is what makes the word both useful and contested.

### Why this arc serves the HPC argument

HPC is an argument about why a kind term keeps working even when no single property holds across all its instances. A book making that argument has to do three things in order: (1) give the reader the model, (2) show the cluster cohering across cases that share most-but-not-all properties, (3) show the cluster fading at the edges without breaking. The current manuscript does (1) buried in Chapter 01 §7, does (2) brilliantly but in scattered fashion, and does (3) at length but without ever naming what the boundary chapters are doing. Reorder along the model-cohere-fade arc and every chapter becomes an instance of one explicit claim instead of a new topic introduced cold.

### Chapters in proposed order

1. **The asterisk.** Where the mark came from, who uses it now, what they disagree about. Ends with the question.
2. **What grammaticality is.** The HPC model, stated plainly. The thesis chapter. (Pull from current 01 §7 plus introduction material.)
3. **Gradient grammaticality.** Tightened from current 02. Shows the model handles the obvious case.
4. **Real patterns, becoming ungrammatical.** Merge current 10 + 12. Conventionalisation and change as the cluster's stability over time.
5. **Generative grammar and its limits.** Current 07, expanded. Why the dominant theoretical frame can't carry the full weight of what speakers mean.
6. **Without grammar.** Current 09, tightened. Languages, codes, communicative systems where "grammatical" doesn't quite apply.
7. **Across language boundaries.** Current 13, absorbing the codeswitching material from 16. One chapter, not two.
8. **Efficiency.** Current 19. Lean and good as it stands.
9. **Impossible languages.** Current 17, paired thematically with 08. What the cluster's outer limit looks like.
10. **A long, long way (Part I: the puzzle).** First half of current 21. Set up the reduplication question. Hold the resolution.
11. **How grammar feels.** Current 05 cut to a third. Drop the ChatGPT vector-space material. Keep the intuitions story.
12. **Fashion and swearing.** Merge current 14 + 18 + relevant pieces of 03 and 06. Style, in-group marking, expressive deviation. One of the most distinctive things the book has to say.
13. **Politics and prescription.** Current 04 and 06 cut hard, merged. AAVE habitual *be*, *whom*, the left-and-right-both-prescribe point. Two pages, not eighty.
14. **Getting it wrong.** *Whose* material from 15 and 20, merged. How even careful linguists misjudge grammaticality.
15. **A long, long way (Part II: the payoff).** Second half of current 21. The cluster cashes out on a construction nobody had quite seen this way.
16. **The asterisk, after.** Three or four pages. A real conclusion. The book currently has none.

Cut entirely: Chapter 08 (coordination, 55 lines of scaffolding), Chapter 11 (neurolinguistics stub, 7 lines — fold the two facts into the new feelings chapter), the duplicated *whose* material in current 20, the scratchpad files in `00 a notes.tex` and `00 b organization.tex`. Merge aggressively rather than preserve. The book earns its length in Movements II and III; it loses length in the bloated theoretical chapters and the stubs. Sixteen chapters, roughly two thirds the current word count, is a real book.
