## Chapter 04: Morality and grammaticality

### Strengths

The double-modal section does the work the rest of the chapter wishes it could do. Steven Coats sifting a 1.29-billion-word YouTube corpus for *would might*, hand-checking the false positives (transcription errors, self-corrections, embedded clauses), and ending with 54 true examples - one per 24 million words - is a perfect Zimmerian set piece: a researcher with a question, a method, a yield, a number you can hold. The comparison ("about as rare as *exigence*") makes the rarity sensible. The follow-up grammar of why double modals are blocked in Standard English (modals are defective, lack a plain form, *will* expects a non-tensed complement) is patient and lands.

The Bentham vignette ("Prolixity is the soul of tedium") is a real sentence by a real person, doing real work for the chapter's argument that pragmatics has been a moral framework all along.

### Major concerns

This is by some distance the most LLM-padded chapter in the book so far. The "Ethical Perspectives on Grammaticality" section appears *twice*, almost word-for-word. The first version runs from Pragmatism through "Conclusion: A Unified Theory" with Parfit as the binding metaphor. The second version, three paragraphs later, opens "Climbing the Linguistic Mountain" and walks the same seven frameworks again, in slightly different order, before ending with another "Synthesis" header that doesn't actually synthesize. This is duplicate output; it must be reduced to one pass.

Within either pass, the writing is the genre that most needs cutting from the book: "These judgments intersect with social values, power structures, and cultural identities in complex ways." Six of the seven framework subsections begin with this kind of opening. Each ends with a "while X, also Y" softening. Together they read as if a model was asked to enumerate ethical theories and tag each with a paragraph on grammar. The text does not have particular humans, particular sentences in particular mouths, particular consequences.

The Jahangir Khan section is doing nothing. We are told he won 555 matches, that his brother died, that he was approachable and humble. Then the chapter pivots to "many spheres of human interaction are built on the bedrock of cooperation" and Khan vanishes. He is wallpaper, not an example. If the point is "non-cooperation is sometimes strategic," a one-paragraph reference suffices, or a different example more obviously linked to language (a cross-examiner, an auctioneer, a poker player).

The Parfit "climbing the same mountain" metaphor is invoked roughly fifteen times. It is also Parfit's image, not the chapter's. Each invocation gestures rather than argues. After the third, it becomes incantation.

The "Eve eats of the fruit" line at the very top of the chapter is intriguing but isolated. It either deserves a paragraph or should go.

### Priority fixes

1. Delete one of the two duplicate ethical-frameworks runs entirely. Then ruthlessly cut the remaining one to a single tight section per framework, each anchored to one specific sentence-judgment in one specific situation.
2. Replace Khan with a language-adjacent non-cooperator, or compress him to two sentences.
3. Cut every invocation of "climbing the mountain" after the first.
4. The chapter still has bracketed placeholders ("Alice Evans and link"; "true?") - resolve these before review.
5. The double-modal section is in the wrong place. It is the only material in the chapter doing real work and it deserves to be the spine.

### One concrete suggestion

The chapter's central question is whether ungrammaticality can have moral weight. The most interesting empirical answer is in the sociolinguistic literature on dialect-based hiring discrimination - the resume studies, the housing-call studies (Baugh on linguistic profiling). Pick one, name the researcher, describe the audio recordings (the same person calling about an apartment in three different voices), report the actual call-back rates. That replaces three subsections of philosophical commentary with a moment in the world where calling someone's grammar "ungrammatical" had a measured cost in dollars.
