## Chapter 08: Help! Help! I'm being repressed

### Strengths

Victoria Horner's 2005 puzzle-box experiments are exactly the kind of empirical anchor a trade book needs. The contrast between chimps and human children - chimps disregard irrelevant actions when the box is transparent, human children keep imitating the unnecessary tap-and-poke routine - is a genuine, vivid, almost shocking finding. "Over-imitation" is a great term and worth the chapter's investment.

The Habermas line - "Not every irregularity indicates a rule violation. One has to know the rule if one wishes to determine whether someone is deviating from it" - is well-deployed. So is the Heath quote on Hobbes, and the move from *what we conform to* (biological) to *that we conform* (social).

The chapter has a real intellectual question: is conforming to a grammar more like conforming to a costume code at school, or more like a behavior that gets locked in by a coordination payoff?

### Major concerns

This is closer to a notebook than a chapter. It opens with "Notes" as a section heading. Block quotes are stacked without commentary. There is a sentence that simply reads "Ungrammaticality is good because it increases robustness?" - a research question, not chapter prose. The middle section lapses into an LLM-generated aside with subheaders like "Learning as Performance," "Shared Language as Ritual," "From Imitation to Innovation," each three sentences of confident handwaving with no sources. That whole stretch needs to come out or be replaced.

The Hobbes-via-Heath block quote runs unanchored to the chapter's question. If it's about why we follow rules, we need the connection drawn, not just the quote.

The Horner-and-Whiten passage is the most concrete material, but it lacks scene. The boxes are there. The children are there. The chimps are there. Where is the lab? What did the protocol look like - did kids and chimps know each other was being tested? What did the kids actually do with the redundant tool-tap? A short paragraph of the experimenter's perspective would change the reader's relationship to the finding.

The chapter argues that human children over-imitate but other animals don't, then argues that this might explain language. It does not say what that explanation looks like. Compare a chimpanzee not echoing an unnecessary action to a child overgeneralizing the past tense to *finded*. These are not the same kind of imitation. A trade reader will not connect them on their own.

The Lewis 1983 quotations on convention are good but isolated. David Lewis's *Convention* is one of the most relevant works in the chapter's range, and he gets two sentences and a citation.

The chapter has no narrative spine. The Monty Python title "Help! Help! I'm being repressed" implies a conceit (Freud, repression, social order) that the body of the chapter doesn't follow.

### Priority fixes

1. Cut the LLM-generated subsections ("Learning as Performance" through "A Bridge to Hurford"). They're space-fillers.
2. Decide what the chapter is doing. Three plausible spines: (a) over-imitation as the route from biology to grammar; (b) coordination conventions in Lewis's sense as the structure of grammaticality; (c) the social-conformist experience of being corrected. Right now all three are gestured at and none is developed.
3. Anchor or cut the Hobbes/Heath material.
4. The Monty Python title is a great line. Make it earn its place. The peasant in the field shouting "help, help" is being grammatically corrected in a way; figure out what the chapter is repressing.

### One concrete suggestion

The Horner experiment deserves a real scene. There are videos from the Whiten lab, papers describing the apparatus (a wooden box, sliding panels, a stick of food inside, deliberate misdirection in the demo), and almost certainly transcripts of toddlers reasoning aloud about why they're tapping the unnecessary panel ("because she did"). Write three sentences from inside one trial - a four-year-old in St Andrews, the demonstrator, the box. Then say what the over-imitation buys: a child who copies the inefficient ritual is a child willing to accept a community's rules without inferring their function from outcome. That's exactly what learning a grammar requires. Make the link.
