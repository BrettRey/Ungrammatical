## Chapter 18: Swearing

This chapter is essentially a stub. There are 16 lines, two of which are unattributed Atwood-sounding epigraphs, one is the chapter's organizing question, and the rest is a sketch of what the chapter would argue. There is no chapter to review, but there is a chapter to design. Given the book's architecture and the book-jacket promise, here's what should be here.

### Strengths (of what's here)
The framing question at line 10 is genuinely good: "Is swearing a norm violation because it is obscene… or because it is ungrammatical?" That's the chapter's hook and it's the right question. It opens space for a sociologically-minded argument that connects swearing to grammaticality without collapsing the distinction. The opening hint that "swearing… exists as a secret language hidden within our own" gestures at a real linguistic-anthropological claim.

The unattributed Atwood-sounding epigraphs ("We were the people who were not in the papers"; "It's impossible to say a thing exactly the way it was") are well-chosen if Atwood is intended; both are from *The Handmaid's Tale*. Source them.

### Major concerns
This chapter is critical for the book's HPC argument. Swearing is the strongest test case for the claim that "ungrammatical" picks out a cluster of overlapping properties without a single criterion. A swear word in the wrong register is "wrong" in the same way a wrong syntactic structure is "wrong" — but is the wrongness the same kind, or different? That's what the chapter has to answer.

The chapter's current sketched answer ("swearing has its own twisted grammar… those within the circle have mastered this rebellious grammar") is one possible answer, and it's the sociologically plausible one. But it isn't argued. It's announced. A generative reader will recognize the assertion and want the data.

What the chapter is missing entirely:
- The genuine grammar of swearing. There's a real syntactic and morphological literature here (Postal on "expletive infixation": *abso-fucking-lutely*; Hoeksema and Napoli on intensifier uses; Quang Phuc Dong's classic 1971 paper on syntax of *fuck*; Steven Pinker's *Stuff of Thought* chapter; Bergen's *What the F*; Mohr's *Holy Sh\*t*). Swearing has rules: *the fuck out of him* but not *out the fuck of him*; *he ate the fucking sandwich* but not *he the fucking ate sandwich*. These are syntactic constraints, not just register constraints.
- The neurolinguistic evidence: aphasic patients can sometimes produce intact swear words even when other speech is lost; Tourette's syndrome and coprolalia. This evidence bears directly on whether swearing engages the same systems as ordinary language.
- The cross-cultural evidence: swearing taps body parts, religion, family — but with strikingly different patterns across cultures. Quebecois French swears with religious terms; English mostly swears with sexual/excretory ones; Mandarin with family-insults. That comparative work is real.
- The reclamation literature: words like *queer* and *bitch* have been reclaimed by in-groups; the conditions under which reclamation works are sociolinguistically interesting.

### Priority fixes (chapter design)
- Engage one of the syntactic accounts of expletive infixation. *Abso-fucking-lutely* is a real, attested construction with structural constraints; making it the chapter's centerpiece would honor the field's data while making the book's broader argument.
- Engage Pinker's six-functions-of-swearing taxonomy (descriptive, idiomatic, abusive, emphatic, cathartic, expressive). It's a useful organizing frame for a popular book.
- Address the neurolinguistic evidence. Even briefly. Swearing-survival in aphasia is a real fact and it bears on the book's claim about whether grammar is one system or many.
- Engage the cross-cultural evidence. Tying English/Quebecois/Mandarin patterns to the chapter's HPC-framing argument would do real work.
- Remove the LLM-padded passages at lines 12-16 ("hurled curses with casual precision," "drawing lines between those in the know, and those firmly excluded"). The phrasing is generic.

### One concrete suggestion
Open with a single specific case: the *abso-fucking-lutely* infixation paradigm. Walk a non-specialist reader through what goes where (between the stressed and unstressed syllable, never word-initially, never word-finally), how to test it, and why it's a syntactic phenomenon rather than just a vocabulary phenomenon. Then ask: what about *abso-bloody-lutely* in British English? *Abso-friggin-lutely*? *Abso-darn-lutely*? The infix slot is grammatical, but only certain words can fill it. Which words? Why those?

That single case study, fully developed, would do more for the book's central argument than five pages of generalization. It shows that swearing isn't just lexical — it has grammar. And it shows that the grammar is real (ruled-governed, predictable) but also community-specific (the swear-words available in the slot vary by community). That is exactly the HPC-style argument the book has been building. The chapter would then have something to land on.

A generative reader who reads a tightly-constructed chapter on expletive infixation will close the book respectful of the analysis. A generative reader who reads the current sketch will close the book unconvinced that the author engaged the swearing literature. Chapter 18 is high-leverage for the book and currently underdeveloped; the suggested case study is a way to use small space well.
