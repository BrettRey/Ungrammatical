## Chapter 17: Impossible languages

### Strengths
The choice to engage Moro's *Impossible Languages* (2016) directly is right. This is a chapter where the book has to take a clear position on the strongest argument for an innate, content-bearing UG: that the brain rejects certain logically possible languages, and the rejection is evidence for what the language faculty is. If the book skips the impossible-languages literature, the generative reader concludes the book hasn't engaged the strongest version of the position. So this chapter matters.

The Sampson 1975 long quote is a real find. Sampson making the point that, with transformations, *any* language can be specified — including the prime-number-length language that no human speaker would ever produce — is the chapter's most important piece of evidence. It's also exactly the right kind of move for a sociologically-minded book: the inability to formally distinguish possible from impossible languages within a transformational framework is a real internal critique of the formal program. Sampson is a generativist-adjacent figure here, which dignifies the critique.

The Kallini 2024 figure (impossibility-continuum) is a smart choice. It places the chapter in a current, ongoing debate (LLMs and impossible languages are now a hot research topic, c.f. Mitchell, Bowman, etc.) and gives the reader a visual aid.

The four-example perturbation paradigm (lines 55-61) — even/odd shuffle, partial reverse, tense hop — is a teaching device that lets a non-specialist see what an impossible language might look like. That's exactly the kind of pedagogy the book needs.

### Major concerns
The chapter has two distinct openings (an outline starting at line 1, and a chapter starting at line 35), which suggests that what's here is half-organized notes rather than a draft chapter. The reader will see both openings unless one is removed before the build runs.

More substantively, the chapter sketches the position but doesn't engage it. Moro's central claim is that there's empirical neuroimaging evidence that the brain treats some kinds of languages differently from others (the Smith and Tsimpli work on the polyglot Christopher, the Musso et al. fMRI experiments). If you don't engage this evidence, you're arguing with the framework but not the data. A generative reader who knows the Musso et al. work will close the chapter feeling that you've punted.

The Kallini 2024 paper on LLM-trained-on-impossible-languages is a recent and important piece of evidence on the question. Their finding is that LLMs can learn some "impossible" languages but with detectable inefficiencies. That bears on Moro's claim. Engage it.

The closing return to Pereira's *colorless green ideas* finding is good, but it's been used in chapter 15 already (line 185 there). Either move it or note the cross-reference.

The chapter also misses an opportunity. The most interesting impossible-language is the one that's been almost-attested: word-counting languages, prime-number-length languages, mirror-image languages. Walking the reader through one of these in detail, with care for what evidence would settle the question, would do real work. Right now the chapter mentions them but doesn't dwell on any one of them.

There's a deeper Becker concern: the chapter is the place where the book has to decide what to say about UG. The chapter has the materials for two opposite positions: that there are impossible languages and the rejection is informative (Moro), or that there aren't really, and the apparent constraints are statistical/processing facts (Sampson, the LLM evidence). The book seems to want to say something like: yes, there are some genuine constraints, but they're a small set (the Left Branch Condition was floated in chapter 1). That's a defensible position. But the chapter doesn't make it. As written, the chapter notes the question and stops.

The "Linguists \& philosophers" bullet list at lines 13-27 is a mix of genuine entries and placeholders. "Functionalism: language as a tool for communication" / "Formalism: language as a mathematical–semiotic system" / "Post-structuralism" — these don't belong in a paragraph about impossible languages and feel like leftover note-taking.

### Priority fixes
- Remove the outline at the top of the file. Start the chapter at "Linguists have ventured…"
- Engage the Musso et al. fMRI experiments and Smith and Tsimpli on Christopher. These are the strongest empirical claims for the impossible-languages thesis and they need a hearing.
- Develop the Kallini 2024 finding. LLM-on-impossible-languages is the current frontier and the book should land its position there.
- Decide what the book's stance on UG is, and have this chapter make it. The hint in chapter 1 about Left Branch Condition as architectural suggests you want a moderate position; argue for it here.
- Cut the philosophers/structuralism bullet list.
- Source-ground the Sampson 1975 page numbers and the Moro 2016 page numbers. Both are cited; verify.

### One concrete suggestion
The chapter is the book's place to say what the book actually believes about innate constraints on possible human languages. That's a difficult question and a serious one. It deserves a clear answer. Take a stance: most "impossible language" demonstrations are demonstrations of what's hard for humans to learn, not what's logically excluded. The Musso et al. result, on careful reading, shows differential brain activation, not categorical rejection. The Kallini result shows LLMs can learn impossible languages with reduced efficiency, not with categorical failure. The picture that emerges is gradient: some constraints (like recursion, Moro's favorite) appear to be hard-wired, but the empirical evidence for absolute impossibility, as opposed to learning difficulty, is much thinner than Moro suggests. That's an argument the book can make. It's a defensible argument that takes the strongest version of UG seriously while reaching a conclusion compatible with the rest of the book's HPC framing.

A generative reader who reads a chapter that engages Moro carefully, takes Musso seriously, and lands on a moderate position will respect the chapter. A reader who reads a chapter that gestures at the question and moves on will not. Chapter 17 is one of the book's most important architectural chapters and currently it's also one of the least developed. Fixing this would do disproportionate work for the book's overall reception.
