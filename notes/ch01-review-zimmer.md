## Chapter 01: Stories from syntactic islands

### Strengths

The opening image - islands hidden in plain sight, "tough to leave them" - gives the reader something concrete to picture. The walk-through of *what* moving from after *see* to the front of the sentence is patient and physical: the underline, the trailing space, the example pairs stacked vertically so the reader can see the displacement. That works.

The Tristan chord section is the chapter's best science writing: 1865, Wagner, Berlioz with the actual quoted dissonance complaint, then Herbie Hancock's anecdote with Miles Davis playing the wrong chord into rightness. Hands clapped over ears, one minute frozen at the piano, hot night. That whole passage is the model the rest of the chapter should be measured against.

The Goold Brown insults ("a miserable jumble," "fudge!") are great, and the discovery that Brown thought Ann Fisher was a man because of *He reduces the moods to three* is the kind of small archival jewel that does a lot of work.

### Major concerns

The chapter takes a sharp turn from islands to a 17th-18th century history of grammar writing, then back. The transition is structurally muddled: a reader expecting the development of Ross's discovery instead lands in Jeremy Taylor's biography in 1613, then the women grammarians, then Lowth, then back to Taylor again, then back to islands. The historical material is interesting on its own but it isn't anchored to the central island question. Right now it reads as if two chapters were stitched together.

The Taylor section ends in five paragraphs of clearly LLM-generated filler ("This insight is at the heart of the modern linguistic understanding of grammaticality. The rules of grammar, whether prescriptive or descriptive..."). These could be cut entirely without loss.

The Ross discovery itself, the most exciting moment in the chapter (a 28-year-old grad student noticing something that had been hiding in plain sight), gets one sentence. We never see Ross. We never see the moment.

The mathematics/physics/music theory list of "questions that call for explanation" is filler. None of these are explored. They function as a hand-wave.

The cow-with-tear-in-his-eye example is genuinely funny but the trees that follow do too much grammatical heavy lifting for a trade book. A reader who needs the trees to understand structural ambiguity has been lost; one who doesn't need them won't read them.

### Priority fixes

1. Decide what this chapter is doing. If it's about islands, the historical detour wants its own chapter or to be cut. If it's about the discovery of ungrammaticality as a concept, restructure around Taylor → Sweet → Ross as a continuous arc.
2. Cut the "this insight is at the heart" coda after Taylor.
3. The "Model of Grammaticality" section with its 13 numbered points is technical inventory belonging in an appendix or much later in the book. It interrupts the chapter's narrative.
4. Restore Ross to the chapter. He gets less screen time than Goold Brown.

### One concrete suggestion

Open the chapter on Ross at MIT in 1966. Where is he? What's on his desk? What sentence finally makes him stumble? The "she doubted the idea that they had seen what" formulation must have come from somewhere - a paper he was reading, a sentence overheard, a conversation with Chomsky. Even a fictionalized but plausible reconstruction (clearly marked as such) of the moment a young grad student first feels the wall the syntax hits would do more than the abstract claim that he "discovered" islands. Right now islands are described as objects; they should be described as discoveries, with the texture of a person bumping into one.
