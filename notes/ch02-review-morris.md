## Chapter 02: How grammatical can you get? (Gradient grammaticality)

### Strengths

The Jamie Ramsden story works. Two high school kids racing the clock to bang out a Basic assignment, drawing the flowchart after the fact, getting caught because their code was identical, then negotiating with Mr. Boyko for a 50% split and proposing he extend the same logic to the classmates they'd helped. That's a scene with character, comedy, and an idea inside it (computer code is too rigid for two teenagers to coincidentally produce twin solutions). The boys are alive on the page in a way the linguists in chapter 1 are not.

The dessert scale is also a real piece of writing. The trampoline-rules-overheard-from-the-yard moment, then the careful escalation: *to have dessert, having dessert, that you have dessert, you have dessert, have dessert.* The reader feels the floor tilt with each step. The escalation is the chapter's single best move because it puts gradience in the reader's mouth instead of describing it.

### Major concerns

The chapter has three holes large enough to fall through. There's an empty enumerate environment at lines 102-103 ("It may"), an unfilled `\section{}` at line 134, and a clear surgery scar where the AI-padding has been spliced in. The Brancusi paragraphs about "broadening the legal understanding of art, recognizing the legitimacy of abstract and avant-garde forms" sound exactly like the Wikipedia wallpaper from chapter 0. So does the close of the pit-bull section: "These cases underscore the classical view's limitations and strengths in categorization." That sentence is filler.

Bigger problem: the chapter buries its best subject. Oscar Pistorius is here as a category-dispute case study and gets a page. By 2026 most readers will think of Pistorius as a man convicted of murdering Reeva Steenkamp. That cannot be ignored. To use him as a cute legal-categorization example without acknowledging what happened later is to perform the very abstraction this book is supposed to push against. Either drop him, or turn that conflict into part of the lesson: a category dispute that played out across a body, then across a courtroom, then across a different courtroom, and the same man at the centre of all three.

On the protagonists/antagonists question: Sampson appears in passing as a foil ("Sampson would say, just go and look at a corpus") but never gets to be a person. He has views the chapter dismisses; we don't know why he holds them. Same for the unnamed "linguists who view\dots" and "linguists who liken\dots" of chapter 1. The book keeps inventing two-camps abstractions rather than putting two specific named human beings in a room.

The Eleanor Rosch section is brief and competent but flat. Rosch's career is full of human texture. She invented prototype theory and then objected to how cognitive scientists used it. There is a rebellion in her own intellectual life. The chapter doesn't show it.

### Priority fixes

1. Fill the holes. Empty enumerate, empty section header, dangling Pistorius example.
2. Decide what to do about Pistorius. Suggested: lead with the Brancusi case (no later baggage) and use Pistorius only if you confront the full arc.
3. Cut the wallpaper paragraph at the end of the legal-categorization section.
4. Rewrite Sampson into a named person with a position one can argue with, not a strawman.

### One concrete suggestion

Open the chapter by walking down the hall during the trampoline scene. Reynolds the linguist hears the kids next door negotiating: "you're not allowed double-bouncing somebody." He notices the sentence is wrong for him but right for them. He tries to make it worse. *You're allowed having dessert.* *You're allowed that you have dessert.* *You're allowed have dessert.* Each step worse. Where does the badness come from? Why does it grow? That's the chapter, and it's a chapter that begins inside a house, with a parent eavesdropping on rule-making. Save the toy generative grammar for the second movement.
