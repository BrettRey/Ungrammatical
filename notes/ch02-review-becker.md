## Chapter 02: How grammatical can you get?

### Strengths
The toy generative grammar at the start is a generous gesture toward the tribe you'll later partly disagree with. Showing a generativist reader that you can do their work, on their terms, with their notation, is exactly the right opening move before you complicate it. The dog-fetches-sticks gradient and the *allowed (to) have dessert* paradigm are also genuinely good teaching examples; they let the reader feel the gradience for themselves rather than being told about it. That's the difference between persuading a reader and lecturing one, and you mostly persuade here.

The "computer code is unambiguously grammatical" detour does real work too. It lets readers see what binary grammaticality would look like if it were true, and how unlike natural language that is. A generativist reader who believes natural language *is* like that gets a chance to articulate the disagreement clearly, which is a kindness. The "Poof, you're a snack" pun is exactly the kind of charm a trade book needs.

### Major concerns
The chapter ends in mid-thought (section 2.5 stops at one sentence about Yorkshire "I were"). Whatever judgement is supposed to land hasn't landed yet, so I'm reviewing a draft that hasn't decided what it wants to argue. That said, what's here positions Sampson and Chomsky as the two extremes and gestures at a middle. That's a fair frame, but currently the middle is rhetorical rather than argued. A generativist will read this and feel that the binary view is being set up as a strawman to be replaced by something gradient and reasonable, without their best version of the binary view ever appearing. Specifically: the modern generative claim isn't that grammaticality is binary in surface judgment but that the underlying grammar deterministically generates a set, and gradient acceptability arises from extragrammatical factors (processing, frequency, semantic plausibility). You haven't engaged that move. If you don't engage it, you're not arguing with the position your reader actually holds.

The Aristotle/Rosch potted history of categories runs long and reads padded ("This approach, which has roots in the work of ancient Greek philosophers..."). The pit bull, burrito, and Brancusi cases are entertaining but unrelated to grammaticality; they're working too hard to demonstrate something the reader will already grant. By the time we return to grammar, the chapter has lost momentum. I would also flag that *worth* as the "ostrich" of adjectives is a nice line but doesn't yet do the conceptual work it's gesturing at; the reader needs to know why peripheral category members aren't an embarrassment for the categorial picture.

### Priority fixes
- Finish section 2.5; it can't end on a Yorkshire sentence.
- Steelman the modern generative position: grammar is binary, gradience is extragrammatical. Then engage it. Right now the chapter implies that gradience defeats the binary view, but a generativist will say that's the wrong target.
- Cut Aristotle/Rosch by half, and tighten the legal-categorization detour. One example would carry the point; you have four.
- The Sampson "everything is grammatical" framing risks being uncharitable. Sampson's actual position is more disciplined than "if you found it, it's grammatical." Quote him at his strongest before disagreeing.
- The dog-fetches-sticks scale (a)-(g) compounds errors; you note the problem and then build (a)-(j) with single errors. Good. But the move from compound-error gradient to single-error gradient deserves a clearer pivot. As drafted, the reader has to do the work of seeing why the second list matters.

### One concrete suggestion
Build the chapter around a single, generative-respectful question: "Given that gradient acceptability is uncontroversial, why do binary models persist, and what do they buy us?" Then answer it: they buy precision, predictiveness, a clean object of study. *Then* describe what they cost: misalignment with speakers' actual judgements, a strained relationship with diachronic change, and a failure to predict satiation effects (which you'll get to). That structure leaves the generativist a position to occupy and a reason to keep reading. The current structure says "the binary view is wrong" before showing you've understood why anyone holds it. Becker's rule: never beat an opponent until you've made them feel understood.
