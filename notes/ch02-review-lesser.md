## Chapter 02: How grammatical can you get?

### Strengths

The chapter has a real argument: grammaticality isn't binary, and the chapter wants you to feel that fact in your gut. The dog-fetched-sticks gradient (with stars stacking from one to four) is exactly right; the reader can feel each step degrade and the cumulative argument lands without much help.

The "you're allowed to have / having / that you have / you have / have dessert" sequence is strong. It puts a single verb under stress and watches it groan. The structure is doing the teaching. Good.

The Mr. Boyko / Jamie Ramsden bubble-sort anecdote is a writer's anecdote, not a lecturer's. The cheeky split of credit lands. The Children of Time spider-DNA disagreement is also useful, though it overstays.

The progressive-aspect history is the chapter's most genuinely interesting passage. The Shakespeare contrast ("O, I die, Horatio") and the slow accretion through Old to Middle English are exactly the kind of small surprise that earns a trade reader's loyalty. Hamlet doesn't say "I'm dying" because the construction wasn't yet there. That's the kind of fact that stays with someone.

### Major concerns

The chapter is structurally broken in three places. There's a section heading that's literally empty (`\section{}`). There's a numbered list that breaks off in midair: "It may " then nothing, then a paragraph about kids on trampolines. And the chapter starts with "Another view" before any first view has been mentioned (the antecedent is presumably elsewhere, but a reader picking up here is lost).

The categories detour, while interesting, is the kind of detour the chapter can't afford. We get Pistorius's prosthetics, Ontario pit bull law, Panera v. Qdoba, and Brancusi's Bird in Space, all in service of the point that classical categories sometimes fail. Two of the four would do. The legal-philosophy framing ("The law often prioritizes certainty and predictability over fidelity to the natural world. Shifting completely to prototype-like models...") is exactly the AI-generated explainer voice that needs to come out.

The "Categories" section repeats itself. The opening paragraph says: "This classical approach assumes that categories have clear-cut boundaries and can be neatly defined by a set of necessary and sufficient conditions. This approach, which has roots in the work of ancient Greek philosophers like Aristotle, assumes that categories have clear-cut boundaries and can be neatly defined by a set of necessary and sufficient conditions." Verbatim duplication.

A typo: "it's work your time" should be "it's worth your time."

### Priority fixes

1. Fix the literal structural breaks: empty `\section{}`, abandoned "It may" sentence, missing first view.
2. Cut the Categories case-study list to two examples. Pit bulls and Brancusi can stay; Pistorius and the burrito belong on the cutting room floor.
3. Repair the verbatim Aristotle duplication.
4. The "key example" of Yorkshire "I were" at the end is filler. The chapter has already done this work; the section break before "Everything is grammatical" should land harder than this.
5. Trim the bubble-sort anecdote. The credit-splitting is good; the explanation of what bubble sort is is not.

### One concrete suggestion

Worst sentence: "These cases underscore the classical view's limitations and strengths in categorization. While the classical approach provides a necessary foundation for legal and practical determinations, it also encounters challenges when faced with evolving societal and cultural norms." Two sentences of pure fluff. After Brancusi, just stop. The reader has the point.

Best sentence: "It's the aspect of the pragmatist who is aware of impermanence, the aspect of autumn." This earns its keep. It's surprising; it gives the reader a feeling for the progressive that no rule-list can give; and it's also pleasantly out of place in a linguistics book, which is exactly what makes it work. Reynolds should be encouraged to write more sentences like this. They're his real voice.
