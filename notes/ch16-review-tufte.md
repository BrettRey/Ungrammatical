## Chapter 16: Unravelling babel (codeswitching)

### Strengths
The Inya / "Daddy register" anecdote (Wiese 2023, lines 36-40) is the chapter's central piece of evidence: a three-year-old refusing to speak English to her British in-laws because for her English was a register restricted to interactions with one specific person. This is the kind of single, vivid case that does the chapter's work for it.

The Tomo translation moment in the Toronto kitchen (lines 24-30) is similarly well-chosen: a toddler differentially translating between his Japanese-speaking parents and English-speaking grandmother. Concrete, dated, personal.

The Grieve 2023 quote (lines 46-48) lays out a useful claim: situational diversity trading off against grammatical complexity. This deserves to be developed.

### Major concerns
The chapter is currently a bullet-list outline (lines 3-15) followed by a partial draft. The first 13 bullets are unintegrated notes; some are also reproduced verbatim later (the Wiese quote appears as bullet 13 and again as block quote at line 39).

The chapter trails off at line 48 with a Grieve quote that is never analysed. The Wiese quote is repeated verbatim. There is no figure, no diagram, no table.

The chapter argues a substantive new framing - "a language is simply a communicative situation" - and never develops it concretely. The reader is told that com-sits are smaller than English/Japanese/Urdu and that bilingual conversations mix languages clause-internally, but is never shown a single bilingual sentence with the language boundaries marked.

The "swearing feels right in some context and wrong in others" line (bullet 14) is the chapter's potential bridge to Chapter 18 (Swearing) but not developed.

The Smith 2019 "showing and guessing" exposition (line 7) appears twice, near-identically (line 32). One should be cut.

### Priority fixes
1. Convert the bullet-list outline at the top into prose, or cut it.
2. Resolve the Wiese-quote duplication: it is once a bullet, once a block quote. Pick one.
3. Resolve the Smith 2019 "showing and guessing" duplication: lines 7 and 32 are near-identical.
4. Add at least one concrete codeswitched example to the chapter. The chapter is *about* codeswitching and contains zero codeswitched data.
5. Develop or cut the Grieve 2023 thesis. As it stands the quote sits unanalysed.

### One concrete suggestion
Build a single nested-bands figure called "What language am I speaking?"

A horizontal axis: from "narrowest com-sit" (e.g., Inya speaking only to Daddy) to "broadest com-sit" (e.g., a written-English book like this one).

Above the axis, layered horizontal bands:
- Bottom band: language-internal microregisters (sports talk, baby talk, hip-hop slang).
- Middle band: dialect-level com-sits (AAE, Singlish, Scots, Indian English).
- Upper band: language-level com-sits (English vs Japanese vs Urdu).
- Top band: codeswitched com-sits (Spanglish, Hinglish, Japanese-English).

Mark a few specific examples on the axis as labelled tick marks:
- Inya / Daddy register
- A toddler interpreting kitchen-Japanese to grandma
- A bilingual professional switching languages within a clause for emphasis
- A swearing-allowed pub conversation
- A formal book

The point of the figure: there is no clear line between "two different languages" and "two different com-sits." The chapter's central claim is that com-sits scale continuously, and the figure should show that.

A second small inset alongside: one codeswitched bilingual sentence, with the language boundaries colour-coded clause-by-clause and morpheme-by-morpheme. The Spanish-English literature has standard examples. Pick one and annotate it. The chapter currently asserts that "bilingual conversation can mix languages within clauses and it will be fully grammatical" without ever showing this happening.

This chapter is short and underdeveloped. The figure plus a single codeswitched example would do roughly half the analytical work the chapter wants to do.
