## Chapter 15: What's ungrammatical

### Strengths
The Mom/Son dialogue at lines 18-39 is the cleanest illustration in the book of how context promotes a string from ungrammatical to grammatical. *I go there yesterday* is starred at line 22 and unstarred at line 37, and the only thing that changed is the surrounding discourse ("Oh, you won't believe this. So..."). This is the right way to show that grammaticality is partly a function of motivation. A reader can hold this in their head for the rest of the book.

The historical reconstruction of the French passé composé is the chapter's single best piece of linguistic exposition. The story moves through phonological erosion (Latin penultimate stress, French final-syllable loss), the resulting conjugational ambiguity, the irregular auxiliaries that resisted erosion, and the social/registral dimension (Parisian *passé composé* vs. provincial *passé simple*). The Matthew-effect footnote at line 83 is exactly the right kind of erudition: a Bible verse repurposed as a sociolinguistic principle.

The horse-raced-past-the-barn-fell explanation works. The reader sees how a single sentence's apparent ungrammaticality is an illusion produced by ambiguity between past tense and past participle. The chapter then uses this to make the broader point about why we default to grammar (rather than lexical) repairs.

The Ex-Lax / Pat Nixon / Spiro Agnew section is delightful. McCawley's ungrammatical-on-its-own *Spiro conjectures Ex-Lax* becomes grammatical as the elliptical answer to a question. This is the kind of pedagogical move that makes a chapter sing.

The whose-gorilla section is rich and shows real research: the topicality argument (both possessor and possessed must be topical), the type/token contrast, the cross-linguistic survey. This is genuinely original work in a trade-book setting.

### Major concerns
This chapter is too long, too repetitive, and too internally divided. The whose-gorilla material was already covered in Chapter 5 ("How grammar feels"). The reader has been told this story once, and now they're being told it again with more detail. The right move is to consolidate: tell the story once, in this chapter, and replace the chapter-5 version with a brief pointer.

The chapter has a major structural redundancy at lines 366-389: the "curious case" subsection introduces the missing whose, then a `\bigskip` break, and then "To recap, in 1973, Hankamer and Postal boldly claimed..." reintroduces the same material as if from the top. Two paragraphs in a row introduce the same case. This is a draft-merge artifact: cut one introduction.

The Brett Pullum email exchange at lines 277-299 about the *Rubymar* sentence is genuinely interesting, but the prose around it gets tangled. "we Geoff and I" at line 295. "we decided to zoomed out" at line 299. "search that long with so many common words" - the reader doesn't know what tool. This is a fine working draft of a working passage, but it needs a careful pass.

The bigram analysis of *colorless green ideas* (Pereira 2000) is the right citation but the prose around it is overlong. "Werner Heisenberg, Heidegger, Nietzsche, de Beauvoir" is a name-stack at line 183 that does nothing the chapter needs.

The five numbered "lessons" at the chapter's end (lines 621-630) are LLM-padded conclusions in the style of a teaching moment ("Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence"). They make the chapter end on five generalizations rather than on the elegant whose conclusion that preceded them. Cut.

The "world tour" at lines 563-611 (German, Spanish, Persian, Japanese) is interesting but underdeveloped. Each language gets one example with little development; the conclusion ("These cross-linguistic patterns suggest...") then makes a strong claim from thin evidence. Either build it out or cut it.

There is also a small typo: "we Geoff and I" (295), "to zoomed out" (299), and the ungainly transition at 433 ("Well, that's where things get really interesting...").

### Priority fixes
- Decide where the whose-gorilla material lives. If chapter 15 is going to do the deep dive (which it does well), then chapter 5 should not duplicate it. Cut one or the other.
- Fix the duplicate introduction at lines 366-389.
- Cut the philosophy name-stack (Heisenberg/Heidegger/Nietzsche/de Beauvoir) at line 183. The Pereira citation does the chapter's work.
- Cut the numbered "lessons" at the chapter's end. End on the cross-linguistic observation; let the reader draw their own conclusion.
- The Rubymar-and-Pullum exchange needs a clean copy-edit pass. The reasoning is good but the prose is muddled.
- The world tour is a good idea undelivered; either give it real evidence or cut it to a paragraph.

### One concrete suggestion
This chapter is two chapters trying to be one. Split into two. Chapter 15a: "When Form Clashes with Meaning" (the *I go there yesterday* example, the French *passé composé* story, the *colorless green ideas* analysis, the *Spiro conjectures Ex-Lax* example, all framed by the same idea: ungrammaticality is felt when no motivation can be reconstructed for the form-meaning clash). Chapter 15b: "The Curious Case of the Missing *Whose*" (the full whose-gorilla deep dive, including the Pullum-Brett correspondence on *Rubymar* and the cross-linguistic survey, framed as one extended case study of how three factors—topicality, type-anaphora, and structural environment—conspire to make a construction rare). Each is a satisfying chapter; together they're indigestible.

### Rhetoric that works
"It's a linguistic ivory-billed woodpecker: long thought extinct, occasionally glimpsed, hotly debated, but stubbornly refusing to be definitively ruled out." (line 422). This is the book's voice working at full strength. The image is precise (the ivory-bill controversy is real and ongoing), it's concrete, and it earns the abstract claim about a rare construction.

### Rhetoric that doesn't
"Now, you might be wondering, 'Wait a minute, I've heard *whose* used without a noun before!' And you'd be right." (line 403). The fake-dialogue with the imagined reader is a trade-book tic that doesn't fit your actual voice. You don't need to imagine the reader's objection; the example will provoke it on its own. Cut the bracket-quote-and-respond move; just present the example. "Well, that's where things get really interesting..." (line 433) is the same problem in another costume.
