## Chapter 15: What's ungrammatical

### Strengths

The mom-and-son dialogue that opens the chapter ("Hi, Mom." / "Hi, Honey! Where were you?" / "The new bookstore." / "Oh, *I go there yesterday*.") and then its rerun where Mom says "Oh, you won't believe this. So, *I go there yesterday*, and..." is a small gem. Same words, two contexts, the second producing the historical-present narrative tense, the first registering as ungrammatical because no purpose can be found. That's the chapter's thesis demonstrated in two beats.

The French passé composé/passé simple history is the chapter's strongest extended treatment. The Latin *fundamentum* > Old French *fondement* > Modern *fondement* /fɔ̃d.mɑ̃/ erosion, the verb-ending tables showing the homophonous collapse of the simple past in spoken Modern French (1st/3rd person /na.vi.ʒe/ vs. /na.vi.ʒa/, etc.), and the resulting migration of the *avoir + past participle* form into past-tense duty - this is a complete, mechanical, vivid causal story for a grammatical change. The "Matthew effect" footnote and the "whiff of provincialism" framing are both well-deployed.

The Pereira 2000 finding that *colorless green ideas sleep furiously* is roughly 200,000 times more probable on a bigram model than *furiously sleep ideas green colorless* is the right kind of empirical zing.

The Lego vs. IKEA analogy for why we default to assuming grammatical errors rather than lexical ones (Lego: lots of pieces, few connection rules; IKEA: a few specific pieces, a few specific assemblies) is one of the book's more useful images.

The Geoff Pullum / *Rubymar* email exchange - "is the ship to have been sunk by the Houthis" registering as ungrammatical, the iterative search for what licenses the *to-have-been* infinitival perfect, the discovery that a present-tense matrix verb clashes with the past-equivalent infinitival perfect - is patient working linguistics on the page. The reader gets to watch the thinking happen.

The Ex-Lax/Spiro Agnew/Pat Nixon's-cake-frosting joke from Morgan and McCawley 1973 is recovered nicely, and the framing - that a sentence is ungrammatical or grammatical depending on what question it answers - sets up the *whose* return.

### Major concerns

The chapter is doing too much. Five distinct topics (the *go there yesterday* puzzle, the French passé composé, the *Rubymar* infinitival perfect, the Ex-Lax response, and the long return to *whose*) are stitched together. Each could be its own short chapter.

The *whose* recap is a near-complete reprise of material from chapter 5, with new pragmatic analysis added. A reader who read chapter 5 carefully will feel they're being walked through the same case twice. Either trim the recap or restructure so chapter 5 sets up the puzzle and chapter 15 resolves it - currently chapter 5 also offers a solution.

The "world tour of *whose*" section is thin. German *dessen/deren*, Spanish *cuyo*, Persian's possessive constructions, Japanese (which lacks relative pronouns entirely) - each gets a sentence or two. The cross-linguistic claim ("similar patterns appear") is asserted but only the German example is genuinely worked through. The Persian and Japanese paragraphs are placeholders.

The "ivory-billed woodpecker" metaphor for the elusive independent relative *whose* is nice once and then deployed three times. Pace it.

The "grammar conveys meaning" coda recapitulates the model in chapter 1's Section 1.6. Either the model belongs in chapter 1 or it belongs here, but not in both.

### Priority fixes

1. Decide on the relationship between chapter 5 and chapter 15 on *whose*. They overlap heavily.
2. The "world tour of *whose*" section needs either real cross-linguistic data (with glossed examples from each language) or compression to a paragraph noting the parallel without the false-pretense world tour.
3. The 1748 Lord Chesterfield first attestation of *could you* in a request is a great find. Let it breathe - the politeness use of the past tense across all the modal auxiliaries is one of the chapter's good ideas and deserves more development than the current single sentence.
4. The "Ex-Lax conundrum" is a good name and a good story but the chapter rushes past it. A reader needs a moment to understand that *Spiro conjectures Ex-Lax* becomes grammatical only in the context of *what does Mrs. Nixon frost her cakes with* - this is actually a deep claim about ellipsis under question semantics, not just an amusing 1973 joke.

### One concrete suggestion

The Putnam-and-O'Hern study from chapter 13 and the Geoff Pullum Rubymar email from this chapter share something the book consistently does well: working linguists thinking through a real instance, in real time, with a paper trail. Of the two, the *Rubymar* episode is more directly linguistic and could be expanded. Geoff sent the email; the author responded; the search through COCA produced unexpected hits; the eventual diagnosis (matrix-clause present tense + infinitival perfect = clash) was a discovery, not a recall. Show the BBC source. Quote Geoff's actual email. This is investigative grammar at the desk, on a Tuesday afternoon, between the two of you. Make it a Zimmer-style scene of two scientists working a problem together. That moment is the chapter's most distinctive.
