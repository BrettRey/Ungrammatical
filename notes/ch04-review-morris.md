## Chapter 04: Morality and grammaticality

### Strengths

There are buried good things here. Steven Coats sifting 1.29 billion words of YouTube transcripts to find 54 instances of "would might" is a real human story: a Finnish linguist refusing to wait for *might could* to come to him, building a corpus from voiced English, and turning up three examples in Reynolds's home province of Ontario. That number, three, is the kind of detail that travels.

The Jeff Brown / Nietzsche moment from chapter 3 echoes here in the chapter's instinct that grammar-policing is morally inflected. The seed of an honest argument is here: that people will treat a misplaced apostrophe as a moral failing, and somewhere inside that disproportion is what the chapter wants to anatomize.

Jahangir Khan, the squash champion, is a real person with a real human shape (the brother dying mid-match of a heart attack, Jahangir feeling his presence courtside afterward).

### Major concerns

This chapter is in the worst shape of the manuscript. There are two complete and parallel sections both titled "Ethical Perspectives on Grammaticality." There is a third section called "Climbing the same mountain from different sides" that re-traverses the same territory a third time. Inside that, there's a literal apology line at line 231: "You're right. I apologize for missing that formatting detail and for not addressing the mirroring aspect of these two perspectives. Here's a revised version that incorporates these points." That is an LLM addressing Reynolds, copy-pasted into the manuscript. It must come out before any reviewer sees this file.

The chapter as a whole reads as if Reynolds asked an LLM to map ethical frameworks onto grammaticality, then asked it again, then asked again. The result is approximately 18,000 words of "let's consider example X through framework Y" prose. Most of it does not have a sentence that could not have been generated by ChatGPT in 2023. Phrases that signal this throughout: "It's worth noting that," "Despite these challenges," "This view of grammaticality has several implications," "Yet the social justice approach offers valuable insights," "This unified theory views grammaticality not as a binary state, but as a multidimensional space."

On the protagonists question: Jeremy Bentham, Derek Parfit, T. M. Scanlon, and Immanuel Kant are walked into the room one at a time, given thirty seconds to be themselves, and walked out. Parfit gets two paragraphs of biography ("Born in 1942 in China to British parents, Parfit showed early signs of his philosophical mindset. As a child, he was deeply troubled by the idea that one day he would cease to exist") that read like Wikipedia, then is reduced to an icon of "climbing the same mountain." That is a man who did not leave his college, ate the same food daily, was haunted from childhood by the prospect of his own non-existence. There's a film in him. The chapter is written as if there isn't.

Jahangir Khan is the chapter's one real character, but the analogy to language is weak. It would be sharper to find one specific moment where a real person's grammar got them treated as a moral failure (a courtroom, a job interview, a teacher's red ink on a student's paper). The chapter never shows us that moment, but the entire argument depends on it.

### Priority fixes

2. Delete the apology line at 231.
3. Decide which scaffolding (the first set of seven, the second set of seven, the third Parfit-frame pass) the chapter actually wants. Cut the other two.
4. Fill the "Ideas" stub at the top (Pirahã, Alice Evans).
5. Find a real first scene of grammar-as-moral-judgment. A teacher, a courtroom, an HR meeting, a parent. Replace the Parfit climb-the-mountain frame with that scene.

### One concrete suggestion

Find one real person whose life was shaped by another person's grammaticality judgment. A student passed over for a job. A child held back a grade. An accent making a witness less credible to a jury. Reynolds could draw on his college teaching experience here. Every linguistics graduate has at least one such case, often a family member. Open with it. Then ask: what is the moral structure of that judgment? Why did it feel justified to the judge? Now you've earned the right to bring in Bentham, Parfit, and the rest. The current chapter starts with the philosophy and reaches for the human texture later. Reverse it.
