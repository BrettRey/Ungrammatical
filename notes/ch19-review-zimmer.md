## Chapter 19: Communicative efficiency

### Strengths

The Highway 427 / Eglinton Avenue interchange figure is a small triumph. A Toronto motorist's everyday irritant - a left-side exit where every other exit is on the right - is reframed as a structural compromise that minimizes the geometric complexity of the bigger system. The same principle, the chapter argues, governs how grammar packages information: when the usual head-dependent order would be too costly, languages re-route. The figure caption and the explanation are both at the right level for trade.

The before-meetings dependency-distance examples in English and Japanese, with the same ungrammatical pattern (*before* placed pre-clause when the dependent is post-head, in (\ref{ex:take-a-rest-Distance}d)) shown to be the inverse pattern in Japanese, are clean.

The heavy NP shift example is patient and well-executed. The dependency-distance arithmetic (basic = 9, basic-with-heavy = 20, post-shifted-heavy = 10) gives the reader a real number to hold onto. The light-NP-shift counter-test (*I gave to my best friend for Christmas a book*, ungrammatical despite the same dependency-distance count) is exactly the right control: shift is licensed only when there is a reason for it.

The "no matter how many times you've been told to avoid the passive voice" passive example, with the dependency distance dropping from 21 in the active to 0 in the passive, is genuinely persuasive and a good shot across the prescriptivists' bow. Same for the *throw out the trash* gradient (a) through (g), which lets the reader feel the pressure for shift building as the noun phrase grows.

The Gildea 2007 alternating-dependent finding (heads with even numbers of dependents minimize total length when dependents alternate sides), and the observation that English approximates this through pre-head determiners and adjectives plus post-head modifiers, is a clean theoretical capstone.

### Major concerns

The chapter is short, dense, and very nearly a stand-alone paper from Futrell 2020. That is not necessarily a problem, but it does mean the chapter sits awkwardly in a trade book whose other chapters are looser and more anecdotal.

The opening - "Languages are not arbitrary in their structure but are shaped by a balance" - is the kind of throat-clearing the rest of the book is trying to avoid. The Highway 427 image should be at the top.

The chapter offers no human face. There is no scene of a speaker hesitating, choosing the heavy-NP shift, sounding more relaxed; no episode of a writer cutting a passive on a copyeditor's advice and the resulting clause becoming awkward; no person deciding to put *out* before or after *the trash*. The arithmetic is correct; the prose is unpopulated.

The Japanese example (\ref{ex:take-a-rest-Distance-J}) is given without a gloss. A trade reader who can't read kana will glance at it and skip. Even a one-line transliteration would help.

The chapter doesn't engage with the obvious counter-question: if dependency-locality is so strong a force, why do center-embedded sentences exist at all? *The horse the man the dog bit raced fell* is a real English sentence, just an unparseable one. The reader is left wondering when the principle wins and when it loses.

### Priority fixes

1. Open with Highway 427 and the figure. Save the abstract framing for after the image.
2. Gloss the Japanese example.
3. Engage briefly with center-embedding as a stress-test for the locality claim.
4. The chapter could carry a sentence-level case study from a real text: a Times opening paragraph, before and after copyediting, with dependency distances. That would let the reader feel the principle at work in the wild.

### One concrete suggestion

The trash-bag gradient (a) to (g) is the chapter's clearest illustration of the working principle, and it could go from useful to memorable with a small Zimmer-style addition. A real cook standing in front of a real bin, holding a sodden bag of two-week-old kitchen waste from under the sink, makes the choice between *I threw the trash out* and *I threw out the trash that had been sitting there for two weeks*. The choice isn't only about dependency distance - it's also about the moment of release. *Threw out* lands the moment of triumph at the end of the heavy phrase; *threw out the trash* lands the moment up front. Either gives the reader a single-frame movie of the principle. The chapter currently shows a syntactic gradient. Show a person.
