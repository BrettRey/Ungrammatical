## Chapter 11: Seeing grammar in the brain

### Strengths

The chapter is a stub of seven lines, but the two findings it cites are strong: pupil dilation as a measure of grammar-processing effort (Just 1993) and heart rate variability dropping in response to article errors (Divjak 2024). The HRV finding has an extra wrinkle Reynolds notes briefly: the drop is bigger when the speaker has an L1 accent and smaller when they have an L2 accent. That is the chapter's hidden subject. Listeners forgive grammatical errors from a non-native voice in a way they don't from a native voice. The body absolves the foreigner. That is a real human finding, and it could anchor a whole chapter.

### Major concerns

This is one of the three stub chapters Reynolds noted in the prompt. It needs a chapter, not a paragraph. The neurolinguistic literature is large and full of human-scale stories: the N400 and P600 ERP components and the people who named them, Friederici's brain-imaging work, the aphasia literature going back to Broca and Wernicke, Caramazza's case studies, agrammatism patients who can understand but not produce, jargon aphasia patients who produce fluent grammar without content. None of this is here yet.

On the protagonists question: there are obvious named people to bring into this room. Paul Broca examining Tan in 1861. Wernicke examining his patients in 1874. Friederici building her brain-imaging program in Leipzig. Kara Federmeier doing ERP work on semantic vs syntactic anomaly. Sprouse running acceptability-judgment studies. The history of the field is full of cases where one person's brain damage taught the next person something true about grammar. That history begs for documentary attention.

Particularly: the moment when Broca opened Tan's brain and found the lesion in the left frontal lobe. That's a scene. So is the moment when Wernicke distinguished his patients from Broca's. So is any of the contemporary cases in the literature. *(Un)grammatical* should not let neurolinguistics in by way of a 7-line summary.

The HRV/L1 vs L2 contrast also has an obvious human angle: there are listeners in the world who experience native-speaker grammar errors as a more bodily insult than non-native ones. Why? What does that tell us about who gets graded for grammar and who gets a pass? That connects to the morality chapter (4) and the politics chapter (6) and the codeswitching chapter (16). This stub is doing more cross-chapter work in seven lines than chapter 4 does in 18,000 words.

### Priority fixes

1. Mark this chapter clearly as a stub in STATUS.md (already noted, but reinforce: this is one of three priority stubs).
2. Decide what the chapter is actually about. Brain imaging? Aphasia case studies? ERPs? Pupillometry? All four would make a chapter; right now it's a paragraph.
3. Identify two or three specific named patients or experiments to anchor the chapter. Broca's Tan, Caramazza's anomic patients, an ERP participant whose N400 spiked at "socks."
4. Treat the HRV / L1 vs L2 finding as central, not incidental. It is the most rhetorically potent finding cited.

### One concrete suggestion

Open inside the experiment Divjak ran. A participant sits in a lab. Headphones. EKG electrodes on the chest. Sentences play through the headphones. Some have article errors. Some don't. A man with a Dutch accent says "I bought new car yesterday." The participant's heart rate variability barely moves. Then a man with a native British accent says the same sentence. The participant's heart rate variability drops. The body, somewhere below conscious notice, has decided to take offense at one and not at the other. From there: what is the body doing, and why does it do it differently for different speakers? Build the chapter from that scene outward, into Broca and Wernicke and the aphasia clinics, then back to the contemporary brain imaging that maps the offense to the angular gyrus and Broca's area. The chapter has a heart in it already, literally. Use the heart.
