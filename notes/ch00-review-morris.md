## Chapter 00: Introduction

### Strengths

The chapter has a documentary instinct buried in it. Two scenes do honest work: the Komodo island memory, with its captain who put Reynolds off the boat after an argument, and the Kinokuniya sixth floor, where a man stands until his legs hurt because he can't afford the books. These are scenes I would film. They have weather, conflict, a body. The argument with the captain in particular: that's the kind of small human break that tells you something about how a person comes to a question. He didn't choose his subject. He got marooned into it.

The six asterisked CGEL examples also work. They are funny because they are specific. "She wrote more plays than he wrote five novels" makes the reader stop and try to repair it, which is the experience the book is about. Show people a thing that breaks, and they will lean in.

### Major concerns

The trouble starts when Schleicher and Sweet enter the room. They arrive as historical action figures, not as people. "Driven by an obsessive desire for clarity and an unshakable belief in language as a natural phenomenon, Schleicher aimed to impose order on the wilderness of words." This is the language of a museum plaque written by someone who never met him. Schleicher died at 47 of tuberculosis with his work unfinished, and the chapter mentions this almost as logistics. There's a film in that sentence. Where is it?

More telling: Reynolds presents Schleicher and Sweet as heroes of progress. There are no antagonists. The "traditional philologists" are a faceless mass, "steeped in the study of literary texts and untroubled by questions of linguistic origins." That is how essentialist linguists will appear later in the book. We need to know now whether Reynolds means to treat his eventual opponents (Chomskyans, prescriptivists) as villains, fools, or honest people who made the mistake anyone would make. Right now, the chapter implies villains-by-omission, because no human face is shown.

The closing sentence about a "silent testament to a profound shift" is the prose of a person who has stepped out of the scene. It reads like narration over slow-motion footage of someone writing with a quill.

### Priority fixes

1. Cut the Schleicher and Sweet biographical filler by half. Keep Aristophanes of Byzantium (specific, weird, memorable). Lose the adjective inflation.
2. Find one moment of real friction for either man. Schleicher being scorned. Sweet being mocked. A letter, a review, a colleague's diary. Make the human cost of an idea visible.
3. Decide what kind of antagonist this book has and signal it now. If essentialists are reasonable people who got captured by a beautiful framework, say so. If they are obstacles, say that.
4. Open with Komodo or Kinokuniya, not Mill. The Mill epigraph is a wall.

### One concrete suggestion

The argument with the captain is the chapter's hidden first scene. Use it. "Somewhere off Komodo, a captain I had argued with put me off his boat. A wooden fishing boat took me out into the strait, where a ferry eventually appeared. I don't remember where I went next. I do remember the feeling of being deposited, of having been wrong about a passage I thought I understood." Then: that feeling is what an ungrammatical sentence does to a reader. You thought you understood the route. The boat won't carry you. Now you start asking why.
