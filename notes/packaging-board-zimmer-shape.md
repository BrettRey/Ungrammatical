## Overall shape: Zimmer's take

### What the book is currently doing structurally

Twenty-two chapters. They fall into rough piles. There's a long, ruminative opening (Introduction + 01) that sells the asterisk, the linguist's quirky obsession, and Brett's own road-to-Damascus story. Then a theory cluster (02 Gradient, 03 Not enough, 07 Generative gap, 10 Real patterns) that develops the technical machinery. Then a domain tour, which is the book's commercial premise (04 Morality, 06 Politics, 14 Fashion, 16 Codeswitching, 18 Swearing). Threaded through are historical-evolution chapters (12 Becoming ungrammatical, 13 Across language boundaries, 15 What's ungrammatical, 19 Efficiency), a long psychology-of-feeling chapter (05 How grammar feels), a brain chapter (11), an "impossible languages" chapter (17), a closer on getting it wrong (20), and a beautifully grounded final chapter (21 A long, long way).

The arc as currently sequenced moves: hook -> theory -> moral domain -> theory -> non-grammar -> historical evolution -> domain tour resumes -> impossible languages -> swearing -> efficiency -> wrap-up -> grounded set piece. Chapter lengths are wildly uneven (forty-line stubs sit beside nine-hundred-line behemoths).

### What's working at the level of shape

The premise is enormously promising. The book has a real one-line elevator pitch (\textit{ungrammatical means many different things, and that diversity is the whole story}) and that pitch is the right kind for a trade reader. Chapter 00's asterisk-genealogy (Schleicher to Sweet to Householder) is exactly the kind of \textit{and then I went to} opening a non-specialist needs. It gives them a symbol to look for, two characters to follow, and a payoff (the asterisk hides variation) that frames everything to come.

Chapter 01's Ross-and-the-islands move is a strong second beat: it grounds an abstract phenomenon (extraction) in a specific 1966 graduate student. Chapter 12's etymology of \textit{very} usurping \textit{much} is the same kind of move and works beautifully. Chapter 21 (long, long way) is the strongest chapter in the book: it has reduplication as a hook, Old English as time travel, Jamaican Creole and Vanuatu for stakes, and ESL students laughing in a Toronto classroom for human warmth. It's where the book is best at being a book.

### What's not working at the level of shape

The book is currently a collection of essays in search of an arc. Three structural problems.

First, the abstract chapters cluster too thickly. After the warm opening, a reader hits 02 (Gradient grammaticality, with Chomsky and toy grammars), 03 (Not enough, with relative-clause syntax and Riau), 04 (Morality, which spends 700 lines on Bentham, contractualism, Kantian deontology, and Parfit's mountain), and 05 (Intuitions, which is 900 lines and spends large stretches on ChatGPT vector-space monologues). That's roughly two thousand lines of philosophical-and-formal terrain before the reader sees another concrete \textit{and then I went to} narrative. They will not survive it.

Second, the domain tour, which is the book's marketing concept, is scattered and badly proportioned. Morality gets 724 lines; Politics gets 40. Fashion gets 63; Codeswitching gets 47; Swearing is 15 lines of notes. The reader has been promised a tour of seven domains but is given one extended philosophy lecture (ch. 04) and a series of stubs.

Third, the book's strongest set piece (ch. 21) is at the end, where most readers won't reach it. Meanwhile the introduction does not telegraph the arc the book actually wants to deliver. A reader finishing ch. 00 doesn't know they're going on a journey across moral judgment, fashion, codeswitching, neurolinguistics; they think they're going to get a syntax-flavoured memoir.

The HPC payoff (the book's intellectual spine) is currently nowhere visible at the structural level. There's no chapter titled along the lines of "What grammaticality actually is" that synthesizes the cluster account. Ch. 15 (What's ungrammatical) gestures at it but is heterogeneous. Without a clear synthesis chapter, the domain-tour chapters feel like a list, not a convergent argument.

### What would change the shape for the better

Open with what's already your best chapter. Move ch. 21 (A long, long way) to slot 01. Keep the asterisk-genealogy as the introduction, but follow it with the reduplication chapter, not islands. Reduplication is more accessible, has cross-linguistic stakes built in, and gives the reader a model for the whole book: a pattern that feels obvious, a history that explains it, a journey across languages that complicates it. Move the islands chapter (current 01) to a later technical position.

Cut 04 (Morality) by half. The Bentham/Khan/Parfit material is interesting but it's the second-longest chapter in the book and currently presents three full ethical frameworks before saying anything about language. Trim to the single argument: grammar judgments carry moral weight, AAVE is the case, here's why that matters. Save Parfit's mountain for an essay elsewhere.

Cut 05 (Intuitions) by two-thirds. The ChatGPT vector-space passages are scaffolding; they shouldn't be in the book. The chapter should be the metacognitive-feeling argument plus Hankamer-and-Postal plus Knobe on conflicting intuitions, no more.

Combine the stub chapters. 06 Politics, 14 Fashion, 16 Codeswitching, 18 Swearing are currently four separate stubs each making a version of the same point: the same grammatical form is licit in one community and illicit in another, and the licensing involves identity, register, and stance. Make them one chapter (call it "Whose grammar?" or "Grammar and group") with five short sections. That fixes the proportion problem and turns a grab bag into a thesis.

Add a synthesis chapter explicit about the HPC argument. Currently ch. 15 (What's ungrammatical) is heterogeneous. Either repurpose it or insert a new chapter (around slot 18 in the new ordering) that says: \textit{here's the cluster, here's why the diversity converges, here's what we should mean by `ungrammatical' going forward}. Without this, the book ends without delivering its own thesis.

Combine 11 (Neurolinguistics, 7 lines) and 19 (Efficiency, 131 lines) into one "Why grammar feels the way it does" chapter that ties the metacognitive-feeling claim to dependency-locality and pupil-dilation evidence. Drop 17 (Impossible languages) unless it can be transformed into the synthesis chapter, where it would actually earn its keep as the limit case.

Suggested new spine: 00 asterisk -> 01 long-long-way (was 21) -> 02 islands (was 01) -> 03 gradient (was 02) -> 04 not-enough (was 03) -> 05 the generation gap (was 07) -> 06 real patterns (was 10) -> 07 how grammar feels (was 05, cut by 2/3) -> 08 grammar in the brain (merged 11+19) -> 09 becoming ungrammatical (was 12) -> 10 across language boundaries (was 13) -> 11 without grammar (was 09) -> 12 whose grammar? (merged 06+14+16+18) -> 13 morality (was 04, cut by half) -> 14 conformism (was 08) -> 15 impossible languages (was 17, only if rebuilt) -> 16 what grammaticality is (synthesis) -> 17 getting it wrong (was 20).

### The one structural move

Open with ch. 21. The book already contains its own best chapter: the reduplication chapter is a model of the trade-book ground-then-abstract pattern Brett needs everywhere. Putting it in slot 01 does three things at once. It tells the reader what kind of book this is. It makes the abstract chapters that follow feel earned (because the reader now trusts the author to come back to ground). And it lets ch. 00 stop trying to do everything at once. Once the reader is hooked on \textit{long long road}, they'll follow him to Riau Indonesian, AAVE, Bentham, and the Parisian sweatsuit.
