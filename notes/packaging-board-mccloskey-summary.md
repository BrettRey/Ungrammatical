# McCloskey: Cross-chapter summary

## Two voices in one manuscript

The book has a charming, specific, working voice and a generic, hedging, encyclopedia voice, and they fight on almost every page. The first voice tells stories from a teacher's classroom in Toronto, a yacht off Komodo Island, a kitchen during SARS, a footnote about NHL elbows, an email from Cory Doctorow ("Yup"). The second voice writes "It is a testament to the resilience and adaptability of the English language" and "the very nature of language as a human invention." The second voice is the LLM-padding from earlier assisted writing, and it is the book's biggest single problem.

The first voice is excellent. The book that exists if you delete the second voice is a real book.

## Where the book is consistently strong

Three things almost always work: (a) anecdotes from Brett's actual life, classroom, and correspondence (Tomo translating in Toronto, his wife's "you still at the gym" text, the John Payne email exchange about *much*, the Geoff Pullum email about *Rubymar*); (b) careful empirical case studies that resolve in the chapter (Chapter 14's *enshittification* and *even it's never approved*; Chapter 21's *long, long road*; Chapter 12's *very/much* historical exchange; Chapter 10's *probable to play*); (c) cross-linguistic comparisons that make the empirical point that English is one option among several (Riau Indonesian, Jamaican Creole *blakblak*, French passé composé). These chapters trust the evidence and let the abstract argument follow.

## Where the book consistently undermines itself

Five recurring patterns: (1) Triple-pasted draft sections (Chapter 4 has the ethics frameworks three times; Chapter 5 has the constructionist section twice; Chapter 14's *whose* material is also in Chapters 5 and 15). (2) Visible LLM-transcript residue (Chapter 6's "You propose..." section; Chapter 5's ChatGPT vector-space dump; Chapter 12's TED-talk closing about "the never-ending dance of language"; an actual LLM apology accidentally pasted into Chapter 4). (3) Bullet-list "outline" sections that haven't been turned into prose (Chapter 8's "Learning as Performance:" headers; Chapter 16's fifteen-bullet introduction). (4) Sentences that trail off (Chapter 9's "But David Gil s"; Chapter 5's "Geoff Pullum has a suggestions"; Chapter 11's two-sentence stub). (5) Source-grounding violations: a garbled OCR'd Hurford quotation in Chapter 9; a possibly-invented Cory Doctorow Dr. Seuss quote in Chapter 14; a misremembered Pistorius narrative in Chapter 2.

## The one rhetorical repair that would do the most work

**Cut every paragraph in the manuscript that says something general about language as if from a great height.** The signature sentences are easy to find: they begin with "It's a testament to," "in this way," "ultimately," "in conclusion," "this is at the heart of," and they always end with "the very nature of," "the rich tapestry of," "the never-ending dance of." Every one of these sentences is a generative-prose tic. Every one of them tells the reader nothing the chapter's examples haven't already told them. Cut all of them. The chapters will become 30% shorter and 100% more credible.

What remains will be a book by a working linguist who notices things, calls his collaborators, runs examples by his international students, watches a toddler translate Japanese for his grandmother, and follows the *very/much* trajectory through Caxton's 1481 prose. That book is well worth publishing. It just has to lose the chorus of an absent stranger doing the throat-clearing.
