## Chapter 07: The Generation Gap

### Strengths
This is the clearest piece of expository writing in the book. The dust-storm example walks the non-linguist through discrete combination, hierarchical structure, and recursion in five paragraphs without jargon. The structural argument that *a dust storm* takes *cosmic* on the inside but not the outside, and that this requires order of combination, is a genuine bit of teaching. A non-linguist reading this chapter learns something specific. The Culicover example (likely/probable) lands. McCawley's *New Yorker* quote at the end gives the chapter a payoff: from the inside, the consensus came apart.

The chapter is also short, which after the bloat of chapters 4 and 5 is a relief. It does its work and stops.

### Major concerns
The chapter is too short. It promises in its title to cover the generation gap, the fact that Chomsky's students broke from him, and it gestures at this in two paragraphs at the end. It doesn't actually tell the story. The Linguistics Wars are arguably the most dramatic episode in 20th-century linguistics; a non-linguist reader will have no idea what just happened. Postal, Lakoff, McCawley, generative semantics: each is a name, none a story.

The Boeckx quote at the end ("island repair shouldn't happen in derivational approaches") will not parse for a non-linguist. The chapter has just spent five paragraphs explaining discrete combination in plain English, then abruptly switches to "post-syntactic operation" and "extraction" without preparation. The chapter ends mid-thought.

The argument's pivot at line 39, "By not being static. The way the language evolves is towards systematicity," is the move the chapter most needs to develop, but it gets one sentence. This is the chapter's actual contribution: that grammar generates and tolerates ungrammar; that "the grammar" is a moving target. The reader needs three more paragraphs explaining what this means in practice.

There's also a missing connective between McCawley/Postal/Lakoff and the rest of the book. Why does the reader need to know about the Linguistics Wars? Probably because the book's HPC argument about gradient grammaticality is a descendant of the generative-semanticists' position. If so, say so. As written, the reader is told a famous group of linguists thought Chomsky was wrong, and then the chapter ends.

### Priority fixes
- Develop the Linguistics Wars in two pages. Who were Postal, Lakoff, McCawley? Why did they leave generative grammar? What was generative semantics? What did Chomsky's "extended standard theory" become? A non-linguist needs to come away with the cast of characters and the stakes.
- Translate the Boeckx quote. The phrase "island repair" needs a sentence of explanation: sometimes a sentence that should be ungrammatical by the grammar's own rules becomes acceptable in context, and this is hard for a generative theory.
- Develop the closing pivot ("By not being static") into the chapter's ending. The book's argument is that generativity-or-not is the wrong question; the right question is what kind of dynamic system grammar is. State this here.
- Give the chapter the right scope. It's currently the shortest substantive chapter in the book, and it's the one with the most explanatory work to do. Two thousand more words.

### One concrete suggestion
Treat this as the book's pivot chapter and give it the weight that earns. Open with the Lego-block image you already have. Walk through discrete combination and recursion with the dust-storm. Pause: "But here's what the early generative grammarians didn't notice: their grammar would generate sentences that no one says, and would fail to generate sentences that everyone says." Use Culicover's *likely*/*probable* example. Then introduce the Linguistics Wars: Postal, Lakoff, McCawley, the meeting at MIT, the breakdown. End with the question that organizes the rest of the book: if grammar is not a generator producing all and only the grammatical strings, what is it? The HPC answer, which the book builds toward, is that grammar is a homeostatic property cluster: a dynamic, internally-stabilizing pattern that tolerates noise. Hint at this here without committing.

### Rhetoric that works
"Like combining building blocks or Lego pieces. Each piece retains its identity and properties when it is combined with others, but the resulting structure has its own properties that are more than the sum of its parts." This is the right level for a non-linguist. It builds intuition without analogy-creep.

### Rhetoric that doesn't
"At least, this was the way most linguists were thinking about it in the late sixties." This is fine, but the chapter needs the next paragraph: not just "by the early seventies" but "and here's what happened, and here's why it matters for the rest of the book." The reader is at the cliff edge with nowhere to step.

### Note on chapter title
"The Generation Gap" is a real pun (generative grammar / generational divide). It works once the chapter delivers on both sides of it. As written, it delivers on the first half and waves at the second. Tell the generational story and the title earns itself.
