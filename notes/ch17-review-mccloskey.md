## Chapter 17: Impossible languages

### Strengths
The chapter has the right anchor: Chomsky's claim that some sequences of words are not just ungrammatical in a particular language but couldn't be part of any human language at all. This is a genuinely fascinating area, and the impossible-language thought experiment (prime-number-length, even-occurrence-counts, tense-hop-four-words-rightward) is exactly the right way for a non-linguist to grasp what's special about real human languages.

The Sampson 1975 quotation is the chapter's intellectual center. It points at the real problem: any reasonable transformational grammar can describe any imaginable language, including the prime-number language. The notation system gives an ordering of complexity, but the discovery that human languages are bunched at the simple end of one particular notation's complexity is itself the fact that needs explaining.

The Kallini et al. 2024 figure with its impossibility continuum is a real recent finding (LLMs trained on impossible languages perform worse than those trained on real ones, suggesting that "impossibility" is a discoverable property). The chapter is plugged into current research.

The Bishop Wilkins 1668 reference and the Okrent quote ("when you speak in concepts, it's too damn hard to say anything") are exactly the right historical anchor. Trade readers will recognize the joke.

### Major concerns
This chapter is a stub. The first 33 lines are notes-to-self: a timeline at the top, two bullet lists, three Moro quotations without prose. Then a `\chapter{Impossible languages}` command at line 35, which means the chapter actually starts there and everything before is a draft sketch (or worse, a duplicate chapter command).

The actual chapter (lines 35-87) is shorter than the notes that precede it. It has the right examples (the four perturbations of "He cleans all the books on his very messy bookshelf"; *colorless green ideas*) but it doesn't develop the argument. The reader doesn't learn:

- What Moro's argument is (the chapter cites him three times in notes but never explains)
- What Kallini et al. actually found
- Why impossible languages are theoretically important (the answer is that they're a probe for what makes human grammar specifically human, which is an HPC-relevant question)
- Whether the impossibility hypothesis has been challenged (it has; ergative-absolutive languages were once thought impossible)

The Chomsky / Pereira contrast about *colorless green ideas* (lines 72-87) is the same material as Chapter 15 (lines 173-185). It's been moved or duplicated. Decide which chapter it belongs in.

The chapter has a structural confusion: line 34 says "An impossible language is a non-recursive one" (Moro). Line 31 says "'Impossible languages' are learnable, but they're learned differently" (also Moro). These two claims need explanation. As written, they sit next to each other without engagement.

The chapter ends mid-thought at line 87. There's no closing claim, no return to the chapter's question.

### Priority fixes
- Decide whether the chapter should exist as written or be expanded substantially. As is, it doesn't earn its place.
- Cut the timeline and bullet lists at the top. Either integrate their content into prose or eliminate.
- Resolve the duplicate *colorless green ideas* analysis with chapter 15.
- Add a clear statement of what makes a language "impossible." Moro's two competing definitions (non-recursive; learnable but differently) need engagement.
- Cite Kallini et al. 2024 properly. The "Mission: Impossible" paper is the chapter's most current evidence, and trade readers love an LLM-experiment story (LLMs trained on impossible-language data do worse than LLMs trained on real-language data, but they still learn to do something).
- Connect the chapter to the book's argument. Why does an HPC view care about impossible languages? Probably: HPC says grammaticality clusters are stabilized by use, not specified by a universal grammar; if so, there should be a continuum from easy-to-stabilize to impossible-to-stabilize, with the "impossible" end being a property of human cognitive constraints, not innate UG. That's a real argument; the chapter could make it.

### One concrete suggestion
Build the chapter as a contest between two views. View 1: Chomsky/Moro-style innatism, which says impossible languages are excluded by Universal Grammar. View 2: an empiricist/HPC view, which says impossible languages are excluded by what humans can stably coordinate on, given their cognitive resources. Use the four perturbations as examples both views can describe. Then bring in Kallini et al. as the empirical pivot: large language models, which have no UG, still struggle with impossible languages. This is awkward for both views, but it's interesting because it shows that "impossibility" might be a property of the languages themselves (low statistical learnability) rather than a property of human-specific cognition. Close with the HPC reframing: a language is "possible" when its patterns are learnable from finite exposure and stable under transmission. Anything that fails either condition is impossible. The book's argument lands.

### Rhetoric that works
"Sometimes, the best way to be clear about what something is is to imagine what it is not." (line 51). The chapter's pedagogical strategy in one sentence. Build outward from this.

### Rhetoric that doesn't
"Linguists have ventured into the realm of impossible languages, crafting systems that are ungrammatical -- ingrammatical, anitgrammatical -- by their very nature." (line 37). The "ingrammatical -- antigrammatical" hyphenated alternatives feel like the writer is throwing in synonyms because none is quite right. Pick one. "By their very nature" is filler; if a language is impossible, it's impossible by definition.

### A note on chapter scope
Currently this is the most underwritten of the substantive chapters. It has good materials (the four perturbations, Kallini et al., Wilkins, Sampson, Moro) and a clear thesis available. With 1500-2000 more words and one good closing argument, it could be one of the book's strongest chapters because the topic itself is so striking. Right now it's a sketch.
