## Chapter 01: Stories from syntactic islands

### Strengths
The opening conceit (Chomsky as island-finder, Ross as cartographer) is genuinely vivid. The arrow-tracking display of "What did they see ___?" is exactly the right pedagogy for non-linguists; people need to *see* movement, not be told about it. The pacing works: a strange sentence first, then a contrasting easy case, so the reader feels the wall in the second. The Tristan chord and Herbie Hancock pairing is bold and earns its place; both stories make the abstract debate about universal grammar versus convention concrete and emotionally legible. Putting these in the chapter where the reader first meets ungrammaticality is structurally smart, the rest of the book hinges on this distinction.

### Major concerns
The chapter wants to be three chapters: an island-extraction primer, a history of English grammars (Fisher, Lowth, Brown, Taylor), and a model-of-grammaticality manifesto with fourteen numbered theses. The model section (4.3) is the most damaging. It crashes a meandering, charming chapter into a wall of bullet points and sub-bullets. A trade book reader will skip it; a linguist will be irritated by the level of compression. Either move 4.3 into an appendix or unfold it as the spine of the next several chapters. Right now it functions as a content-dump.

The Jeremy Taylor section ends in classic LLM-padding: "This insight is at the heart of the modern linguistic understanding of grammaticality... The structure and form of our words is not just a mundane aspect of communication, but a powerful tool for conveying meaning. As such, the concept of grammaticality is central to any serious study or use of language." This is meta-conclusion bloat. It also follows the "On the one hand X / Despite this" frame three times in a row, which is an LLM tic.

The chapter has at least two textual artifacts of restoration: the first sentence of section "As for Jeremy Taylor" appears truncated, with a stray comma at line 108: ", he became a chaplain to King Charles I". The footnote "this is the only footnote in this book" is contradicted by a long footnote at the end of section 1, line 186.

The Ann Fisher detour is fascinating and largely earns itself, but the detail about her reluctance to put her name on the cover, her plagiarism by Kirkby, and the *he* rule needs to either feed the argument (about who gets to define grammaticality) or be cut. Right now it reads as digression for digression's sake.

### Priority fixes
- Decide what 4.3 (the 14-thesis model) is for. Either appendix or chapter spine. It does not belong here.
- Cut the Jeremy Taylor closing paragraphs (lines 124-130) wholesale. They say nothing the reader needs.
- Repair the broken first line about Taylor at line 108.
- Resolve the "only footnote" claim. Either cut both footnotes or own the irony.
- Cut "His background in philosophy had fuelled..." and similar boilerplate from the Fisher and Lowth sections; tighten by 30%.

### One concrete suggestion
Make the chapter promise smaller and the payoff bigger. Title aside, the chapter promises "stories from syntactic islands." Tell three: Ross discovering them, Householder doubting the asterisk, and one modern story (perhaps Hofmeister and Sag, or a satiation experiment). End the chapter exactly where the Tristan/Hancock anecdotes end. Move the history of English grammars to its own chapter or into ch 7 on generative grammar. Move the model to an appendix or break it into pieces that anchor each subsequent chapter. Right now, you've put the engine block, the windshield, and the exhaust pipe in the same packing crate.

### Rhetoric that works
"They saw what?" / "What did they see ___?" laid one beneath the other, with the underline. This is teaching by showing, which the rest of the book should imitate.

### Rhetoric that doesn't
"As such, the concept of grammaticality is central to any serious study or use of language." This sentence flatters the topic and the reader simultaneously. It is the rhetorical equivalent of clearing your throat for a minute and then sitting down. Cut on sight.
