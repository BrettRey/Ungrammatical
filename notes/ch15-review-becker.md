## Chapter 15: What's ungrammatical

### Strengths
The *I go there yesterday* mother-and-son contrast at the start is a clean, direct demonstration of the chapter's central claim: a meaning-clash that can't be motivated reads as ungrammatical, while the same surface form, motivated by storytelling, reads as fine. This is a teaching example that earns its place. The Old French to Modern French passé composé story is also strong: a real diachronic case where what was once ungrammatical became fully grammatical, with phonological and social factors traceable. The *Rubymar* investigation with Geoff Pullum (lines 277-323) is the chapter's standout. It is a real piece of collaborative empirical detective work, walks the reader through hypothesis generation, hypothesis falsification by COCA browsing, and arrives at a defensible new claim (the clash is between matrix-clause present tense and an infinitival perfect). That's genuinely good linguistic argument and it earns reader trust.

The *whose* return at section 15.5 is also valuable architectural work. The book has been promising a multi-factor account of how grammaticality emerges from the interaction of syntax, semantics, pragmatics, and information structure, and this section actually delivers on the promise. The "double anaphora" / topicality story for *whose* is a real explanation, not a hand-wave.

The Spiro/Ex-Lax/Mrs. Nixon McCawley-and-Morgan example is gold and beautifully placed.

### Major concerns
At ~635 lines this is by some margin the longest chapter, and it's doing too many jobs. *I go there yesterday*, French passé composé, *colorless green ideas*, biopic vs myopic, vowel watersheds, /siːb/ as Persian apple vs Zoogocho Zapotec goat, trilled /r/ iconicity, past-tense politeness, *could*-as-request, *Rubymar*, the McCawley/Morgan Ex-Lax case, and the *whose* recap and world-tour. Each of these is a real piece, but they don't all want to be in one chapter.

The chapter title and opening "What's ungrammatical" set the reader up for a definition. The chapter then sprawls across phenomena rather than converging on one. By the end the reader has seen many examples but doesn't have a single answer to the title's question. A book of this kind probably can't deliver a single answer (that's the HPC point), but the chapter could deliver an *architecture* — a list of three or four mechanisms that produce ungrammaticality — and exemplify each with one good case.

There's also a structural anomaly. Section 15.5 ("The curious case of the missing *whose*") repeats much of chapter 5's *whose* material. The recap is signposted ("Earlier in our exploration… we encountered a puzzling case"), but the chapter then re-walks the entire investigation. A reader who read chapter 5 will feel they're being re-served the same dish. The new material — the topicality and double-anaphora analysis — is genuinely new and chapter-worthy, but it's buried inside a recap. Either trim the recap drastically or reorganize so that chapter 5 sets up the puzzle and chapter 15 solves it. Currently both chapters do both.

The "world tour of *whose*" subsection (lines 563-611) is the most LLM-padded part. It walks through German, Spanish, Persian, and Japanese in quick succession, asserts patterns, and then offers a five-point summary list. The German example is fine; the Spanish, Persian, and Japanese paragraphs are content-light. Source-grounding is missing for the Spanish *cuyo* judgement and the Persian/Japanese claims. A reader who knows any of these languages will notice.

The closing "five lessons" list (lines 619-631) has phrases that read as AI tics: "absence of evidence is not evidence of absence," "language is always full of surprises," "even after centuries of study." Cut.

The Winckel et al. (2025) reference at the very end (line 633) is a forward link that needs verification. If it doesn't exist yet at the time of writing, it shouldn't be cited.

### Priority fixes
- Decide whether the *whose* puzzle lives in chapter 5 or chapter 15. It can't live in both, identically, with a recap connecting them.
- Cut the world-tour subsection or develop it with primary sources.
- Cut the closing five-point list. The chapter's argument is already made by then; the list is an AI-style sign-off.
- The *Rubymar* / infinitival-perfect investigation deserves to be a section of its own, fully developed. Right now it's tucked at the end of a long sprawl.
- The vowel watershed / phonology section (lines 200-237) is good but reads as a separate chapter on phonological variation. Consider whether it belongs here.
- Source-ground all foreign-language data: the Old/Modern French IPA (the Old French is "speculative" by your own admission, which is fine if you're explicit), the Italian *Non è venuto nessuno*, the Persian *sib*, the Zoogocho Zapotec *sib*, the trilled /r/ iconicity claim.
- Trim the closing references to Heidegger, Heisenberg, Nietzsche, de Beauvoir at line 183. The list of obscure-but-comprehensible utterances is doing rhetorical work but it goes on too long.

### One concrete suggestion
Replace the chapter's current sprawl with three case studies, each one carefully developed, each one demonstrating a different mechanism producing ungrammaticality:
1. *Rubymar* — clash between matrix tense and infinitival perfect (semantic/structural)
2. *I have seen it yesterday* / *J'ai changé hier* — clash between perfect-as-present and past-time adverbial (semantic/diachronic, with the French diachronic case as the comparative resolution)
3. The *whose* topicality story — failure of multiple discourse conditions to align (information-structural)

The Ex-Lax case can be a transition between (1) and (2). The phonology section can move into chapter 11. The closing five-point list comes out. The chapter ends on the *whose* analysis as the chapter's culminating example.

This restructure honours the book's central HPC claim — that ungrammaticality is a cluster phenomenon arising from multiple distinct mechanisms — and shows the reader, by example, what that means. A generative reader will see three carefully-argued cases and conclude the author can do disciplined empirical work; the current chapter shows the same author doing too many things in one go.
