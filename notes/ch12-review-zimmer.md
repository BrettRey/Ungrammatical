## Chapter 12: Becoming (un)grammatical

### Strengths

The opening narrative is the chapter's strongest material. *Verray* arrives in late-13th-century English from Anglo-French, meaning *true*. *Much* is already there from Proto-Germanic *mekilaz*, meaning *great* in size, then in amount. By 1481, Caxton can write *moche fair*, *moche grete*, *moche noble*. Today, *much fair* is ungrammatical. The Caxton passage, presented in both modernized and original Middle English, is exactly the kind of textual artifact that makes a historical claim concrete.

The frequency chart of *very* and *much* across decades from 1470s to 1690s shows the actual handover. Trade readers love this kind of evidence because the line plots match the linguistic claim - *very* rises while *much* dips before recovering as the two specialize.

The Jespersen's Cycle treatment of French *ne... pas* is well-paced and the etymology of *pas* (`step'), *point* (`point'), *personne* (`person'), and *rien* (`nothing') as originally intensifiers that ate the negation is genuinely lovely - the words themselves are tiny fossils of a grammatical change.

The English NPI cluster - *drink a drop*, *sleep a wink*, *lift a finger*, *budge an inch*, *bat an eyelash*, *miss a beat*, *show a spark of decency*, *hurt a fly* - is a delightful list and the proposed parallel to French *pas* (smallest unit > intensifier > polarity item) lands.

The discovery of *much* becoming an NPI in the 19th century - John Payne's email observation, the corpus search yielding 8% then 15% then 28% then 31% non-affirmative environments by decade - is real archival linguistics happening on the page.

### Major concerns

The "frith" section (one paragraph) is an orphan. The Old English word for `peace' is named, the question is asked ("how does it register?"), nothing is answered.

The Assertion-and-Presupposition section reads like notes pasted in mid-chapter. The Cohn-Gordon quote is excellent but lacks lead-in and aftermath. The transition into Kinzler's accent-bias work in infants is abrupt.

The chapter ends with a section labeled as a new chapter ("The onward march of *do*-support") that is actually about embedded SAI (*I wonder what did you say*), and the bottom half of that section is heavy LLM padding: "Whatever its underlying causes, the rise of embedded SAI serves as a powerful reminder that language is not a static entity but a dynamic, ever-evolving system." That's a textbook concluding paragraph that adds nothing to the empirical observation.

The McWhorter quote at the end ("Language is not a protocol legislated by an authority but rather a wiki...") is fine, but the chapter then continues with two more paragraphs of generic celebration ("we are all just witnesses to the never-ending dance of language..."). Cut.

The "Matthew effect" footnote about Latin penultimate stress eroding final syllables is good content but applied to a generic "this is a common driver of change" claim that the chapter doesn't actually develop.

### Priority fixes

1. The chapter should end where the *much* corpus story ends. Cut from the do-support section onward, or move the do-support material to its own short chapter and trim the LLM-generated celebratory ending.
2. Resolve the *frith* aside - either anchor it or cut it.
3. The S-curve figure of language adoption is generic; if it stays, anchor it to a specific case (the *much* NPI shift, with the actual decade-by-decade percentages from the corpus search).
4. The Joseph block quote on Saussure's centripetal/centrifugal forces is well-deployed but introduces a new framework right at the chapter's exit. Either make it the chapter's spine or cut it.

### One concrete suggestion

The Caxton 1481 passage is the chapter's best textual artifact and it should get the kind of slow attention it deserves. Where was the book printed? (Westminster.) Who was buying it? (English nobility wanting French romances.) What did the typeface look like, and how did *moche fair* read to a 1481 customer who would have written exactly that themselves? Five sentences of context for the passage, and a pointer to the actual EEBO image of the page, would let a reader feel the historical distance between *moche fair* and *very fair* not as a chart point but as a printer's choice in a particular shop. The book deserves to be a thing in the world, not a citation.
