## Chapter 09: Without Grammar

### Strengths
The Ethan-and-his-mother transcript is the chapter's best material. Real speech, real child, real CHILDES data, with a clear analytic point: a one-year-old's utterances aren't ungrammatical-by-violation, they're ungrammatical-by-absence-of-grammar. That distinction does real work for the book's larger HPC argument. The "Mommy sock" example, with its two readings (Mommy is putting a sock on me / Mommy's sock), is exactly the right level of evidence: short, classic, immediately interpretable.

The conceptual move from Ethan to Riau Indonesian is also a productive one. Linking developmental ungrammaticality to (claimed) typological ungrammaticality and (suggested) phylogenetic ungrammaticality is the kind of architectural move the book needs to make: the same phenomenon — communication without much grammar — appears at multiple time scales. The "logogeny" coinage is reasonable shorthand if you keep using it.

The Gil quote on Riau Indonesian's "vagueness with respect to the predicate/attribute distinction" is exactly the right citation. It's the most defensible version of Gil's claim, and it lets you make the chapter's argument without overcommitting to Gil's most controversial positions.

### Major concerns
This chapter is currently the most exposed of the whole book to the source-grounding rule, and the most exposed politically. Riau Indonesian as "a language with little grammar" is a Gil claim that has serious detractors (Engelhardt, Cole, Hermon, etc.); McWhorter's creole-prototype argument is also contested (DeGraff has argued forcefully that creole exceptionalism is, at best, an artefact of researcher attention). A chapter of a trade book that asserts "Riau Indonesian is a linguistic enigma where speakers do without grammar" without flagging the controversy is going to look uninformed to anyone in the field, and is going to look politically careless to readers who care about how we describe languages of the Global South.

The chapter also embeds an OCR-corrupted block quote at line 178 ("condensing a mess variety, and sections of this chapter so las, creoles pagins…"). That has to come out before any reader sees it.

A separate problem: the prose is heavily LLM-padded. The Sumatra geography passage at lines 95-105 reads like a tourism brochure ("rich tapestry of cultural influences," "melting pots of diverse cultures," "rich and diverse landscape," "vital role in global trade and economics"). It doesn't earn its place; the reader doesn't need three paragraphs on volcanoes and the Malacca Strait to grasp where Riau is. The "Continuity of Ungrammatical Communication" section at lines 63-91 is also generated-looking, with its closing flourishes ("a poetic affirmation of our innate ability to connect with one another," "speaks not just to the mind but to the soul"). That's fatal in a chapter that's already on thin empirical ice.

The Ernst Haeckel "ontogeny recapitulates phylogeny" gesture at line 24 deserves more care. You note that Haeckel was wrong, but the Hurford two-stage developmental story you then describe is exactly the kind of argument that *needs* Haeckel's discredited frame to do its work, because it claims that individual language acquisition recapitulates language phylogenesis. If you don't engage that move, a careful reader will notice you've smuggled it in.

Hurford's "two-stage development" claim is also a strong claim about language origins and shouldn't be presented as settled. There are competing accounts (Tomasello, Christiansen and Chater, Kirby, Smith and Brighton, Bickerton). The chapter currently presents Hurford as if he were uncontroversial.

### Priority fixes
- Fix the corrupted block quote at line 178.
- Cut the Sumatra geography passage by 80%. One sentence locates Riau on the island; you don't need the rest.
- Cut the "Continuity of Ungrammatical Communication" subsection. The Ethan/Riau pair already makes this argument better than the LLM-generated paragraphs do.
- Acknowledge the controversy around Riau Indonesian and creole exceptionalism. The chapter doesn't have to resolve the controversy, but it has to be visible.
- Flag the Hurford-style story as one position among several.
- Source-ground all linguistic data. The Italian sentence ("Il museo d'arte moderna è interessante") and the Japanese banana example need verification (the Japanese is fine but the gloss "I ate the banana (mush)" is making a unit/mass argument that needs care).
- Decide whether logogeny earns the new term. Chesterton's fence applies: if the field hasn't coined a term for "the historical emergence of language," there might be reasons.

### One concrete suggestion
Lead with Ethan, end with Ethan. The chapter's strongest claim is the comparative one: Ethan, Riau Indonesian, and (perhaps) early human language all share a feature — communication without much grammatical apparatus. Open with Ethan, then say "this isn't a phase, or even just a phase. There are real cases of language used adult-to-adult that work like this." Bring in Gil's Riau data carefully, with the controversy flagged. Then a brief Hurford gesture. Then back to Ethan, who is becoming a grammatical speaker before our eyes. The chapter then has a clear arc, the controversial claims are appropriately hedged, and the LLM-generated tourism prose can come out without losing anything. A generative reader who knows the Gil/Cole/Hermon controversy will respect a chapter that flags it; they'll close a chapter that doesn't.
