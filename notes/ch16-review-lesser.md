## Chapter 16: Unravelling babel

### Strengths

The Tomo-Yoko-Toronto-2003 anecdote is the chapter's heart. A toddler in a Japanese-and-English household, listening to his parents speak Japanese, turns to his grandmother and translates for her. "It wasn't just that he knew there was a difference between Japanese and English; he also knew who understood what language." That's a small story doing big work. Children sort languages by interlocutor; communication is fundamentally addressee-relative; what counts as the right code is not "English" but "this conversation between these people."

The Wiese / Inya passage is its companion. A three-year-old refuses to speak English to her British grandparents because "Daddy speaks that way" with her, and English is for Daddy. The convention that a language is bound to a person rather than to a community is exactly the kind of error that illuminates the rule, and Wiese's "com-sit" terminology (communicative situation) is doing real work for Reynolds's argument.

The vervet-monkey contrast (one-to-one signs versus contingent communication) is well-chosen and short.

The Smith (2019) showing-and-guessing framing is the right theoretical anchor for the chapter.

The Grieve (2023) quote on situational diversity ("Languages that have been adapted for a wider range of communicative contexts are more situationally complex") is a real find and a substantive provocation.

### Major concerns

The chapter is forty-seven lines, ten of which are a duplicated Wiese block-quote (it appears once inside the bullet list, once standalone). The Smith showing-and-guessing paragraph also appears twice verbatim (once in the bullet list, once after the Tomo-Yoko anecdote).

The chapter opens with a bullet list, not with prose. Thirteen bullet items that are mostly thesis-statements: "the most general communicative situation is something like 'English' or 'French' or 'Japanese,'" "what's grammatical in one may not be in another," "if two people share the context of 'English' and 'Japanese,' they can mix constructions from both in the same sentence and it will be grammatical." These need to be turned into a chapter, not a list of things the chapter could say.

The chapter's title is "Unravelling babel" but the codeswitching argument promised by the chapter file's name barely arrives. Codeswitching is mentioned (item 12 in the bullet list), but the chapter doesn't deliver examples. A trade book on grammaticality really needs to handle Spanglish, Singlish (already covered in chapter 13), and code-switching constraints (the matrix-language frame, the equivalence constraint, etc.).

The Grieve quote at the end is left without follow-through.

### Priority fixes

1. Turn the bullet list at the chapter opening into prose. As bullets, the ideas are flagged but not developed.
2. Cut one of the two duplicated Wiese block-quotes.
3. Cut one of the two duplicated Smith paragraphs.
4. Deliver on the codeswitching promise. The chapter title's filename is "16 codeswitching.tex." The book has a swearing chapter coming next; both register-switching and code-switching deserve their own examples and constraints.
5. Develop the Grieve passage. The trade-off between situational diversity and grammatical complexity is a substantive claim with real empirical content; the chapter shouldn't just quote and walk away.

### One concrete suggestion

Worst sentence: "The ultimate point of the chapter, eventually, will be that a language is simply a communicative situation." This sentence, with its "ultimate point" and "eventually," reads as a note-to-self. The chapter must arrive at its argument, not announce that it will.

Best sentence: "It wasn't just that he knew there was a difference between Japanese and English; he also knew who understood what language." A small sentence about a toddler making a sophisticated metalinguistic distinction. The structure ("not just X but Y") is a register Reynolds uses well; the content is the chapter's theoretical core in plain English. A trade reader will remember this sentence weeks later.
