## Chapter 09: Without Grammar

### Strengths

The Ethan transcript is exactly what the chapter needs at its opening. The CHILDES data is real, dated, and specific, and the back-and-forth between mother and child carries the chapter's central question: what does it look like when speech happens without grammar? Ethan's "Ball." "Baby." "That." is unforgettable. A trade reader will not need persuading that this is something different from the language they speak.

The Italian-museum example ("Il museo d'arte moderna è interessante") makes a genuine point: even adult learners can extract meaning before extracting grammar. Reynolds's coinage of "logogeny" (alongside ontogeny and phylogeny) is a writer's flourish that lands because the chapter has set up the parallelism well.

The Riau Indonesian sections, when they finally arrive, contain the chapter's core argument. Gil's quote on the lack of a grammaticalized predicate/attribute distinction in Riau Indonesian is striking. The example of \textit{makan pisang} (`eat' + `banana') being context-dependent for tense, definiteness, and even who is eating, is exactly the kind of fact that breaks an English speaker out of their assumptions.

The Japanese banana example is also useful: Reynolds is leveraging his own L2 Japanese to make English speakers feel the contingency of their grammatical apparatus.

### Major concerns

The Sumatra travelogue is overlong. Six paragraphs on Sumatra's western volcanoes, eastern lowlands, ethnic blending, religious history, and port cities, before we hear a word about Riau Indonesian. This is the AI-explainer voice at full Wikipedia pitch. The chapter is about a language; the geography is incidental.

The "Pragmatic Communication" / "Creative Language Use" sub-bulleted lists (Gestures, Onomatopoeia, Emoji, Poetry and Art, Advertising) are filler. Each item gets one phrase. None of them deepens the argument.

The pidgin / creole excursus is repeated. There's a paragraph explaining what pidgins are, then a paragraph on creoles, then a separate "\textbf{Creoles:}" bullet block that explains creoles again. The second creole explanation appears to be left over from a different draft.

The chapter trails off in mid-sentence multiple times: "John McWhorter, who cut his teeth in linguistics working on creoles." Then: "But David Gil s". Then later: "Now, returning to Riau Indonesian," followed by silence.

Then there's the Hurford block-quote at the end, which appears to be raw OCR output from a damaged scan: "condensing a mess variety, and sections of this chapter so las, creoles pagins, the B normal innate capac sign languages..." This is unreadable nonsense and must come out.

There's also AI-tic prose: "Sumatra is a land of contrasts and diversity"; "Riau Indonesian stands as a poetic affirmation of our innate ability to connect with one another"; "It's a language that speaks not just to the mind but to the soul."

### Priority fixes

1. Cut Sumatra travelogue from six paragraphs to one (or, better, none).
2. Repair the truncated McWhorter sentence and the truncated Gil sentence.
3. Cut the duplicate creole explanation.
4. Re-OCR or re-type the corrupted Hurford block-quote, or replace with a different quote.
5. Remove "It's a language that speaks not just to the mind but to the soul."
6. Trim "Pragmatic Communication" / "Creative Language Use" sub-bullets to a single paragraph or cut entirely.
7. Decide whether the chapter is about Ethan or Riau Indonesian. Right now it's both, structured as Ethan + theory + Sumatra + Riau Indonesian, but the connective tissue is missing.

### One concrete suggestion

Worst sentence (a tie): "It's a language that speaks not just to the mind but to the soul, resonating with the very essence of what it means to communicate." Pure AI register, with all the moves: "speaks not just to X but to Y," "the very essence," "what it means to." Cut.

Best sentence: "I think it's fair to say that Ethan's language is ungrammatical, just in a slightly different sense than what how we usually use the word." Even with its small typo ("than what how"), this sentence is doing exactly the work a trade book needs: distinguishing two meanings of "ungrammatical," letting the reader feel the distinction without lecturing about it. Fix the typo, keep the sentence.
