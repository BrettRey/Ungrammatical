## Overall shape: Morris's take

### What the book is currently doing structurally

There's a witness on the stand and there's a suspect, but the camera keeps wandering to the wallpaper. The witness is the asterisk. The suspect, slowly emerging across twenty-one chapters, is the linguistics profession's belief that some sentences are objectively impossible. Brett, you are not yet sure which of those two you've decided to film.

The introduction opens like a documentary should: with an object (the asterisk), a man (Schleicher), a death (tuberculosis at 47), and a small, irreducibly strange fact (Sweet repurposed his marker for unattested words to mark sentences nobody would ever utter). That is a beginning. Then chapter 01 introduces the second beat well, Ross's islands, and lets Hankamer and Postal walk on as characters who will return. So far, so Morris. By the time we reach chapter 01's section seven, though, the camera has stopped moving and we are reading an enumerated taxonomy with five levels of nested numbering. The witnesses have left the room.

From that point through chapter 19 the book proceeds as an anthology of demonstrations that grammaticality is various: morality (04), feelings (05), politics (06), the formalists (07), conformity (08), the absent grammar of toddlers and Riau Indonesian (09), conventional patterns (10), the brain (11, seven lines), historical drift (12), dialect boundaries (13), fashion (14), constructions (15), codeswitching (16), impossible languages (17), swearing (18, fifteen lines), efficiency (19), and a stub on getting it wrong (20). Chapter 21, the long-long-road piece, is a beautifully shot short film that has no idea it has been hung at the back of the museum.

### What's working at the level of shape

The asterisk doorway is alive. Schleicher's reconstructed words and Sweet's transposition of the symbol from `unattested' to `impossible' is a Morris-shaped scene, a small notational accident with consequences nobody could see at the time. Ross's island in chapter 01 is the right second witness. The Hankamer-and-Postal squib about whose, picked back up in chapter 15, is the right kind of recurring character, a thread that should be braided through the whole picture. Chapter 21 is the only chapter that fully understands what kind of film this is. It picks one tiny, eccentric construction, follows it through five languages and a thousand years of English, and lets the reader see something the speakers in the story can't see, that what feels inevitable is the residue of accident.

The personal threading, when it appears, also works in the documentary register: Tokyo bookstores, Humber FANBOYS, the bilingual son surprising his English-only grandmother, the kitchen-table shock of overhearing your three-year-old translate. Those are interview-room moments. They lower the camera.

### What's not working at the level of shape

There is no protagonist and no antagonist. The book is full of grammaticality but empty of grammarians as people. Schleicher dies on page two and we never meet Ross beyond his age, Hankamer and Postal stay flat as their squib, Chomsky is mentioned without ever being filmed, and the voices in the long sociological middle (04-08) are mostly disembodied frameworks: utilitarianism, deontology, contractualism, conformism, repression. A film with no faces is a slideshow. The HPC view, which is the protagonist's argument, has no antagonist on screen. The essentialist who believes there is a fact of the matter about whether *He offered her the steak fiendishly hungry* is grammatical never gets cross-examined.

There is no turn. Documentary needs a moment when the witness reveals more than they meant to, when McNamara's face changes, when the asterisk slips. The book offers no such moment. The model in 01 §7 is not a turn, it's an information dump that arrives too early to feel like revelation. Chapter 15's relative-whose payoff (the beautiful piece where Brett finds GPT-4 rating it a 6 and works out what Hankamer and Postal couldn't see) IS a McNamara-revealing-himself moment, the linguists' own intuitions caught failing in real time, but it's buried 600 lines into a long chapter and never staged as such.

There is no confrontation. The chapters on morality, politics, fashion, swearing, codeswitching are introduced as analogues to grammaticality, but they never get into the room together. They take turns testifying. Each one's contribution to the homeostatic-property-cluster case is asserted, never built.

The middle is a jury that won't deliberate. By chapter 13 the reader has heard the same point made in nine different domains, and the chapters cumulate by addition, not by argument. The reader needs to feel the case closing, the noose tightening on the essentialist account. They feel only the parade getting longer.

The end has no verdict. Chapter 19 (efficiency) sounds like a closing argument. Chapter 21 plays like a closing parable. Between them sits chapter 20, a twenty-five-line stub repeating chapter 15's whose material. The film cuts to black with the projectionist still loading reels.

### What would change the shape for the better

Make it a documentary. Specifically:

**Cast a protagonist and an antagonist on screen.** The protagonist is the working linguist who marked an asterisk and now isn't sure why. Brett is the obvious candidate; the personal sections suggest he's willing. The antagonist is the essentialist, embodied. Use Postal. Use Chomsky. Use the Cambridge grammar's editors marking that whose with a star. Quote them. Show the moment they decided. Make them characters, not citations.

**Promote chapter 21 to chapter 1.** Lesser and McCloskey will both have told you this. They're right. Long long road is a Morris cold open. Open on the construction. Open on the international students laughing at *the road is long long*. Then, only then, walk back to Schleicher and the asterisk. Asterisk-history becomes the second-act orientation, not the first.

**Move the model out of chapter 01 §7 and to where parables put their morals: late, quiet, after the case is built.** Possibly an appendix. The trade reader doesn't need the eleven-point taxonomy to follow the argument; the academic reader will find it. Right now the book stops being a documentary and becomes a manifesto exactly here. Cut, and don't restore until you've earned it.

**Brett's whose paper IS the McNamara moment. Stage it.** Build a chapter, not a buried section, around the linguists' own grammaticality intuitions failing. Hankamer in 1973, Postal in 1973, Cambridge grammar in 2002, Cinque in 2020, all marking *someone whose had left* with a star. GPT-4 in 2024 calls it a 6 out of 7. Then Brett finds the construction in the wild. That's a turn. That's the moment readers see what the people inside the story couldn't. Fold chapter 20's stub into this; this is the chapter chapter 20 wants to be.

**Combine and confront.** Merge 04 (morality) and 06 (politics) into one chapter that puts moral intuition and grammatical intuition in the same interrogation room and asks whether they really have the same shape. Currently the analogy is asserted, not tested. Merge 09 (no grammar), 13 (varieties), 16 (codeswitching) into one chapter on where grammar starts and where it stops. Currently they take turns saying the same thing in different rooms.

**Cut to fourteen chapters.** Stubs (11 neurolinguistics, 18 swearing, 20 whose) either get promoted to real chapters or get folded into the chapters whose work they were meant to do.

**Order:** open with long-long (current 21). Asterisk and Schleicher (current 00). Islands and the boundary phenomena that started it all (current 01, minus the model). Without grammar / Riau / dialect / codeswitching / impossible languages, as one tightening sequence on what counts as a grammar at all. Generative grammar history as the antagonist's testimony. Becoming ungrammatical / efficiency / real patterns, the mechanism case. The grammarians' intuitions failing (whose). Then the social-judgment chapter (morality+politics+fashion+swearing). Then the model. Then a real closing that names the verdict.

### The one structural move

Stage the linguists being wrong about whose as the centerpiece of the book.

Right now Brett buries his strongest scene halfway through chapter 15 and reduces it to a stub at chapter 20. That scene is the only place in the manuscript where the people inside the story are caught not seeing what the camera sees. Hankamer and Postal mark a sentence with a star in 1973. The Cambridge grammar repeats them in 2002. Cinque calls it a residual puzzle in 2020. GPT-4 rates it 6/7 in 2024. Brett finds it in the wild. That sequence, told slowly, is the whole book in one chapter, because every claim the book is trying to make about grammaticality, that intuition is fallible, that judgments are conventional, that the asterisk hides a question rather than answers one, lives inside it.

Build the book around that scene. Open with the long-long parable. Spend the middle constructing the case (the homeostatic cluster, the historical contingency, the conventionality, the sociological domains). Then put the whose chapter where the turn belongs, two-thirds in. Let the reader watch the field's most respected syntacticians fail to see something a chatbot and a Humber College professor can. Then close with the asterisk and ask what it has been doing all along.

That's a film. The current manuscript is research notes for one.
