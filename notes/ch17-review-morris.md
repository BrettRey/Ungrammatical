## Chapter 17: Impossible languages

### Strengths

The cleanest move in the chapter is the perturbation series: an English sentence (*He cleans all the books on his very messy bookshelf*) progressively scrambled by even-odd reordering, by partial reverse, by tense-hop. The reader can feel the ground tilt as they go through. The Kallini 2024 figure showing an impossibility continuum of these perturbations is a smart visual aid that does heavy lifting.

The Sampson quotation is beautifully chosen. He picks the prime-number-length language and the even-occurrence language as cases that "could never occur as a natural language" (in 1975), then shows that transformational grammar in fact \textit{can} define them, and concludes that Chomsky's notation imposes a complexity ordering rather than a possibility-impossibility partition. That's a real argument from inside the formalist literature, fairly cited, doing the chapter's work.

The Bishop John Wilkins detail (1668's "An Essay towards a Real Character and a Philosophical Language" with its tree-structured ontology, and Okrent's verdict "when you speak in concepts, it's too damn hard to say anything") is a perfect trade-book aside. It connects the chapter to a long human history of trying to construct a perfect language and failing for human reasons.

### Major concerns

The chapter has two openings. The first (lines 1-34) is a working timeline and bullet list (Languages given by gods, UG evolves, humans evolve language; magical spells, languages of gods, Borges, Wilkins). The second (line 35) is a clean `\chapter{Impossible languages}` start. This is two false starts coexisting in one file. The bullet list should be moved to NOTES.md or developed.

The Andrea Moro book (*Impossible Languages*, MIT 2016) is the canonical contemporary treatment and the chapter cites it in the bullet list but doesn't engage. Moro is on the advisory board list as the formalist-position steelman. This chapter is the place to engage him directly. Right now he gets three quotation marks and a citation.

Borges's "Tlön, Uqbar, Orbis Tertius" is the chapter's missing literary anchor. The fictional language families of Tlön are exactly the kind of thought-experiment-impossible-language a trade-book reader would love. The bullet mentions Borges; the chapter never uses him. There's a film here: a Borgesian library, an encyclopedia of an imaginary world, a language that has only verbs in one hemisphere and only adjectives in the other.

The "What could this be for?" line at line 49 (Moro's "place no after the third word; first article must agree with last noun; closed interrogatives invert order") is one of the chapter's best asides. Reynolds is putting his finger on the hidden assumption in the impossible-languages literature: even when you can construct the rule formally, no human community would ever evolve into using it because no functional pressure would push them there. That's the chapter's HPC point in one line. But the line is alone, undeveloped.

There's no engagement here with sign-language emergence (Sandler's ABSL) or homesign or Nicaraguan Sign Language, all of which are the closest thing to actually-emerging-grammars we have in the modern era. Sandler is on the advisory board list specifically for this chapter and chapter 13.

On the protagonists question: this chapter has Chomsky (who claimed there are languages that could not exist), Sampson (who showed the formalism doesn't actually define them), Moro (who tried to redeem the program), and a brief Borges-shaped shadow. They are all named. None of them is in the room. The reader doesn't know what the stakes are for any of them, what their personalities were, or why this debate has gone on for sixty years.

### Priority fixes

1. Move the front-of-file timeline and bullet list to NOTES.md or develop into prose.
2. Engage Moro substantively. He is the most articulate contemporary defender of the impossible-language program.
3. Develop the Borges thought experiment.
4. Bring in Sandler / ABSL / Nicaraguan Sign Language as the empirical counterweight to the formalist thought experiments.
5. Mark stub status in STATUS.md.

### One concrete suggestion

Open with Bishop Wilkins in 1668. A clergyman trying to invent a "real character and a philosophical language" by classifying every concept into a hierarchical tree. The book is dedicated to the Royal Society. Wilkins truly believes that if humans speak in concepts arranged this way, the world's confusions will resolve. He fails. Why? Because, as Okrent says, "when you speak in concepts, it's too damn hard to say anything." Then leap forward: the impossible-languages program in modern linguistics is asking the same question from the other side. Not "what would an ideal language look like?" but "what would no language ever look like?" Sampson, then Moro. End with sign languages emerging in real time in real communities, doing the empirical work the thought experiments cannot. The chapter has its bones; what it needs is one specific human at each end of the historical arc.
