## Chapter 06: Prescriptivism on the right and on the left

### Strengths
The chapter's basic move — that prescriptivism comes in left and right flavours, and that both the gender-neutralizing English project and the gender-marking German project are forms of social linguistic engineering — is a good corrective. Most readers, especially academic ones, will be carrying the unstated assumption that descriptivism is on the political left and prescriptivism is on the right. Showing that prescriptivism cuts across politics is the right kind of de-tribing move for this book. The Susan Neiman example is a real find: a generative reader who walks in expecting the book to side with one political camp will be jolted (productively) when both camps appear with their language-policing tendencies in view.

The Nunberg quote — racists don't use slurs because they're derogative; slurs are derogative because racists use them — is a genuinely good piece of citation. It does serious work: it dignifies the reader's intuition that some words really are bad while locating the badness socially rather than semantically. That's the kind of careful sentence the chapter needs more of.

The blindness example, with the CFB position, is also good. It's the kind of case that resists the easy left-right frame and forces the reader to think.

### Major concerns
At 41 lines, this is a stub. What's here is interesting but it doesn't develop. There's no argumentative arc. We get an opening on gender-neutralizing English, a flip to gender-marking German, a quick Nunberg, a quick blindness example, a brief metaphorical-extension passage, and then notes about Beaver and Stanley and Schiller's "Good Vibrations" framework that haven't been integrated. The Beaver and Stanley discussion is presented as if it were dialogue between an author and a critic ("You propose...", "If your multi-factorial approach is to..."). That's clearly a chunk of LLM-generated commentary copied in, not chapter prose.

More substantively, the chapter has a politically tricky brief — accusing both left and right of policing language — and right now, the left side gets harder treatment than the right. The English-feminist gender-neutralizing project gets a paragraph, the German feminist gender-marking project gets a paragraph (sympathetic), the Nunberg slurs paragraph leans toward the conservative side of the slur question, and the metaphorical-extension passage explicitly reframes a "woke program" as making the word *blind* unusable. There's nothing wrong with any single move, but cumulatively, a left-leaning reader will close the chapter feeling scolded; a right-leaning reader will feel agreed with. That asymmetry is a problem given the chapter's stated even-handedness.

The Becker question to ask is: which reader is being given a place to stand? Currently the chapter privileges the reader who already thinks the language-of-disability project is silly. That's a tribal-membership move. A book about HPC categories and grammaticality should not align itself with one camp's view of disability terminology in passing.

The chapter also doesn't cite or engage actual generative work on slurs, which is a significant philosophy-of-language literature now (Anderson and Lepore, Camp, Hom, Jeshion). A reader who knows that field will notice the absence.

### Priority fixes
- Finish the chapter. As written it's three pages, not a chapter.
- Strip out the LLM-dialogue passage about Beaver and Stanley. Either incorporate their framework as cited prose, or drop it.
- Make the symmetry actually symmetric. If the chapter is going to argue both sides do this, give a fully developed example from each side, with charity to each. Right now the German-feminist case is the one example where you describe a left-aligned project as productive; it's an outlier in a chapter that mostly catalogues left-side overreach.
- The metaphorical-extension passage on *blind* needs more care. The argument that we shouldn't restrict metaphorical uses isn't trivial; it touches debates about reclamation, semantic narrowing, and disability politics that deserve a hearing.
- Source-grounding: the Cameron, Lakoff, Miller, Spender, Baron, Maggio, Diewald, Vervecken citations in lines 2-8 are stacked plausibly but need to be checked. They look like LLM generation of plausible-sounding bibliography.

### One concrete suggestion
The Nunberg quote is the chapter's gold. Build around it. Open with the Nunberg observation: that the badness of a word lives in who uses it, not in what it denotes. Then ask: who, then, are the language police on either side? The English feminist project tries to remove gendered occupational nouns; the German feminist project insists on them. The disability-language reformers try to ban metaphorical *blind*; some blind activists keep it. Each project is making a guess about who uses words, and what the use does. Then ask the harder question: should grammar (the syntactic-morphological category) be policeable in this way at all, and if so by whom? The chapter's natural argument is that grammar in the strict sense isn't policeable but vocabulary is, which is exactly where HPC-style thinking pays dividends. Right now the chapter bumps around between grammar and lexis without flagging the difference. Generative readers will notice.
