## Chapter 03: Being grammatical isn't always enough

### Strengths

The chapter is built around real examples that mostly do their job. The "you still at the gym?" / "I still at the gym" exchange is the kind of texture only a writer-linguist can supply, and it does the work of two pages of explanation. The Henk-and-Trouble passive from Richard Osman is a genuine find, and Pullum's contribution to the chapter (the citation-via-anecdote, "my friend and co-author shared with me") would be charming if the surrounding paragraph weren't trying to be a Buzzfeed article.

The Pirahã and Riau Indonesian sections are valuable. The distinction Reynolds draws (Riau speakers can't say it; Pirahã speakers won't talk about it) is a real distinction and a memorable one for a trade reader. The "we don't talk about that" framing is the kind of small, true observation that justifies the book's wandering scope.

The Jeff-Brown-and-Nietzsche anecdote is splendid. A philosophy professor warns against the passive; the very book he assigns has a passive in the second paragraph. That's the chapter's real argument compressed to ten lines.

### Major concerns

The chapter is closer to a working draft than the others. Three section heads exist with no body or only fragmentary content: `Syntactic Satiation` is empty; `Pragmatics` is just a quotation; multiple sentences trail off mid-thought ("But membership in ", "[additional examples needed]", "[is that really why?]", "Why, then, can they be so bad?" answered only with a stub).

The "1970s, when bell-bottoms were groovy and disco was king" passage is the worst stretch in the manuscript so far. "They didn't know much about how context affects language use." "Faster than you could say 'Stayin' Alive.'" "Don't worry. All you need to know is..." This is podcast-host register dropped into a Language Science Press book, and it grates against everything good around it. It also condescends to the reader.

The Anne Carson section has a great setup (Reynolds working through what he won't bore us with: Shakespeare, hip-hop, Simpsons writers) and then arrives at Carson and stops. The chapter promises a discussion of artful rule-breaking and never delivers.

The list of yes/no inversion examples (seven `\ea` blocks) is too long for the small point it makes. Three would suffice.

### Priority fixes

1. Fill in Syntactic Satiation, Pragmatics, and the truncated intransitive-verbs passage, or move them to NOTES.md until they're ready. As stubs they damage the chapter.
2. Cut the bell-bottoms-and-disco passage entirely. Rewrite the Henk-and-Trouble setup in the book's actual voice.
3. Trim the inversion examples from seven to three.
4. Decide what the chapter is. Right now it's "Being grammatical isn't enough" + "Conventions break, sometimes well" + "Old syntactic rules are sometimes pragmatic rules" + "Group membership matters." Two of those threads are buried.
5. Replace "[is this true]?" "[is that really why?]" with answers or cuts.

### One concrete suggestion

Worst sentence: "Back in the 1970s, when bell-bottoms were groovy and disco was king, linguists had some funny ideas about grammar." Cut. The whole bell-bottoms paragraph. Replace with: "Until the 1970s, linguists treated information-packaging effects as syntactic rules. Take this pair." Then go straight to the Jack-and-recycling examples.

Best sentence: "It's not that these things can't be expressed -- though there \textit{are} propositions that are simply not entertained in some languages." That parenthetical does an enormous amount of work in a small space. It signals that the question of what languages can and can't say isn't going to be answered glibly, and it earns the reader's trust for the harder argument coming. The italicized \textit{are} is also doing real work, exactly the kind of typographic touch a Lesser-edited essay would keep.
