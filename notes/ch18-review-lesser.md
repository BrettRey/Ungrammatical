## Chapter 18: Swearing

### Note on the chapter's stub status

This is a stub. Fifteen lines, no chapter title in the file, two unattributed Margaret Atwood quotations (from \textit{The Handmaid's Tale}), and four paragraphs that gesture at an argument without developing it. The review must therefore be partly prospective: what should belong here?

### Strengths of what's here

The provocation is real and one of the book's most interesting questions: "Is swearing a norm violation because it is obscene, like a couple lustily copulating in a McDonalds booth or a pile of shit on the floor of an elevator, or because it is ungrammatical?" That is exactly the question the book's HPC framework is positioned to answer. If grammaticality is a homeostatic property cluster that includes social-conformity and community-recognition properties alongside formal ones, then swearing-out-of-context ought to fit the pattern.

The "secret language hidden within our own" framing (swearing as a register with its own grammar, fluency, and in-group conventions) is the right metaphor and connects to the book's argument that what counts as ungrammatical depends on the community in question.

The fluency observation ("Those within the circle have mastered this rebellious grammar. They hurl a curse with casual precision, knowing exactly when it adds emphasis or earns knowing laughter") is sharp.

### What belongs here that isn't yet

- Linguistic-data swearing literature. Geoff Nunberg's "racists don't use slurs because they're derogative; slurs are derogative because they're the words that racists use" already appears in chapter 6 but belongs more naturally here. The Beaver and Stanley work on "vibrations" mentioned at the end of chapter 6 belongs here too.

- The emerging swearing-grammar literature. There is a genuine syntax of expletive infixation ("abso-fuckin-lutely") with constraints (the infix has to land before a stressed syllable). This is the kind of rule a trade reader will love because they will recognize their own intuitions. Other examples: the prosodic constraints, the syntactic positions where swears can appear, the difference between adjectival "fucking" and intensifier "fucking."

- The contrast between "fuck" as expletive and "fuck" as actual semantic content. "What the fuck?" versus "We fucked." The same morpheme behaves like different categories in different constructions.

- The cross-linguistic dimension. Italian uses different body parts; French uses different relatives; Japanese has different swearing strategies altogether. The HPC point is that the cluster of properties that makes a word a swear is loosely held together: profanity, taboo, in-group fluency, register-coding, syntactic flexibility.

- The Doctorow "enshittification" story from chapter 14 is in dialogue with this chapter and could be cross-referenced.

- The intonational and prosodic dimensions. Swearing has a stress and rhythm that matters; getting it wrong reads as L2 or as ungrammatical, depending on context.

### Priority fixes

1. Write the chapter.
2. Choose one anchor anecdote. The book has worked from anecdotes throughout; this chapter needs one too. Maybe the first time Reynolds heard his daughter swear, or the first time he caught himself swearing in front of his mother, or a swearing-in-context observation from a context he knows well (the EFL classroom, a Toronto college).
3. Engage the expletive-infixation rule directly. It's a phonological constraint with syntactic consequences, and it's easy to demonstrate. "Abso-bloody-lutely" works because BLOO is the heavy syllable; "ab-bloody-solutely" doesn't.
4. Tie to the rest of the book. The swearing chapter should land the HPC argument: swearing is grammatical when the community recognizes it, ungrammatical when it doesn't, in the same way that "ain't" is grammatical for one community and not another. The chapter is positioned to do this work; it just needs to do it.
5. Cut or attribute the Atwood quotes. They are quoted without source.

### One concrete suggestion

There is no chapter to evaluate at the sentence level. The single sentence worth quoting is: "Imagine swearing isn't about the shock value of certain words, but instead exists as a secret language hidden within our own." Build out from this. Open the chapter with a paragraph in which Reynolds describes the moment he realized that fluent swearing is its own competence. Then ask: what makes someone a fluent swearer? The answer is: knowing the grammar.

The chapter's title is "Swearing." A trade reader will pick this chapter up out of curiosity. Reward them with a chapter that delivers on the implicit promise: the grammar of how to swear well.
