## Chapter 03: Being grammatical isn't always enough

### Strengths
The chapter's central observation, that perfectly grammatical strings can still be wrong (the *I have 30 years* / *presser 2* pair), is exactly the right move at this point in the book. You're showing the reader that grammaticality and acceptability come apart, and you're doing it with conventionalization data that almost no one disputes. That's a friendly opening for any reader. The Riau Indonesian example is also strategically smart: you're using one of the field's most-discussed cases of cross-linguistic grammatical surprise, and you're using it to make a point that supports rather than undercuts the discoverers of the constraint. A generativist reader can stay with you here.

The Geoff Pullum / Richard Osman *by him* example is real gold. The 1970s syntactic rule banning a passive *by*-phrase from referring to the matrix subject was canonical generative grammar. Showing that it crumbles in a real novel is the kind of empirical move that respects the field's own evidential standards. That's how you persuade an opponent: not by switching the rules of evidence but by playing on their pitch and winning.

The texted-with-wife example (*you still at the gym?*) is the chapter's other gem. The reader feels they've learned something, the prose has Brett's voice in it, and the linguistic point lands. Keep more of this kind of thing.

### Major concerns
The chapter is currently a stub plus drafting notes ("[is this true]?", "[etc]", "[additional examples needed]", an unfinished sentence at line 149). I'm reviewing intentions as much as text. Where the prose is finished, two tonal moves worry me.

First, the "1970s linguists with bell-bottoms" frame. That's the chapter's most overt instance of attacking the tribe. A generativist will read it as cheap. The argument doesn't need it. The Pullum/Osman example does the work; the disco joke just signals "I think they were silly," which is the opposite of the Becker move. You can disagree with 1970s syntactic theorizing while taking it seriously.

Second, the "passive haters" passage. Currently it's a swipe at a strawman (anonymous "writers", a dim philosophy professor) that doesn't actually advance the chapter's argument about grammaticality versus convention. It risks confirming the suspicion that this book is going to be more polemical than analytic. Consider whether the passive belongs in this chapter at all; it might fit better in chapter 4 (morality) or in the politics chapter, where the moralizing about language use is the actual subject.

The "[is this true]?" about Orwell is exactly the kind of place to use the Source Grounding rule. Don't write the sentence until you've checked. (You haven't yet, which is fine, it's a draft.)

The Pirahã passage at line 75 is risky. Everett's claims about Pirahã are some of the most contested in the field, and a parenthetical wave at "we don't talk about that" is going to make a lot of generativists distrust everything else in the chapter. Either commit and engage the controversy properly, or cut.

### Priority fixes
- Cut the "Stayin' Alive" / disco frame. Replace with a sentence that takes the 1970s claim seriously, then breaks it with Osman.
- Decide what the passives section is doing. If it's about prescriptive moralizing, route it into the morality chapter.
- Either resolve the Pirahã reference or remove it; a one-sentence wave is the worst option.
- Finish the satiation subsection (currently empty). Satiation is the linchpin of your argument that ungrammaticality isn't binary; if it's missing here, it'd better be present somewhere else.
- Fill or remove the "[is this true]?" Orwell line; nothing reads worse to a hostile reader than a marker that the author didn't bother to check.

### One concrete suggestion
Reframe the chapter's argument as a positive claim, not a critique. The current architecture is "linguists used to think X, but actually Y," which positions the reader as a member of a dim past you've now corrected. That posture loses generativists, who hear themselves implicated. Try instead: "even when the syntax is fine, communities have conventions about how to say things; here are six places that matters." Then you can use the Osman example, the *I have 30 years* idiom, the Riau Indonesian relative clauses, the brewer/lifter intransitives, the 1970s passive *by*-rule, all positive cases of conventionalization. The reader builds a picture rather than scoring points against an unnamed past. The argument is the same. The reader-relationship is much better.
