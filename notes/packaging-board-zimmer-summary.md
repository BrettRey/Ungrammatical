# *(Un)grammatical*: Cross-chapter review summary (Zimmer perspective)

## Where the book is consistently vivid

The book is at its best when a working linguist is on the page following a real lead. The *whose* investigation (chs 5 and 15), the *probable to play* discovery (ch 10), the Caxton 1481 *moche fair* passage (ch 12), the Putnam-and-O'Hern 1955 D.C. alley study (ch 13), the Cory Doctorow email exchange (ch 14), the Tomo-in-the-kitchen scene (ch 16), the Geoff Pullum email about the *Rubymar* (ch 15), the international students laughing at *the road is long long* (ch 21), the Hancock anecdote about Miles Davis turning a wrong chord right (ch 1) - these are the passages a science-writing reviewer underlines. They share a structure: a person is in a room, holding a question, finding evidence, registering surprise. They are also disproportionately the author's own first-person material, drawn from teaching, family, correspondence, and corpus searches he actually ran.

## Where the book consistently floats

The chapters drift abstract whenever the author hands the keyboard to a generated frame. The Parfit-mountain ethical-frameworks survey in chapter 4 (which appears twice in succession), the Lisa Feldman Barrett constructed-emotion subsections in chapter 5 (also doubled), the cross-linguistic *whose* "world tour" in chapter 15, the Beaver-and-Stanley conversational paste in chapter 6, the closing "we are all witnesses to the never-ending dance of language" peroration in chapter 12 - these are passages where a reader can feel the text turning into a paraphrase of a paraphrase, with no scene, no specific person, and no testable claim. Several chapters end this way; several open this way; chapter 4 is almost entirely this way. Cutting these passages is the single largest editorial intervention the manuscript needs, and would shorten the book by 15-20 percent.

## The structural pattern

The book has roughly twelve genuinely vivid set pieces and roughly the same number of LLM-padded longueurs. The vivid material can carry the book; the padded material is dragging it down. Stub chapters (11 neurolinguistics, 18 swearing, 20 getting it wrong) are easier to fix than the over-padded ones - they need to be filled in with the kind of physically anchored material the author already knows how to write.

## The single most useful repair

**Replace every abstract framework section with the working-linguist version of the same idea.** The book has the natural science-writing structure already - in the *probable to play* corpus search, the *whose* email-and-friends-and-family-and-Wikipedia investigation, the Pullum *Rubymar* exchange, the Putnam-and-O'Hern alleys. These are the chapters' centers of gravity. The Parfit metaphor, the Barrett summary, the seven-ethical-frameworks list, and the various "in conclusion, language is dynamic and evolving" passages are interchangeable filler that any reader has read elsewhere. If the author trusts his own work the way the reader does, the framework-sections become unnecessary, and the book becomes a 250-page Carl Zimmer-style exploration of a working linguist's questions. That is the book that's hiding inside the current draft.
