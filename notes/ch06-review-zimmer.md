## Chapter 06: Prescriptivism on the right and on the left

### Strengths

The German vs. English contrast on gender-marking is a genuinely interesting puzzle. English feminists fought for *flight attendant* over *stewardess*; German feminists fight for the *Lehrer/Lehrerinnen* split because making women linguistically visible has the opposite politics in that grammatical landscape. That comparison alone is the chapter's strongest move and could carry the whole.

Nunberg's reversal - "racists don't use slurs because they're derogative; slurs are derogative because they're the words that racists use" - is the right kind of compact intellectual gem. The author's adaptation of it to *blind* (bigots make a word derogatory by using it) is good thinking.

The footnote about identity-first language and the Canadian Federation of the Blind reframing blindness as "characteristic, not handicap" is the kind of organizational, archival detail that makes a political claim vivid.

### Major concerns

This is a stub. Total length is 41 short paragraphs, several of which are notes ("In 'Good Vibrations' Henry Schiller talks about Beaver and Stanley's ideas"), Beaver-and-Stanley discussion that reads like a chat-window paste, and an unfinished section on metaphorical extensions ending mid-thought.

The opening paragraphs - "It will be news to nobody," "What may come as a surprise to many" - rely on framing rather than scene. The 1960s-70s feminist movement gets cited (Lakoff 1973, Miller 1976, Spender 1980) but the actual claims are made entirely in summary. We never see a particular activist, a particular newspaper headline, a particular style guide change, a particular legal-or-employment consequence.

The middle section pivots to the "use vs. mention" distinction with *fucker* but trails off before anchoring it to anything specific.

The Beaver and Stanley material at the end appears to have been pasted from another conversation (notice the bullet-list-with-bold-clauses formatting and the "you propose" / "this lines up with your notion" voice that addresses the author rather than the reader).

The chapter has no real cases. Where is the substitute teacher fired for code-switching? The school district debate over *whom*? The Académie française ruling on *iel*? The Canadian census changes? These exist; the chapter doesn't use them.

The "left polices words / right polices grammar" thesis, which is the chapter's announced through-line, is gestured at but never argued with cases. It's also unclear and possibly wrong: prescriptivism on grammar is found across the political spectrum.

### Priority fixes

2. Replace the Beaver/Stanley conversational paste with a written paragraph drawing on their published work (and verify the citation).
3. Find one or two real-world episodes where a grammaticality call had political consequences. The Académie française's 2017 *iel* dictionary inclusion. The 2020 American Dialect Society word-of-the-year for singular *they* (announced 2016). The CRT-era Florida statute language. Something concrete.
4. The unfinished sentence "the blind and deaf communities" needs completing or cutting.

### One concrete suggestion

For the German contrast, find the actual 2014 Hannover University guideline that introduced gender-star spellings (*Studierende*, *Lehrer\*innen*) and the political reception it got. There were court cases. There were tabloid columns. There was a 2023 Bundestag debate. A reader who watches a German parliamentarian thunder about *Lehrer\*innen* on the floor will understand what's at stake more vividly than reading "the rationale behind this approach is that, by using gender-coded nouns, the German language can more accurately represent..." The chapter argues; it should report.
