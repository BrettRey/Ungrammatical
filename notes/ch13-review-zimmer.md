## Chapter 13: Across language boundaries

### Strengths

The 1955 Putnam-and-O'Hern study at Catholic University - two Catholic graduate students (Father George Putnam, Sister Rosina O'Hern) running phonetic and spectrographic analysis on 74 of 88 known adult African American residents of D.C.'s "inhabited alleys," recording readings of *Aesop's Fables*, then playing twelve speakers' recordings to 70 listeners and finding social-status rankings tracking actual social status with high correlation - is a small documentary marvel. The methodological caveat from Labov in the footnote is honest. This is the chapter's centerpiece and it deserves to be.

The four-way analysis of *Ain't nobody come to help us* vs. *Nobody has come to help us* (the first two words alone differ in *have* vs. *be*, in the polarity of negation, in the verb form, and in the position of subject) is the kind of patient structural break-out that lets a reader see why dialects diverge in deep rather than surface ways.

The BIN-living example from Spears - *they've BIN living in Chicago* meaning specifically "for a long time," camouflaged inside what looks like Standard *they've been living* - is one of the best illustrations of why some non-Standard forms hide in plain sight. The Spears block quote on language policing within Black schools, by students and not just teachers, is hard-hitting and specific.

The Cantone 2009 finding on bilingual German-Italian adjective placement (*una glückliche Frau* works, *i verdi Augen* doesn't) is small and concrete.

### Major concerns

The opening pages are generic ("we speak in many ways," "academic language for scholarly articles," "literary English showcases a broad spectrum") and read as a list of registers rather than an argument. Pages on this material can be cut or compressed to a paragraph.

The Bill Withers reference - "ain't no sunshine when she's gone" - is invoked but the analysis is one sentence: "even though its grammatical structures might not be ones we typically use." A song about the mood of grammatical absence deserves more than a passing nod. What does *ain't no sunshine* do that *there's no sunshine* doesn't? The chapter would gain from naming it.

The "Microdialects" section opens with the rich Putnam-and-O'Hern study but the section before it ("Gendered language") is a stub: a list of eight gender-coded sentences and the question "is your feeling upon hearing a masculine person say *Oh my gosh!* fundamentally the same as when you hear an Anglophone say *I no like that*?" The question is good, the answer is unattempted.

The Singlish examples (*Wah lau! This guy Singlish si beh hiong sia*) are taken from Wikipedia and are ungrammatical for the wrong reason - we don't know what the words mean. A reader can't judge what's grammatical in a system whose lexicon is opaque to them.

The Balinese subsection is one bullet list of six terms (Intentionality, Clarity, Familiarity, Group attitudes towards novelty, Ideas of propriety, Status maintenance) with no surrounding text. A placeholder.

The Matt Shirley Venn diagram on contextual factors is referenced but the figure is described, not analyzed. A reader who can't see the figure has no anchor.

### Priority fixes

1. Compress the opening pages on registers to one paragraph. The chapter doesn't need a register tour.
2. Develop or cut the Singlish examples. If you keep them, gloss them.
3. Develop or cut the Balinese list.
4. The gendered-language section needs a thesis. The question posed is good; answer it (or pose it more carefully).
5. The Putnam-and-O'Hern study should be reframed as the chapter's centerpiece, not its closing example.

### One concrete suggestion

The 1950s alley-dweller study has the feel of a piece of oral history that's being lost. The participants were recorded in the very neighborhoods that were demolished in D.C. urban renewal. There may be archival audio at the Library of Congress or in the AAA. Even if not, a paragraph on what those alleys were like - the wooden tenements, the proximity-but-isolation from white-collar Washington, the demographic specifics - would help a reader understand what microdialect difference *did* in the social geography of mid-century D.C. and why the listeners' status-rankings worked. Right now Putnam and O'Hern are heroic data-collectors, but the people they recorded are abstractions. Bring one of them - even from a single interview transcript - into the chapter.
