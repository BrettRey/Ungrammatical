## Chapter 05: How grammar feels (intuitions)

### Strengths
The "Whose gorilla?" story is the chapter's, and possibly the book's, best research narrative. Hankamer and Postal's squib leads to your Wikipedia editing, which leads to LLM probing, which leads to OED-checking, which leads to your own paper. That arc is what reflective equilibrium actually feels like in a working linguist's life. It is unfaked. The Mia/Sasha singular *they* anecdote earns the abstract point about ballistic processing. The opening list of sensations (sand in your shoe, a branch, an ice-cream headache) is a real moment of voice; it sets the metacognitive-feeling theme without lecturing.

The Pullum biography (lines 202-203) is briefer and more affectionate than the Schleicher/Sweet/Bentham passages elsewhere; it works because it's specific (high school dropout, piano in a band, two of the biggest UK albums of the 60s).

### Major concerns
This chapter is two chapters wedged together, possibly three. There is a clear duplicate of section 7.4 ("Reinterpreting grammatical intuitions through a constructionist lens" appears verbatim twice, lines 701-715 and 717-731). There is also a duplicate framing of section 4 ("What does (un)grammaticality feel like 1?" and "What does ungrammaticality feel like 2?" with overlapping Proust passages and metacognitive-feeling material).

Two whole sections feel grafted from another draft and don't earn their length: section 7 (Barrett's Theory of Constructed Emotion), and section 8 (A neuroscientific perspective). They reach the same conclusion ("our experience is constructed") via different vocabularies, then say it three more times. The Bayes-theorem walkthrough at lines 538-585 is genuinely embarrassing: you make up probabilities ($P(A_1) = 1.0$ already breaks probability axioms; the calculation is performative not informative). A non-linguist will see numbers conjured from nowhere and think the chapter doesn't trust them. A linguist will see arithmetic that doesn't compute and lose trust. **This passage damages credibility on every front.**

The "Free adjuncts, danglers, and howlers" section drops Donaldson into a paragraph and then never quite finishes the argument; the *50 Shades* example is good but the chapter doesn't decide whether danglers are ungrammatical or merely bad-mannered.

The chapter also contains a literal LLM dump at the end (lines 858-899). "The following is from chatGPT" introducing seven hundred words of vector-space speculation about emotions is a notes-file paste left in the manuscript. **Cut immediately.**

There are typos and breakage: "perceives" becomes "percieves" at line 35; line 230 has a stray instruction "the post/sentence deserves the weight"; "Geoff Pullum has a suggestions" at line 201; "this error feel" at line 534 is the in-prose grammatical-violation example, but the surrounding text doesn't quite cue the reader.

### Priority fixes
- Pick **one** version of the constructionist section and delete the other.
- Pick one version of "what ungrammaticality feels like" (the predictive-processing version is cleaner) and delete the other.
- **Cut the entire Bayes-theorem section.** Replace with one paragraph: predictive processing predicts what's coming; mismatch produces a felt sense of wrongness scaled to surprise.
- **Cut the ChatGPT dump** at the end of the chapter.
- Cut the Barrett/Berridge sections to one paragraph each, or cut entirely; predictive processing already does the work.
- The whose-gorilla section can be the spine; everything else either supports it or goes.

### One concrete suggestion
Build the chapter around three felt experiences in sequence: (1) The Hankamer-Postal *whose* (your discovery; experts vs. natives vs. LLMs); (2) The 50 Shades dangler (felt wrongness without rule violation); (3) Singular *they* with named referents (felt wrongness against your own theoretical commitments). Then a single short section on why feelings of wrongness are graded (predictive processing, no Bayes arithmetic). End with reflective equilibrium as the takeaway: even linguists must sometimes update against their own intuitions. Right now the chapter says this thing four times in four vocabularies (Bayes, Barrett, Berridge, predictive processing). One vocabulary is enough; the others can become a single closing paragraph saying "neuroscience converges on the same picture from several directions."

### Rhetoric that works
"There's nothing I can do do stop it." (Typo aside.) The image of seeing the word "cat" pop into your mind, the singular *they* arriving "ballistically", does more work than any of the academic sections. This is your voice telling on its own brain.

### Rhetoric that doesn't
"In this way, the predictive processing framework offers a powerful and unifying account of the cognitive and neural dynamics of language~-- an account that sees linguistic processing not as a passive reception of information, but as an active, hypothesis-testing process of prediction and error-driven learning." This sentence reads as if it were generated to fill a slot. "Powerful and unifying," "active hypothesis-testing process": every adjective is a tell. The version of you who wrote about Sasha and Mia would never write this paragraph.
