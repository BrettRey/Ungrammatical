## Chapter 19: Communicative efficiency

### Strengths
The Highway 427 anecdote (lines 40-47) is the chapter's organizing image and it's a beauty. Highway exits are usually on the right where you drive on the right; sometimes a contra-lateral exit happens because the alternative would be even more complex. The same is true of language: there's a default pattern (head-first or head-last), and there's a heavy-NP shift when the alternative would be worse. This is the kind of vivid mechanical analogy that lets a non-linguist feel the linguistic point.

The dependency-distance argument with the *book... to my best friend... for Christmas* example (lines 53-66) is excellent. The reader sees, with actual word counts, why the heavy-NP shift exists. The 9 vs 20 vs 10 distance counts are the right kind of evidence. This is the chapter at its best.

The CNN-architecture passive/active example (lines 76-79) lands the point about the passive: "No matter how many times you've been told to avoid the passive voice, I think you'll agree that the passive is better here." That sentence is exactly the right register for the trade book.

The trash-out cline (lines 85-94) is a teacher's gift. The reader feels the building pressure across (a) through (g) and feels the resolution at (g). This is empirical pedagogy.

### Major concerns
The chapter is currently labeled `\subsection{Keeping right and keeping left}` (line 9) instead of as a section. This is a layout artifact but suggests the chapter has been moved or is incomplete.

The chapter's exposition of Futrell's efficiency hypothesis (lines 11-13) is mostly right but feels generic ("a balance between the need to convey information effectively and the cognitive constraints of human brains"). The footnote acknowledges the heavy debt to Futrell 2020. A trade book reader doesn't need to know the academic name "dependency locality"; they need to feel the pressure that produces it. The first paragraph is more abstract than the chapter's own examples.

The Japanese example at (\ref{ex:take-a-rest-Distance-J}) shows mirror-image structure with dependents on the opposite side. Good. But the romanization is missing, the gloss is missing, and the typography is messy (the brackets `\ob...\cb` interleave awkwardly with the underlines). A non-linguist won't be able to read the example. **Add romanization and gloss for each example.**

The chapter ends at line 109 mid-argument, after introducing the alternating-dependents idea (Gildea & Temperley 2007) and the asymmetry between short determiners and long relative clauses. There's no closing claim, no return to the chapter's question. The chapter feels truncated.

There's also an issue with the chapter's relationship to the book's overall argument. Communicative efficiency is presented as an external pressure that shapes grammar. That's true but it raises a question the chapter doesn't address: how does efficiency interact with the HPC view of grammaticality as homeostatic-property-cluster? Probably: efficiency is one of the homeostatic forces. But the chapter doesn't say so.

There are some small issues: line 49 "is a heave NP" should be "heavy NP". Line 64 "the heavy noun phrase in the basic sentences structure" is a typo for "in the basic sentence structure" or similar.

### Priority fixes
- Promote the subsection to a section. The chapter needs proper top-level structure.
- Romanize and gloss the Japanese example. As written, no non-Japanese-reader can interpret it.
- Write a closing paragraph that returns to the chapter's question. Communicative efficiency explains why some patterns are widespread; it doesn't explain everything; what does it leave out?
- Connect the chapter to the book's HPC argument. Efficiency is one of the stabilizing forces in a homeostatic property cluster of grammaticality.
- Fix the typos at lines 49 and 64.
- Consider adding the well-known cross-linguistic generalizations: SOV correlates with postpositions (Greenberg's Universals 4 and 16); SVO correlates with prepositions. These are textbook examples of efficiency in action and a reader will appreciate seeing them named.

### One concrete suggestion
Promote the trash-out cline to the chapter's organizing example. Use it twice. Open with the cline (a) "I threw the trash out" through (g) "I threw out the smelly kitchen trash from under the sink that had been sitting there for two weeks." A reader feels the pressure across the cline and ends up understanding why heavy-NP shift exists. Then walk through the dependency-distance idea using the *book... to my best friend... for Christmas* example. Then the highway-427 analogy. Then the cross-linguistic mirror image (Japanese, with proper gloss). Then the alternating-dependents extension. Close with the HPC connection: communicative efficiency is one stabilizing force for grammaticality, but it's not the only one. Conventionalization, identity-marking, and learnability all play in.

### Rhetoric that works
"No matter how many times you've been told to avoid the passive voice, I think you'll agree that the passive is better here." This is the writer talking to the reader as an equal, asking them to look at one example and judge for themselves. It works because the example earns it.

### Rhetoric that doesn't
"Languages are not arbitrary in their structure but are shaped by a balance between the need to convey information effectively and the cognitive constraints of human brains." (line 11). This sentence is true but bloodless. The chapter's examples (highway 427, trash-out, book-for-Christmas) are vivid; the abstract framing is abstract. Either trust the examples to do the framing or write a more concrete opening sentence. "Why do we say *I threw the trash out* instead of *I out threw the trash*?" is a better hook than "languages are shaped by a balance."
