## Chapter 07: The Generation Gap

### Strengths

The Lego-vs-alloy framing of discrete combination is a genuinely good piece of pedagogy. A reader can hold the difference in their hands. *Cosmic dust storm* with its prohibition against *cosmic a dust storm* is the right kind of small puzzle for a trade book: simple, bracingly counterintuitive, and right at the edge of the reader's introspection.

The McCawley quotation in the *New Yorker* ("Chomsky assumes that there are sentences which belong to the language and other sequences of words which don't") is the chapter's punchline and lands - a former student going public against his teacher in a national magazine. That dispute has dramatic potential the chapter hints at but doesn't develop.

The Boeckx point about island repair as an embarrassment to derivational approaches ("How can something be repaired if it was never constructed in the first place?") is the kind of specific theoretical aporia that makes for a memorable chapter ending.

### Major concerns

The chapter is a stub in disguise. Its substantive claim - that the late-1960s consensus around generative grammaticality crumbled by the early 1970s - is asserted in two sentences citing Harris 2021 and Shenker 1972. The Linguistics Wars, which were real, prolonged, and personally vicious, deserve more than a paragraph in a chapter dedicated to the gap they opened up. Where are the parties? Where is the room? The chapter mentions "Postal and Lakoff and I" without telling us who Postal and Lakoff were as people, what their account of grammaticality was, why it differed.

The Culicover *likely/probable* contrast appears mid-chapter without being earned. A reader who has not read Culicover 1999 has no way to know what's at stake.

The "yin and yang of order and disorder" closing of the first section is a placeholder phrase doing the work of a thesis. What does it mean concretely? What is one example of a language evolving toward systematicity while admitting new irregularities?

The chapter ends mid-thought with a single Boeckx quotation. The "Generational failures" section is essentially blank.

The mathematics-of-generative-grammar section is surface-level: it tells us what discrete combination is and that languages have rules, but it never addresses the reader's likely question: what *is* generated by a generative grammar? Is it strings? Trees? Pairs of strings and meanings? The Chomsky hierarchy? Phrase structure rules vs. transformations? At a trade book's level, even a one-paragraph orientation would help.

### Priority fixes

1. The chapter needs at least three more sections: who Lakoff and McCawley and Postal were, what generative semantics actually argued, and what came of the Linguistics Wars. Without this the title "Generation Gap" lands without specificity.
2. Earn the *likely/probable* example. Hurford's 2012 contrast (probable can't take *to*-infinitival; likely can) is set up as if the reader knows it. They don't.
3. Develop or cut the Boeckx aporia. If you keep it, make it a section, not an orphan paragraph.
4. The opening's "circularity of these claims" - that grammar generates grammatical strings, and we know which are grammatical because the grammar generates them - is the chapter's most interesting potential thesis but is dropped on page 1.

### One concrete suggestion

Randy Harris's *The Linguistics Wars* (2021, the citation already in the chapter) is full of scenes. Pick one. The 1969 La Jolla conference is the canonical flashpoint - Lakoff, Postal, Ross, McCawley on one side, Chomsky's loyalists on the other, in a room in California, with people calling each other names that ended careers. Or, more domestically, Postal's IBM exile and his self-directed thesis (already mentioned in chapter 5 in passing). A trade reader will remember the scene of the breakup of a graduate-school cohort over what counts as a grammatical sentence. They will not remember "the broad consensus had crumbled."
