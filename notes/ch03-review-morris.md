## Chapter 03: Being grammatical isn't always enough

### Strengths

The chapter has two genuine human moments worth keeping. The first is the text exchange with Reynolds's wife: she texts *you still at the gym?* and he replies *I still at the gym* as a joke. That is the chapter's argument in three lines. A grammatical question with a non-syntactic-but-pragmatically-licensed shape, and the deadpan reply that exposes it. It works because we're inside a marriage for a moment.

The second is Jeff Brown and the passive-hating professor. A philosophy supervisor writes "Avoid passive voice. Look at Nietzsche." Jeff and Reynolds open the Nietzsche, find a passive in the second paragraph. Two sentences. Whole comedy. A man advising in a register he himself ignores. The professor isn't named; he doesn't need to be. The scene is the argument.

The Geoff Pullum / Richard Osman cat example is also good, especially because the argument depends on a specific cat named Trouble being persistent enough that Henk's stroking matters. There's a creature in the room.

### Major concerns

This is one of the most clearly unfinished chapters in the manuscript. Section 1 is labelled "ideas" with two literal bullet points (Haspelmath, Zwicky and Pullum). The Anne Carson section trails off at "challenges traditional literary boundaries" with no actual Carson quote. Section subheading "Syntactic Satiation" has no body. The Brown-and-Nietzsche scene ends in two centered em-dashes (visible scaffolding). The intransitive-verbs section explicitly says "[additional examples needed]" twice. The passives passage breaks off at "writers\dots seem to believe that\dots". And the chapter ends on a Pragmatics quotation with no chapter close.

This is fine. It is what it is. But the disclaimer is required: this is a stub-with-pockets-of-real-prose, not a draft.

The opening voice is the worst LLM tic in the book so far: "Back in the 1970s, when bell-bottoms were groovy and disco was king, linguists had some funny ideas about grammar." Then a "Stayin' Alive" reference. This is ChatGPT doing a parody of a popular-science book. Cut every word of it. The argument that follows (1970s linguists called things ungrammatical that were really pragmatic) is a real argument; let it land in your voice.

On the protagonists question: 1970s linguists arrive as a comic-book "they" with their bell-bottoms. There is no specific person, no Postal or Ross or Chomsky in 1972 with a particular paper. To genuinely re-evaluate the field's mistake, you have to pick one specific paper, one specific claim, one specific name. The strawman "they" is exactly the kind of move the book is criticizing in others.

David Gil and Dan Everett show up as parenthetical figures in the Russian-dolls section. Both are field-researchers with rich human stories (Everett especially, given the Pirahã controversy and his own loss-of-faith narrative). Either expand them or cut.
