## Chapter 09: Without Grammar

### Strengths

The Ethan transcript is the chapter's gift. A real eleven-month-old, eleven months and twenty-one days old to be exact, captured by researchers in his own house. His mother bouncing the ball into his head ("Bounce, bounce, bounce, bounce whoops!"). Ethan saying "ball," then "baby," then "that," then laughing, then being told he's "a sleepy boy." A reader watches a small human acquire English in three minutes of transcript. The fact that Reynolds reproduces an actual stretch of recorded speech rather than paraphrasing it is the right move. The mother is half-tired, half-warm; the child is half-engaged, half-distracted. There is a tiny domestic film here, and it carries the chapter's argument: language without grammar is not a deprivation but a starting condition that human beings still inhabit.

The Bloom *Mommy sock* example is also doing work. The child uses two words to mean either "Mommy is putting a sock on me" or "Mommy's sock," with no internal structure to disambiguate. That is the chapter's thesis in two words: meaning before grammar. Jackendoff and Gil supply the theoretical scaffolding for what Ethan and Bloom show.

The first appearance of Riau Indonesian is also memorable, for its claim that an entire adult language can do what Ethan does, only on a wider scale.

### Major concerns

The chapter is split-brained. The first half is Ethan, Bloom, Jackendoff, Gil. The second half is Riau Indonesian. The pivot from one to the other is missing the bridge it needs.

Worse, the Riau Indonesian section is salted with LLM travel-guide writing. Two paragraphs (lines 95-101) about Sumatra's volcanoes, lush vegetation, the eastern lowlands, the Malacca Strait as "one of the world's most important and busiest maritime routes," the cultural heritage of Riau as a "rich blend of Malay, Chinese, and indigenous influences." None of this is grounded in a person, none of it advances the argument, and it does not sound like Reynolds. It sounds like ChatGPT after a request to "set the scene in Riau."

Lines 122-125 are mid-sentence: "John McWhorter, who cut his teeth in linguistics working on creoles. But David Gil s\dots" The chapter then continues. These are unfinished thoughts left in the file.

Line 178 is a corrupted Hurford quote with OCR damage: "condensing a mess variety," "creoles pagins," "B normal innate capac sign languages give us a windedles." That entire quotation is unusable in its current form and needs to be re-fetched from the source.

The Riau Indonesian conversation at lines 141-146 (Person A: You go where? Person B: Market.) is presented in English. It should be presented in Riau Indonesian with English glosses. Right now the reader can't tell whether the alleged "stripped down" form is a property of Riau or of Reynolds's translation.

On the protagonists question: David Gil is the chapter's hidden hero, an Indonesian-speaking field linguist who has documented this remarkable language. He gets two cited papers and most of the framing, but he never becomes a person. Where is Gil sitting on a verandah in Riau, with a notebook and a recorder, talking to a fisherman? Reynolds knows this kind of fieldwork (Komodo, chapter 0), and the chapter would benefit from importing his own register of attention to the working linguist.

Ethan is also under-used. We get a transcript and then he disappears. He could anchor the chapter as a recurring character: meet him at three minutes, return to him at one year and four months, return again at two years. The reader follows a specific child into a specific grammar.

### Priority fixes

1. Cut the Sumatra travel paragraphs (95-105). Replace with one sentence of geography and a fieldwork scene with David Gil.
2. Fix the corrupted Hurford quotation. Re-fetch from source.
3. Finish the broken sentences at lines 122-125.
4. Re-do the Riau Indonesian conversation in actual Riau Indonesian with glosses.
5. Decide whether the chapter is about (a) the developmental stage of pre-grammatical language, (b) the existence of low-grammar adult languages, or (c) both, and structure accordingly.

### One concrete suggestion

Open with Ethan saying *ball*, then *baby*, then *that*. Hold the moment. Then move directly to David Gil in Sumatra, listening to two adults having the conversation Ethan can't yet have, and noticing they're using a similar amount of grammar. The chapter's startling claim is right there: an adult language that uses about as much grammatical machinery as a one-year-old does. Don't bury that claim under travel-brochure prose. Lead with the contrast and let it shake the reader. Then, and only then, bring in Hurford's developmental thesis. The eleven-month-old and the adult Riau speaker should be in the same room on the page.
