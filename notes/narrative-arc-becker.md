## Proposed narrative arc: Becker's take

### The arc in one sentence

A reader is handed the asterisk, walked through a series of small puzzles where it does something different each time, and only then offered the cluster account that makes the variation make sense.

### The opening

The reader I'm imagining is a working linguist who learned generative grammar in graduate school, more or less believes it, and picks up the book because the title intrigues them. Maybe a syntactician at a teaching department; maybe a phonologist who still teaches Intro. Not hostile, but trained to spot the move where someone uses fashion or morality to smuggle in an attack on the field. They will close the book the moment they smell that move.

The opening (chapter 00 + chapter 01) is already close to right and should be left mostly alone. Schleicher to Sweet to Householder; the asterisk as a borrowed symbol; Ross's islands handled with respect. What I'd add to chapter 01, as a one-page closing move, is the contract. Tell the reader, in plain prose, what the book is going to ask of them: I'm going to show you that the asterisk is doing many different jobs, and that those jobs hang together in a way that is neither the generative story nor the descriptivist story. I'm going to do it case by case, mostly with my own data, and I'll tell you the theoretical claim only after you've seen the cases. If you want the theory now, skip to chapter twenty.

That last sentence does the work. It tells the suspicious reader that you know they're suspicious. It performs solidarity with the empirical core of their training: we'll look first, we'll theorize after. It earns the right to make the argument because it asks the reader to be an investigator with you rather than a target.

I'd also drop the biographical note that drifts into the introduction. A trade book can carry the author's voice; it shouldn't carry the author's CV. The asterisk can carry chapter 01.

### The development

The middle is where the book has been losing readers, and the fix is sequencing, not new writing. The chapters Brett has already written empirically are strong; they need to be put in an order where each one teaches the reader something the previous one couldn't.

Three movements, each ending with a small "huh" that sets up the next.

**Movement A: the asterisk does more than one thing.** Open with chapter 02 (gradience) trimmed of its toy generative grammar. Then chapter 09 (life without grammar / Riau, child language) and chapter 10 (real patterns: spray/load, *probable to play*). The point of this movement is empirical: by the end, a reader has seen ungrammaticality used as gradient, as conventional, as language-specific, and as pattern-violating, all without being asked to accept a theory. The "huh" is that these don't seem to be the same property.

**Movement B: the asterisk moves.** Chapter 12 (becoming ungrammatical: *much*/*very*, with the *do*-support material absorbed) followed by chapter 15 (the *Rubymar* infinitival-perfect, the *whose* investigation). This is the reader's first encounter with diachrony as ungrammaticality's natural habitat. The "huh" is that the asterisk's territory shifts. Yesterday's grammar is today's error, and the system tolerates the motion.

**Movement C: the asterisk crosses lines.** Chapter 13 (across language boundaries: dialects, AAVE habitual *be*) and chapter 16 (codeswitching). Then, and only then, chapter 14, retitled and reframed: not "fashion" as a consumer-magazine topic but "innovation as glamour", a chapter on the social mechanics by which ungrammatical forms become prestigious. Then chapter 04, but cut to a third of its length: the AAVE habitual *be* discussion, the brief observation that linguists routinely use moral-sounding judgments without flagging them, and out. The seven ethical frameworks go. Chapter 06 (politics) gets the same treatment: keep the linguistic content (the politics of corpora, of *they*, of style guides) and cut the editorializing.

This is the movement where the imagined reader is most at risk of bailing. They stay if the work is empirical and the framing is restrained. They go if the chapter heading reads like a takedown.

End movement C with chapter 05 (intuitions, how grammar feels), trimmed and re-placed. The reason it goes here, late in the middle rather than early, is that chapter 05 is about the detector. Putting the detector after the reader has watched it operate in eight different domains lets the chapter do its real job, which is to ask why a single feeling tracks so many different things. That question is the whole book in one chapter, and it has to be set up before it can be asked.

### The turn

Chapter 19 (efficiency) and chapter 17 (impossible languages), in that order. These are the chapters where the question stops being "what does the asterisk track" and starts being "what couldn't it track". Efficiency is the strongest functionalist account on the market and it deserves to be honoured before being supplemented; impossible languages is the strongest UG account and it deserves the same. The turn is that neither alone explains the scatter the reader has just seen, but each is right about a piece of it. The reader is now ready for an account that takes both seriously.

### The resolution

Chapter 07+08 combined into one chapter on the generative legacy: what they got right, what they missed, why a cluster account is the natural successor rather than a refutation. Then chapter 20 (now no longer empty, because the cluster account is what fills it). Twenty is where the HPC framework finally gets named, with the fourteen-point list, but recast: not as a list the reader has to accept, but as the inventory of properties the reader has already encountered across the book. The work is done; the model is just notation for what's already on the page.

Chapter 21 closes. The *long, long road* puzzle is the right finale because it is small, single, and shows the cluster operating on one construction across five languages. It's the book's argument running on a single track.

Chapter 11 (neurolinguistics) gets cut unless Brett can draft it as a short coda after 20, used to anchor the cluster claim biologically.

### Why this arc serves the HPC argument

HPC is hard to teach because it asks the reader to accept that a category is real without being defined by necessary conditions. You can't argue the reader into that; you can only show them, repeatedly, that the necessary-conditions story keeps failing while the cluster keeps holding. The arc above is twenty chapters of failed necessary-conditions stories, each one different, ending with the only model that absorbs the variation. That's the strongest form the argument can take. State the model in chapter 01 and the reader spends nineteen chapters waiting to argue. State it in chapter 20 and the reader has already done the work.

The arc also performs the right kind of solidarity. By honouring the generative tradition's empirical work in the early and middle chapters, by giving efficiency and UG their due in the turn, and by reserving the political and moral material for the chapters where it has linguistic content to support it, the book becomes one a generative reader can finish without flinching. That's the precondition for being heard.

1. 00 Introduction (the asterisk: Schleicher, Sweet, Householder)
2. 01 Stories from syntactic islands (Ross, the contract with the reader)
3. 02 Gradient grammaticality (trim the toy grammar)
4. 09 Life without grammar (child language, Riau)
5. 10 Real patterns (spray/load, *probable to play*)
6. 12 Becoming ungrammatical (*much*/*very*, *do*-support absorbed)
7. 15 What's ungrammatical (*Rubymar*, the missing *whose*)
8. 13 Across language boundaries (dialect, AAVE habitual *be*)
9. 16 Codeswitching
10. 14 Innovation as glamour (retitled; the social mechanics of prestige forms)
11. 04 Moral-sounding judgments (cut to a third; AAVE *be* + linguists' usage)
12. 06 Politics of grammar (cut to linguistic content only)
13. 05 How grammar feels (the detector, after the reader has seen it operate)
14. 19 Efficiency (the strongest functionalist account)
15. 17 Impossible languages (the strongest UG account)
16. 07+08 The generative legacy (merged: what they got right, what they missed)
17. 20 Getting grammaticality wrong (now: the cluster account, named)
18. 11 Neurolinguistics (short coda, if it can be drafted)
19. 21 A long, long way (the cluster operating on one construction)

Cut: 18 (swearing, unless absorbable into 14 or 13). Retitle: 14, 20.
