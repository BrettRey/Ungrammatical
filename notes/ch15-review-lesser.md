## Chapter 15: What's ungrammatical

### Strengths

The opening Mom-and-Son dialogue is the clearest demonstration of context-dependent grammaticality in the manuscript. "I go there yesterday" reads as ungrammatical in one mother-son exchange and as a perfectly grammatical narrative present in another. Two short dialogues, six lines apiece. That's editorial economy, and it's the chapter's strongest moment.

The French passé composé section is the chapter's other anchor and a genuine piece of historical-linguistic explanation. The phonological-erosion-leading-to-conjugational-confusion-leading-to-disambiguation-via-passé-composé argument is laid out cleanly with three tables (Old French verb endings, simple-past conjugations, passé composé conjugations). The passé simple acquiring "a whiff of provincialism" is exactly the right idiomatic register for a trade book.

The horse-raced-past-the-barn-fell garden-path discussion does its work. So does the Chomsky "colorless green ideas sleep furiously" passage with the Pereira bigram-model finding (the grammatical-but-meaningless string is 200,000 times more likely than the word salad). That's a small empirical victory worth keeping.

The Lego-versus-Ikea analogy for grammar-versus-lexis flexibility is clever. The Cratylus / Persian "sib" passage on phonemic non-iconicity is also good, and the Winter (2022) trilled-r study is a real find.

The Ex-Lax / Pat Nixon / Spiro Agnew context-dependent grammaticality story is one of the book's most charming anecdotes; the parenthetical "(In case you're unfamiliar with the historical context, Ex-Lax is a chocolate-flavored laxative, and Spiro Agnew was the Vice President under Richard Nixon. More I will not say.)" is a writer's joke that lands.

The Rubymar-and-Pullum collaboration narrative is exactly the sort of intellectual-detective-work passage that lifts a trade book. Geoff emails, asks what's wrong, proposes the than-than analogy, Reynolds tests it, Reynolds searches COCA, the corpus tool refuses the search, Reynolds reverts to concordance browsing, finds "She seems to be the one to have changed the most," realizes the difference is between tensed "is" and untensed "to be." That sequence is real research at trade-book pace, and it's the chapter's intellectual climax.

### Major concerns

The chapter is 632 lines. It is two chapters in a trench coat, and the second chapter ("The curious case of the missing whose") is mostly redundant with chapter 5.

The "World tour of whose" section delivers German, Spanish, Persian, and Japanese in quick succession. The German example with "dessen" is good. The Spanish "cuyo" example is weak (the asterisk is on a sentence with no clear license for the construction even in Spanish). The Persian and Japanese sections are gestural ("doesn't have a direct equivalent to whose, but it uses a possessive construction that behaves in a similar way"), which is the AI-explainer voice describing what the author would have written if they had written it.

The "Lessons" five-item bulleted list at the end ("Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence" / "Even native speakers' intuitions can be misleading" / "What we perceive as ungrammatical may simply be an overlooked form-context mismatch" / "Linguistic theories need to account for rare as well as common phenomena" / "Language is always full of surprises") is the AI-summary tic at full volume. Five generalities the chapter has already shown. Cut.

The Winckel et al. paragraph at the very end ("These restrictions on antecedents and their relationship to ellipsis and pronouns mirror broader limitations found in syntactic structures like filler-gap constructions") feels grafted on. There's even a stray closing quotation mark at the end of the chapter.

There's significant duplication with chapter 5's whose discussion. Either fold them together or differentiate the chapters' work clearly.

The middle of the chapter (lines 219-275ish) wanders. The phonemic-iconicity, Geertz-meaning-seeking-animals, and "But seriously, [come back to this]" are signs of a draft mid-passage that needs structural surgery.

### Priority fixes

1. Cut the second pass through the whose material. Chapter 5 already establishes the central case; this chapter should briefly recap and move on.
2. Cut or rewrite the "World tour of whose" section. The German is fine; the others are gestural.
3. Cut the five-item "lessons" bulleted list at the end.
4. Repair the stray closing quotation mark at the very end of the chapter.
5. Decide where the Rubymar / Pullum collaboration belongs. It's the chapter's gem. It might warrant its own short chapter.
6. Trim the phonemic-variation, McGurk-effect-question, Cratylus-Persian, and Winter trilled-r material to the essentials. As written, this stretch is 50+ lines for a small point about phonology not registering as ungrammatical.
7. The "but seriously, [come back to this]" placeholder should not survive into a finished chapter.

### One concrete suggestion

Worst sentence: "Now, you might be wondering what all this has to do with our elusive independent \textit{whose}." This is the AI-tic check-in voice ("you might be wondering," "as we'll see," "let's pull this all together"). The chapter is full of these scaffolding sentences. Cut them all on a final pass.

Best sentence: "It's more like a linguistic ivory-billed woodpecker: long thought extinct, occasionally glimpsed, hotly debated, but stubbornly refusing to be definitively ruled out." This is the chapter at its imaginative best. The metaphor (an actually existing rare bird in linguistic clothing) is precise; "stubbornly refusing to be definitively ruled out" carries the right tone of evidential hedging. The sentence does the work of a paragraph of theory.
