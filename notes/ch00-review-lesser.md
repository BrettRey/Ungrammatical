## Chapter 00: Introduction

### Strengths

The chapter has a real opening. The asterisk-as-marker-of-things-not-said gambit is a writer's instinct: take a small symbol most readers will recognize and use it as a doorway. The six starred examples from CGEL land well, especially read aloud. They earn their keep because the strangeness is doing the work the prose later has to do less of.

The Schleicher and Sweet history is more readable than most book introductions on linguistics dare to be. The detail about Schleicher repurposing Aristophanes of Byzantium's marker for repeated lines is exactly the kind of thing a trade reader will remember a week later. And the Indonesian harbour memory in the personal section is genuinely lovely. Reynolds is showing he can write.

### Major concerns

The chapter is two introductions stitched together. The asterisk history is one book; the personal "how I got interested in grammar" section is another. The seam between them shows. By the time we hit the Tokyo bookshop and the Komodo island detour, the reader has already made the down payment for one kind of book and is being asked to pay again.

Worse is the LLM padding around Schleicher and Sweet. Whole paragraphs read like Wikipedia compressed. "Sweet was a multifaceted scholar, known for groundbreaking contributions to phonetics, Old English studies, and language teaching methodology." That sentence has no business in a trade book. The two commented-out paragraphs about Sweet's "deep conviction that spoken language held the key to understanding linguistic development" should stay commented out, or rather, deleted.

The first paragraph about the asterisk meaning "additional information, a small detour from the main text" is also throat-clearing. Reynolds knows what he wants to say two paragraphs later: the asterisk marks ungrammaticality. Start there.

The Mill epigraph is fine but unearned. It will read better in a chapter that actually engages with logical or universal-grammatical claims about syntax.

### Priority fixes

1. Cut the entire Sweet biographical paragraph (he was multifaceted, Heidelberg, etc.) down to two sentences before the descriptive-grammar quote.
2. Decide whether the personal section belongs here at all. If yes, push it later, perhaps after a tighter "what this book is about" close. If no, save for a preface.
3. Strip the closing paragraph of "It became a silent testament to a profound shift" prose. That's the AI-tic register the reader will sniff out instantly.
4. Look at the Householder paragraph. It dangles. Either land the punch (judgments vary, the asterisk lies, and that's the book's central problem) or cut.

### One concrete suggestion

Worst sentence: "It became a silent testament to a profound shift -- a moment when linguistics embraced its identity as a natural science, a relentless search for patterns in the seemingly chaotic evolution of human language." Throw it out. Replace with: "What began as a placeholder for missing words became, in Sweet's hands, a placeholder for sentences that shouldn't be said. The marker for what was lost became a marker for what is forbidden."

Best sentence: "Often the ungrammatical sentences linguists are 'starring' are those they've made up themselves." It works because it's plain, it's a small reversal of expectation, and it sets up everything that follows. The book's argument lives in that observation.
