## Chapter 06: Prescriptivism on the right and on the left

### Strengths
The chapter has a real argument hidden in it: that left and right both engage in linguistic prescriptivism, but on different terrain. The Neiman comparison (German feminist movement uses gender-marked forms; Anglophone feminist movement removes them) is a beautiful concrete instance of how anti-sexist rhetoric can travel in opposite linguistic directions. That single observation could carry a chapter. The Nunberg quotation ("slurs are derogative because they're the words that racists use") is precisely the right pivot: the meaning of a word as social rather than semantic.

The blindness-as-metaphor section asks a genuinely interesting question: if we deprecate the metaphorical sense, do we leave only bigots holding the literal sense, with the consequence that the word fully shifts. That's a real argument and it earns its place.

### Major concerns
The chapter is the shortest in the book (41 short paragraphs) and visibly an outline. There's no payoff structure, no introduction of the Neiman thesis as such, no working through of the asymmetry between left and right. The Beaver-and-Stanley section at lines 29-41 is **literally a chat-with-an-LLM transcript pasted in.** "You propose an interplay of formal constraints..." is the LLM addressing Brett, not the book addressing the reader. The footnote at line 23 ends "and the blind and deaf communities" with no completion of the sentence. Multiple stray sentences at the end of paragraphs.

The chapter also leans hard on academic citations (lakoff1973, miller1976, spender1980, baron1986, maggio1998) in a sequence that reads like a literature-review opening, the kind of cite-stacking a journal paper does, not a trade book. A non-linguist will glaze.

The chapter conflates several different phenomena in passing: prescriptivism about syntax, lexical taboo, identity-first vs person-first language, slur reclamation, metaphorical extension. Each of these deserves separate treatment. The current chapter sketches the territory and stops.

The argument that the left polices vocabulary while the right polices syntax is a sharp claim, but it's never made cleanly. It's a one-sentence observation buried in line 10, and the chapter doesn't develop it.

### Priority fixes
- **Cut the LLM transcript** at lines 29-41. Replace with one paragraph that says what the chapter actually owes to Beaver and Stanley if anything.
- Finish the footnote at line 23.
- Cut the citation pile-up in the first six paragraphs. Trade-book readers don't need eight citations to be told that the feminist movement raised awareness about gendered language.
- Land the chapter's actual claim: write one explicit paragraph that says "Left and right both prescribe; they differ only in what they're prescribing about."
- Decide which examples drive the argument. Probably: gendered job titles (the Neiman comparison), slurs (Nunberg), identity-first vs person-first (CFB), metaphorical *blind*. Pick three; treat each in two paragraphs with a clear example and a clear takeaway.

### One concrete suggestion
Rebuild the chapter from a single concrete confrontation. Open with the Neiman observation. State the chapter's claim in one sentence: people on the left are not anti-prescriptivist; they prescribe vocabulary while the right prescribes syntax. Then four short cases: (1) German job titles vs. English job titles; (2) Nunberg on slurs; (3) Person-first vs identity-first (with the CFB and CNIB names actually filled in); (4) Metaphorical *blind* and the risk of leaving a word to its bigots. Close with the question that the book is actually about: what does grammaticality have to do with all this? The answer might be that none of it is about grammaticality at all, and that's the chapter's contribution. It's worth saying clearly. As written, the chapter implies it but never says it.

### Rhetoric that works
"slurs are derogative *because they're the words that racists use*." Nunberg's sentence carries this entire chapter. Build around it. The blindness-rebound argument also works because it follows the same logic: words mean what their users make them mean, and that has political consequences.

### Rhetoric that doesn't
"In some cases, friends or in-group members might use this term in a lighthearted or even affectionate manner, without intending any harm. This highlights the significance of context and intent when assessing the appropriateness of language." This sentence appears to be writing about itself. It doesn't tell us anything we didn't know two sentences ago. The actual content is "context matters", which the example already showed. Cut it.

### A note about the LLM contamination
The "You propose," "I'll avoid the etymological fallacy" tone in the Beaver-Stanley section is identifiable as transcript-residue. A reader who has used an LLM will recognise it. This is not just a draft hiccup, it actively damages credibility. Cut it before any colleague sees the manuscript.
