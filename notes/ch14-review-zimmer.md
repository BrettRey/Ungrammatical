## Chapter 14: Grammar and glamour

### Strengths

The Cory Doctorow exchange is the best material in the chapter and one of the best moments in the book. Doctorow's *Boing Boing* sentence ("if they test it on kids -- even it's never approved for use on kids!") gets emailed to the author, who asks if it was intentional. The reply: "Yup. It's colloquial, but expressive, unambiguous, and colorful." That email exchange settles the descriptive question (yes, *even* without *if* is grammatical for some users) and earns the chapter's central claim: a proficient speaker can innovate, a learner cannot.

The morphological dissection of *enshittification* - *shit* (Old English *scitte*, `diarrhea') + *-ify* (Latin *-ficare* < *facere*) + *-ic* + *-ate* + *-tion* + *en-* - is patient, fun, and the right length. The aside that *embiggen* entered the OED in 2017 and Merriam-Webster in 2018, twenty-odd years after the Simpsons used it in 1996, is the kind of fact that reframes the etymology section. The Ben Zimmer quote at the end of that paragraph is well-placed.

The 2024 American Dialect Society Word of the Year vote at the Sheraton New York Times Square hotel is a real scene with a real date.

The tracksuit analogy is risky but it works: an unfashionable matching sweatsuit is glamorous when worn with a trench coat and a "nice handbag," and a learner's *a pair glasses* is ungrammatical until a proficient speaker deploys it ironically against *I have a dozen tracksuits*. The Vanessa Friedman Jean Harlow quote is the right kind of small concrete reference. The quote does the work that a reader would otherwise need a paragraph of analysis to feel.

### Major concerns

The chapter is short and stitched together. Three subsections (*Even it's never approved*, *Tracksuits*, *The glory of enshittification*) each work on their own, but the chapter has no opening, no transition between them, and no closing. The reader has to assemble the thesis themselves.

The "grammar and glamour" pun in the title is good (the words are etymologically related; *glamour* arose as a Scots variant of *grammar* meaning enchantment, learned magic), and the chapter doesn't use this. That's a missed opportunity for an opening hook.

The footnote about *dozen* taking a partitive *of them* but not *of words* is a good aside but is parked in a footnote where most readers won't get to it.

The chapter argues that an innovation must "achieve some kind of semantic or pragmatic goal" but the catalog of possible goals (humour, group membership, irony, emphasis, in/formality, in/directness, intimacy, immediacy, skepticism) is enumerated rather than illustrated. Each of those nine motives could earn an example, and currently none does.

### Priority fixes

1. Write an opening that uses the *grammar*/*glamour* etymological link. Sir Walter Scott popularized *glamour* in *The Lay of the Last Minstrel* (1805), drawing on the Scots usage in which a *glamour* was a magical illusion cast by someone with arcane knowledge - a *grammarian*. That's the chapter's thesis in a single etymological note.
2. Write a closing paragraph that names what the three vignettes share: they're all cases where a proficient speaker's innovation lands and a learner's same innovation wouldn't.
3. Consider giving Doctorow more space. His role across this chapter (the *even it's never approved* example, the coining of *enshittification*) makes him this chapter's central figure. Right now he appears twice as if by accident.
4. Pull the *dozen* footnote up into the body if you can.

### One concrete suggestion

The 2024 Word of the Year vote is a scene with built-in drama. American Dialect Society members in a hotel ballroom in midtown Manhattan, January 5, 2024, voting on words. Ben Zimmer chairs the New Words Committee. The candidates each year include slang, political coinages, technical jargon, and structural newcomers. Sit the reader down at the back of the room. Who else was on the shortlist? *Enshittification* won; what came second? Was the vote close? The chapter cites the proceedings as a footnote, but a paragraph of the room would let the reader feel the social ritual that ratifies a coinage as grammatical. That ratification is itself the chapter's argument: glamour requires recognition.
