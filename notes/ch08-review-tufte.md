## Chapter 08: Help! Help! I'm being repressed (Coordination)

### Strengths
The Horner-Whiten over-imitation experiment (lines 27-33) is the chapter's core empirical anchor and a good one. The 4x2 design (chimps vs. children, opaque vs. transparent box) yields a clean and counter-intuitive result: chimps emulate, children imitate. This is exactly the kind of paired-comparison evidence that a small chart could compress into a glance.

The Habermas quote ("Not every irregularity indicates a rule violation. One has to know the rule if one wishes to determine whether someone is deviating from it.") is well-placed and load-bearing for the chapter's larger point that grammaticality is normative, not merely descriptive.

### Major concerns
The chapter is essentially a notebook. Section 8.1 is literally titled "Notes" and contains six block quotes from Heath, Hobbes, Rawls, Lewis, and O'Connor strung together with brief connective tissue. This is a worthy reading list but it is not a chapter draft.

The over-imitation discussion (the only substantive empirical content) gets four paragraphs of summary followed by four labelled subheadings ("Learning as Performance," "Shared Language as Ritual," "From Imitation to Innovation," "A Bridge to Hurford") that are each a single paragraph of speculation. Those paragraphs read as LLM-generated brainstorm, not analysis.

The whole chapter is text. There is no figure showing the puzzle-box experimental conditions or its results. There is no figure showing the conformity / over-imitation gradient across species. For a chapter whose central evidence is a comparative experiment, that is a missed opportunity at the center of the design.

The connection between conformity and grammaticality is asserted but never traced concretely. The chapter never says "here is a grammatical norm; here is the over-imitation that produced it; here is the norm-violation; here is the social punishment." Instead, the closing line ("Ungrammaticality is good because it increases robustness?") is a question to itself.

### Priority fixes
1. Move all the block quotes (Hobbes/Heath, Lewis, Rawls, O'Connor, Habermas) to a coherent middle section that argues a position rather than aggregates citations.
2. Build a small chart showing the Horner-Whiten result (see suggestion).
3. Replace the four "From Imitation to Innovation" speculative subheadings with a single tighter paragraph or cut them. They read as scaffolding.
4. Connect over-imitation to grammar with one concrete sustained example: a child over-imitating an irregular plural (e.g., a Standard English child saying *foots* and getting corrected, vs. an AAVE child producing habitual *be* and getting reinforced). This is the chapter's argument and it is missing.

### One concrete suggestion
Build a single 2x2 table titled "Over-imitation across species and box-transparency."

Rows: Chimpanzee | Human child (3-4 yrs)
Columns: Opaque box | Transparent box

Cells: percent of irrelevant actions imitated (approximate, from Horner-Whiten 2005).
- Chimp / opaque: ~ near-100%
- Chimp / transparent: ~ low (selectively emulates)
- Child / opaque: ~ 75%
- Child / transparent: ~ 75% (still over-imitates)

The cell that breaks pattern is (child, transparent). Highlight it in a contrasting tone. Annotate: "Children over-imitate even when they can see the steps don't matter."

Below the table, a single sentence: "This is the cognitive substrate for grammaticality as norm: humans copy ritually-marked behaviour, *especially* when the ritual is visibly arbitrary."

This 2x2 plus that sentence would do what eight pages of citations gesture at. The deeper Tufte point: a comparative experiment with two factors and two levels is a 2x2 grid by definition, and the chapter is currently telling the reader about a 2x2 grid in narrative form. Show the grid. Compress the prose. Use the recovered space for the missing grammar-specific case study.
