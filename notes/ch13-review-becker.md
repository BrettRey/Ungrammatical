## Chapter 13: Across language boundaries

### Strengths
The Singlish/AAE/Standard English Venn-diagram move is a useful organizing device. The asymmetry that the chapter identifies — non-standard speakers are extensively exposed to Standard, and treat both as grammatical, while Standard speakers treat non-standard forms as ungrammatical — is sociolinguistically right and worth foregrounding. That asymmetry has serious consequences for how we test grammaticality, who gets called ungrammatical, and what "ungrammatical" actually means. A book about HPC clusters of grammaticality should make this point cleanly, and the chapter mostly does.

The four-way contrast at the heart of the *Ain't nobody come to help us* analysis is genuinely good. You're showing that what looks like a single departure from Standard English (using *ain't*) actually involves at least four separate grammatical decisions: copula vs. *have*, subject inversion, double negation, and the lexical *ain't* itself. That's the kind of careful unpacking that respects AAE as a system, not as Standard English minus features. A generative reader who works on AAE syntax (Green, Spears, Wolfram) will find the analysis fair.

The BIN/been distinction citing Spears is exactly right. Camouflaging is the classic AAE phenomenon, and using it to make the larger book point — that grammaticality requires noticeability — is a genuinely good move.

The Putnam and O'Hern microdialect experiment from 1955 is a real find. It does the work the chapter needs.

### Major concerns
The chapter's opening (lines 3-11) is heavily LLM-generated. "We speak in many ways. Some of us use academic language…", "showcases a broad spectrum of styles," "amalgamates English with elements from Malay, Mandarin, and other Chinese dialects," "reflecting a mix of linguistic heritages." A generative reader who reaches paragraph two will recognize this. Cut.

The Bill Withers reference is charming but isolated. It's not a good entry into the chapter's actual claim about register and dialect.

The Singlish examples in line 33 are taken from Wikipedia, footnoted but not deeply engaged. *Wah lau! This guy Singlish si beh hiong sia* is a fine example, but the chapter doesn't say what makes it Singlish (the Hokkien-derived particles, the loss of copula, the focus structure). Without that, it reads as exoticism, not analysis. A reader who knows Singlish will notice that the chapter exoticizes the variety while claiming to defend it.

The "gendered language" section (lines 105-122) puts a complicated set of claims in two paragraphs and ends with a question that's left hanging. *Oh my gosh* feels gendered, *I'm stoked* feels gendered, but the chapter doesn't engage the literature (Lakoff, Tannen, Eckert, Trechter) or the controversies. The closing question ("is the competence mismatch somehow special?") is a real question and deserves an answer.

The "One language, multiple grammars" section (lines 94-103) is just a list. *Balinese* with six unconnected category labels. Either develop or remove. Right now it's a placeholder.

The bilingual-syntax section (lines 79-85) gives one example pair (*Una glückliche Frau* / *i verdi Augen*) and then says "I won't try to generalize from these two examples. The data seems inconsistent." That's an honest signal, but it's a strange way to write a paragraph: introduce a phenomenon, give one example, decline to argue. A generative reader will see this and conclude you don't yet know what to say about codeswitching syntax — which is fine, but say so explicitly, or save the topic for chapter 16 (Codeswitching).

The chapter also flags but doesn't engage the most interesting Becker-relevant point: who gets to call whose language ungrammatical, and what does the social asymmetry of that judgment do? The chapter touches it (Spears's quote on Black students policing each other) but doesn't develop it. That should be the chapter's spine.

### Priority fixes
- Cut the opening 11 lines. Open with the Singlish/AAE/Standard Venn argument.
- Develop the Spears observation about in-group language policing. That's the chapter's strongest sociological move.
- Cut or develop the "gendered language" section. Two paragraphs and a hanging question is the worst option.
- Remove or develop the "Balinese" placeholder section.
- The bilingual-syntax pair (Cantone) is too thin for a section of its own. Either move it to chapter 16 or expand here.
- Source-ground the Singlish examples. Use a primary source (Lim, Bao, Wee), not just Wikipedia.

### One concrete suggestion
Build the chapter around the Spears observation: that in-group speakers of stigmatized varieties police each other, sometimes harshly. That observation cuts through both the descriptivist and the prescriptivist common positions and lets you make a real sociological argument: grammaticality is enforced socially, and the enforcement is most intense not at the borders of varieties but inside them. Open with the Spears quote, then lay out the *Ain't nobody come* multi-departure analysis, then bring in BIN/been to show how camouflaging works inside a variety, then close with the Putnam-and-O'Hern experiment showing that listeners pick up these distinctions. That arc is tight and gives the chapter a single argument. Right now it sprawls. A generative reader who reads a chapter where AAE is taken seriously as a grammatical system, and where in-group judgements are honored alongside out-group ones, will close the book with respect. That's the kind of chapter this should be.
