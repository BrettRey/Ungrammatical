## Chapter 10: Real patterns

### Strengths

The opening is one of the best in the manuscript. "Amelie showed Basil Clara." The chapter loads four short words with role information (agent, recipient, patient) and then walks through how Azerbaijani and Hixkaryana would do it differently. The walk is brisk, the contrast precise, and the move into "the pattern is grammar" lands. Reynolds is writing well here.

The data-compression framing is the right framing. English encodes role information into word order and case so that "any English speaker knows without the need for words like agent and patient or presenter and audience who is showing who to whom." That sentence does the work of two pages of theory.

The probable-to-play story is genuinely surprising. Reynolds had agreed with Hurford that "John is probable to leave" was ungrammatical, then noticed sports media uses "probable to play." The Bert Bell 1947 NFL injury-reports story plus the 1986 LA Times first-attestation is exactly the kind of small archival success that justifies a book. The chapter treats this as the discovery it is.

The Old Norse / Old French etymology and the "linguistic rails" metaphor are good. The hockey body-part shorthand ("Matthews is out with an elbow") is a nice grace note.

The Hurford-on-November-the-fifth section is also useful. Reynolds has gone to the EEBO corpus and traced the construction's emergence through the 1630s-1660s, and notes a transitional form ("the same moneth of february the twentith day"). This is real scholarship at trade-book pace.

### Major concerns

The chapter deflates at the end. After the Bert Bell triumph and the EEBO history, we get a "long tail" detour from Chris Anderson's Wired article that hints at a connection to micro-grammars but never quite makes it. The sentence "Computational linguistics didn't have the massive corpora or the sheer amount of compute needed to learn the micro-grammars, but" trails off in a "but". That "but" is doing a lot of unwritten work.

Then comes a numbered list (1-4 plus "Frequency and Entrenchment") titled "A functionalist approach to grammar emphasizes..." that reads exactly like an LLM filling in placeholder content. Each numbered point is generic ("subtle semantic difference," "conventionalization," "gradience and acceptability," "diachronic development"). The "In summary, a functionalist would likely address Hurford's challenge by considering factors such as..." closer is the AI-explainer summary tic.

There's a leftover comment "% consider a simulation like that in the code.py run in https://trinket.io/embed/python3/a5bd54189b // this simulation doesn't work well" that doesn't belong in a finished chapter.

The "Spraying and loading" section is competent but doesn't push as hard as the surrounding material. Reynolds names the verbs and notes that linguists haven't figured out why the alternation works for some verbs and not others. A trade reader will accept this; but the section doesn't have the narrative drive of the probable-to-play passage.

### Priority fixes

1. Cut the "long tail" detour. It's not earning its place. Either tie it tightly to the micro-grammar / Large Language Model claim or drop it.
2. Replace the numbered functionalist list with one paragraph in Reynolds's voice. The list reads as an LLM brainstorm and lands as a deflation after the EEBO triumph.
3. Repair the trailing "but" sentence.
4. Cut the "code.py" comment.
5. Strengthen the Spraying and Loading section by tying it to the chapter's argument: the patterns we recognize as grammatical are exactly those the language has happened to settle on, even when no semantic reason explains the settlement.

### One concrete suggestion

Worst sentence: "In summary, a functionalist would likely address Hurford's challenge by considering factors such as subtle semantic distinctions, language use and conventionalization, gradience in acceptability, and diachronic development." That's the AI-explainer voice giving you a wrap-up of the previous list, which itself was a wrap-up. Cut both list and summary; replace with: "A functionalist's reply to Hurford would lean on entrenchment, subtle semantic difference, and historical accident, all of which are visible in the probable-to-play case."

Best sentence: "And we have a new construction." It's the chapter's smallest sentence and one of its most important. After two pages tracking the bribery scandal, the 1947 commissioner's mandate, the 1986 LA Times column, the spread to the New York Post and CBS Sports, the chapter says: this is how a construction is born. The plainness is the punch.
