# Overall shape: McCloskey's take

## What the book is currently doing structurally

You're writing three books, Brett, stitched together with the same word
"ungrammatical." Book A is a graceful trade-history of the asterisk and
its kin, told through Schleicher, Sweet, Ross's islands, Hankamer and
Postal, Doctorow's *enshittification*. Book B is a positive theoretical
proposal: grammaticality as homeostatic property cluster, dropped into
chapter 1 as a numbered "Model of Grammaticality" with eleven items
and sub-items. Book C is a sociological tour: morality, politics,
fashion, swearing, codeswitching, neurolinguistics, impossible
languages, efficiency. The introduction promises Book A. Chapter 1
delivers Book B in a kind of front-loaded manifesto. Chapters 2-21 are
mostly Book C with some retreat to Book A.

The arc, as it stands, runs roughly: asterisk-history (intro) →
syntactic islands and HPC model (1) → gradient grammaticality (2) →
"not enough" / pragmatics (3) → morality (4) → intuitions/feelings (5)
→ politics (6) → generative grammar history (7) → coordination /
conformity (8) → no grammar / Riau Indonesian (9) → real patterns (10)
→ neurolinguistics stub (11) → diachrony (12) → varieties (13) →
fashion (14) → "what's ungrammatical" (15) → codeswitching (16) →
impossible languages (17) → swearing stub (18) → efficiency (19) →
"getting it wrong" stub (20) → reduplication coda (21).

Word counts tell the truth your table of contents disguises. Chapters
01, 02, 04, 05, 15 are full-size (337-900 lines). Chapters 06, 07, 08,
11, 14, 16, 17, 18, 20 are stubs or half-drafts (15-86 lines). The
book is not yet a book; it is a long first draft of a book whose spine
hasn't decided what it carries.

## What's working at the level of shape

The opening of the introduction is genuinely lovely: Schleicher's
asterisk, Sweet's repurposing, Householder's unease. That's a colleague
inviting you in, not a prophet preaching. Your sailing-boat tachiyomi
memoir paragraphs do real work, lowering the barrier for a reader who
might fear linguistics.

Chapter 1's syntactic-islands story is a strong second move. Ross,
Chomsky, and the wonderful ungrammatical examples earn the reader's
trust before any model arrives.

Chapter 21's *long long road* close is inspired. It is the best
single chapter in the manuscript at the level of voice, and it
performs the central thesis (grammaticality as historical-contingent
convention) without ever using the phrase "homeostatic property
cluster." That's the right register for the close.

## What's not working at the level of shape

**The model is in the wrong place.** Section 1.6's eleven-point
"Model of Grammaticality" with five levels of nested numbering arrives
roughly 50 pages in. The non-linguist will fall off here. This is
where the book turns from colleague to manifesto. A trade reader
doesn't want a numbered taxonomy of when something can be wrongly
judged grammatical. They want to know, on encountering the asterisk,
"what is this thing and why should I care?"

**The middle is a sociological grab-bag without a building order.**
Morality, intuitions, politics, generative grammar, coordination,
no-grammar, real patterns, neurolinguistics: these are not a sequence,
they are a pile. Chapter 4 (morality) opens with utilitarianism, runs
through Jahangir Khan playing squash, double modals, AAVE and
contractualism, and ends with "Synthesis: Ethical Frameworks." It
reads as if you typed everything you know about ethics-and-grammar
into one file. The reader cannot tell whether this is a load-bearing
chapter or a digression.

**The book repeats itself rhetorically.** The "ungrammatical-in-X-but-
grammatical-in-Y" move recurs in 3, 4, 9, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 19. By
chapter 13 the reader has grasped the point. The chapters need to
cumulate, not merely list new domains.

**It skips a beat between 1 and 2.** Chapter 1 ends in a numbered
manifesto plus a paragraph about emergent properties; chapter 2 opens
"Another view is evident in Chomsky's very early work." There is no
landing. The reader closes 1 wondering what the eleven-point list was
for, and opens 2 with no orientation.

**The history-of-generative-grammar (7) shows up too late.** It
belongs as part of the Ross-and-islands material in 1, or in dialogue
with the model. Coming at chapter 7, after morality and politics, it
breaks the sociological run.

**The end has three closings.** Chapter 19 (efficiency) reads like a
real conclusion (passive voice, heavy NP shift, dependency locality
synthesise the whole argument). Chapter 21 (long long) reads like a
better real conclusion. In between, chapter 20 is a half-page stub on
relative *whose*, salvaged from chapter 15. The book doesn't know
where to stop.

**The promised cross-domain payoff is unbuilt.** The chapters on
morality, politics, fashion, swearing, codeswitching are pitched in
the framing as the same shape as grammaticality. But the book never
cashes that in. The reader ends knowing grammar is convention-bound,
not knowing why moral intuitions and grammatical intuitions are
homologous in the same technical sense.

## What would change the shape for the better

**1. Cut the eleven-point model from chapter 1.** Keep the
constructions material, the soccer-handball analogy, the islands
story. Move the formal model to a short bridge chapter near the end
(after the cross-domain tour has earned it), or to an appendix. The
trade reader does not need it to follow the argument; the academic
reader will find it.

**2. Promote chapter 21 to be chapter 1.** *A long, long way* opens
with Hollies lyrics, demonstrates reduplication across English, Old
English, Japanese, Russian, Jamaican Creole, and North-East Ambae,
and shows historical contingency. It is the whole argument in one
chapter, in plain prose. Open the book here, not with Schleicher.
Move the asterisk-history (current intro) inside as historical detour.

**3. Combine 6 (politics) and 4 (morality) into one chapter:
"Grammar and the moral world."** Both currently confuse the analogy
(grammatical-judgment-as-moral-judgment) with the politics of
correctness. Pick one. The morality-as-shape argument and the
politics-of-prescriptivism argument want different chapters or one
unified chapter.

**4. Combine 9 (no grammar), 13 (varieties), 16 (codeswitching) into
"Where grammar comes from and where it stops."** These chapters share
the same insight (grammars are bounded com-sits) and currently take
turns saying it.

**5. Cut chapter 7 (generative grammar) as a freestanding chapter.**
Fold its content into the islands material in chapter 1, or into a
single "history of the idea" sidebar.

**6. Make chapter 5 (intuitions) the pivot, not a side excursion.**
The metacognitive-feeling argument is genuinely original; it earns
its size. But it currently sits at the front of a sociological run
without telling the reader why feelings of grammaticality are the
linchpin between the cognitive and the social. Reframe as "the
hinge."

**7. Promote chapter 19 (efficiency) to be the last argument
chapter.** Dependency-locality and heavy-NP shift give the reader
the mechanism. Chapter 21 (reduplication) stays as the closing
parable.

**8. Retitle chapter 15 from "What's ungrammatical" to "When
clashes resolve and when they don't."** The current title rehearses
the book's title; the chapter is actually about form-meaning
mismatch under pragmatic salvage.

**9. Stub-promote or cut.** Chapters 11, 18, 20 are too thin to
stand. Either commission them properly (neurolinguistics is real
content, swearing is its own book) or fold the salvageable bits
elsewhere. *(Un)grammatical* doesn't need 21 chapters; it probably
needs 14.

## The one structural move

Move chapter 21 to chapter 1.

Open with a Modern English speaker's intuitions about *long long
road*. Show the same construction patterning across five languages.
Show its historical accident in Old English. End with: "this is
what the rest of the book is about, the strange way that what feels
inevitable about grammar is mostly the residue of historical
contingency, social convention, and a few real cognitive limits."
Then let everything else, asterisk history included, fall into
place inside the question that opening raises.

Right now the book sounds like a colleague who agreed to write a
trade book and couldn't help including the technical paper as
chapter 1. The single move that turns it back into a colleague's
argument, rather than a manifesto with charming asides, is to put
the parable first and the model where parables put their morals,
quietly, near the end.
