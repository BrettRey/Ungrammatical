## Chapter 04: Morality and grammaticality

### Strengths
The double-modal data is the chapter's best moment: Coats's 1.29-billion-word corpus pulled 54 instances of *would might*, one per 24 million words. That single number does enormous work; the chapter then anchors it with a calibration ("about as rare as *exigence*"). This is exactly the kind of magnitude-comparison Cleveland would commend. The Pirahã anecdote about "we don't talk about that" is good evidence-by-anecdote.

### Major concerns
The Parfit / "climbing the mountain" frame is repeated three times: Section 4.4 introduces seven ethical perspectives. Section 4.5 introduces them again under the same name. Section 4.6 introduces them a third time as "climbing the same mountain." Six pages of LLM-paddinged prose triple-cover the same content. The reader sees the same scaffolding (consequentialism, deontology, virtue ethics, social justice, elitism) repeated with cosmetic rearrangement.

The chapter is dense with stubs and orphan content: section 4.1 ("Ideas") is bullet notes, section 4.2 ("Utilitarianism") has the heading "Utilitarianism is..." trailing into nothing. Section 4.3 ("Laws and morality") has a one-line stub and stops. The chapter ends mid-section ("Synthesis: Comparing Ethical Approaches") with no body.

The Jahangir Khan biography (lines 28-58) is roughly 2000 words to make the small point that competition is sometimes a virtue. He never returns. Cut almost all of it.

There is not a single figure or table in this chapter. For a chapter arguing that seven ethical frames yield seven different verdicts on the same sentences, the absence of a comparison grid is glaring.

The chapter relies entirely on prose to do work that begs for a comparison table.

### Priority fixes
1. Remove sections 4.5 and 4.6 entirely. Section 4.4 is sufficient and the duplication is embarrassing. (4.5 starts at line 90 with the same heading as 4.4 at line 78. This is not even a deliberate rewrite; it is two passes left in side by side.)
2. Replace the seven repeated subsections with a single comparison table (see suggestion).
3. Cut Khan biography to two paragraphs.
4. Section 4.4.1 and 4.6.4 (consequentialism), 4.4.4 and 4.6.5 (deontology), 4.4.6 and 4.6.6 (elitist power), 4.4.7 and 4.6.7 (social justice) are duplicated. Pick one and delete the other.

### One concrete suggestion
Build the chapter around a single 2D matrix that supersedes the entire repeated discussion.

Rows (ethical frame): pragmatism, virtue ethics, consequentialism, deontology, moralism, elitism, social-justice. (Seven rows.)
Columns (test sentence): "Me and him went to the store." / "Janet brought their own lunch." / "He be working late." / "would might not" / "I have 30 years." / "*Is the man who tall is in the room?" (Six columns.)

Each cell contains a one-word verdict: GRAMMATICAL / UNGRAMMATICAL / CONTEXT-DEPENDENT / IRRELEVANT, plus a one-line reason in 8-point type. Shade cells: white for grammatical, light grey for context-dependent, dark grey for ungrammatical, black with white text for irrelevant.

This matrix will reveal the actual structure of the argument: certain sentences (the AAVE habitual *be*, singular *they*) split the ethical frames sharply. Others (the structure-violation *Is the man who tall is...*) get the same verdict from every frame. The convergence-divergence pattern is exactly Parfit's "climbing the same mountain": where do the frames converge? Where do they split? The reader sees this in one display.

The current chapter takes 22 pages to make a point that wants to be made in one matrix plus 4 pages of commentary on what the matrix reveals. The deeper Tufte point: when seven things vary along three or four dimensions, prose is the wrong instrument. Use a small grid.
