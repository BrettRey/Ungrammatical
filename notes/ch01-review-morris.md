## Chapter 01: Stories from syntactic islands

### Strengths

The Herbie Hancock anecdote is the best thing in the chapter, possibly the best thing in the book so far. A 21-year-old at a piano with Miles Davis, the wrong chord, the hands clutched to the ears in shame, and Davis playing notes that retroactively make the wrong chord right. That is a film. That is a scene. The micro-detail (a minute of paralysis afterward, unable to touch the keys) is exactly the human texture documentary thrives on. It also does the chapter's argumentative work without strain. The reader feels in their body what "context-dependent grammaticality" means before any linguist explains it.

The Ann Fisher passage is the second-best thing. A woman writes the first English grammar by a woman, a man (Goold Brown) misgenders her in his survey, and another man (Kirkby) plagiarizes her almost immediately. There is real human stakes there. And Fisher inventing the masculine generic *he* with one hand while opening a school for girls with the other is the kind of biographical contradiction that can't be invented.

### Major concerns

The Jeremy Taylor section is in trouble. There's a comma-orphaned sentence at line 108 ("\, he became a chaplain to King Charles I") that suggests this was patched together from notes. Worse, the prose around Taylor reads like LLM expansion: "His most famous work\dots became spiritual classics and influenced generations of Anglican believers." That's a Wikipedia introduction, not a chapter in a trade book. The prose then begins to repeat itself: lines 116-130 paraphrase the same insight (Taylor used "ungrammatical" semantically, not formally) four times, with adjective rotation. A reader will notice. Cut it down to one paragraph.

The chapter also can't decide what kind of book it is. Three quite different chapter-openers fight inside it: (1) the Ross/Chomsky island story, (2) the history of English grammars from Bullokar to Lowth, and (3) the model of grammaticality with its 13 numbered sub-points. The model section in particular is structurally jarring. It belongs in an appendix, or as the spine of a much later chapter. Right now it sits in chapter 1 like a textbook page glued into a memoir.

On the protagonist question: Chomsky and Ross enter as protagonists who "discovered something new." They are heroes of curiosity. The prescriptivist grammarians (Lowth, Brown) are treated more ambivalently, with Brown getting room to mock his rivals (good) but no inner life. Where is Lowth, the bishop writing a grammar for his four-year-old son and accidentally producing the most influential English grammar ever? That is a story. A father trying to teach a small boy to write properly. The chapter mentions it in one clause and moves on.

### Priority fixes

1. Cut the Taylor section to one paragraph. Fix the orphaned comma.
2. Move "The Model of Grammaticality" with its 13 numbered points to a later structural chapter or an appendix.
3. The Herbie Hancock passage should not be quarantined to one section. Reference it back at the end of the chapter, or use it as a structuring device.
4. Decide how this chapter relates to Chapter 2. Right now they may overlap on gradient grammaticality.

### One concrete suggestion

Open with Hancock at the keyboard. "I played the wrong chord. A chord that just sounded completely wrong." Cut to him with hands over his ears. Cut to Miles Davis, who plays notes that make Hancock's mistake correct. Then ask: was it a mistake or wasn't it? Then bring in the asterisk and Ross. The reader has now been told, in a scene with stakes and a body, what the rest of the chapter is going to argue. Save Lowth's bishop-and-his-son story as the epigraph for the prescriptivism chapter.
