# Packaging-board summary (Becker)

## Cross-chapter patterns

The book has the bones of a serious argument and, in places, the prose to match. The strongest passages are the empirical investigations Brett conducts himself: the missing *whose* (chapter 5), *probable to play* and the NFL injury reports (chapter 10), the *very*/*much* and *moche*/*much* historical tracing (chapter 12), the *Rubymar* infinitival-perfect collaboration with Pullum (chapter 15), the *long long road* / *the road is long long* puzzle (chapter 21). When the chapter is doing this kind of work, the book persuades. A generative reader sees careful evidence handling, real corpus work, primary-source attention.

The weakest passages are LLM-padded surveys: the seven-frameworks ethics chapter (4), the redundant cognitive-science frameworks in chapter 5, the long Sumatra geography in chapter 9, the closing paragraphs of the *do*-support chapter in 12, and the world-tour-of-*whose* in chapter 15. These passages lose generative readers fast. The rule of thumb: when the chapter has Brett's voice in it, it works; when it has LLM voice, it doesn't.

## Where disciplinary politics work

Chapters 1, 2, 5, 7, 10, 19 mostly succeed at letting a generative reader stay in the room. They take the field's data seriously, they cite the canonical papers, they engage the strongest version of the position before disagreeing. Chapter 1 opens by celebrating Ross's island discoveries; chapter 7 quotes McCawley turning on Chomsky from inside the field rather than outside; chapter 19 honours Futrell's efficiency program; chapter 5's *whose* investigation respects Hankamer-Postal's intuitions before showing they were incomplete. These are textbook Becker moves: persuade by understanding first.

## Where disciplinary politics sabotage uptake

Chapter 3's "1970s linguists with bell-bottoms" frame is a cheap shot. Chapter 4's "elitist power" and "social justice by any means necessary" labels strawman both sides of a serious debate. Chapter 6's blindness-as-metaphor passage tilts politically without acknowledging it. Chapter 9's Riau Indonesian / creole-exceptionalism claims don't flag the controversy. Chapter 17 on impossible languages punts on the strongest UG arguments. Chapter 20 ("Getting grammaticality wrong") is empty when it should be the book's most disarming chapter.

The pattern: when the book is doing empirical work, it respects the tribe. When it's stating positions, it slides toward in-group signalling for a non-generative readership. A generative reader feels these moments and disengages.

## The single repositioning repair

Move the book's stated thesis to the end and let the chapters do the persuading.

Right now the central model of grammaticality (the 14-point list, currently buried at section 1.5) appears in chapter 1 before the reader has any reason to accept its terms. A generative reader meeting that list reads: "you've already decided, you're going to spend twenty chapters proving it." Move the model to chapter 20 or chapter 21. Let chapters 1 through 19 raise the question, lay out the empirical cases (the strong investigations the book already contains), let the reader notice the pattern, and only then state the model. That single restructure does three things: it lets the empirical chapters carry the argument, it makes the imagined reader an investigator rather than a captive audience, and it puts the book's most contestable claims after the work that earns them. A generative reader who reaches chapter 20 having seen *whose*, *probable to play*, *very/much*, *Rubymar*, the Hollies puzzle, and the Pereira refutation of Chomsky's bigram claim — that reader will grant the model. The same reader, given the model in chapter 1, will spend twenty chapters waiting to argue.
