## Chapter 21: A long, long way

### Strengths
This is one of the strongest chapters in the book. The reduplicative-intensification phenomenon is a small, sharp, well-motivated puzzle, and the chapter solves it with a satisfying historical argument plus cross-linguistic confirmation.

The Pullum / Turkish-woman / pope anecdote is excellent: a near-zero-English speaker produces *very very good* and the chapter uses it to argue calquing from Turkish *çok çok* and to show that intensifying reduplication is a low-effort cross-linguistic resource.

The cross-linguistic survey (English, Japanese, Russian) at lines 46-70 is exactly the right format: same construction, three languages, paired (a) attributive (b) predicative, all with the predicative starred. This is paired-comparison evidence-display done right. The Japanese and Russian examples are properly glossed.

The historical argument (Old English compound *burggeat*, Middle English declension loss, 1579 *small little* attested, 1600s *long long* taking off) is well-paced and lands a real explanation.

The Jamaican Creole *blakblak* and North-East Ambae *qi-qilodo* counterexamples (lines 222-234) are doing important work: they show that the pattern isn't universal, only sociolinguistically dominant. This is anti-Universal-Grammar evidence delivered with surgical precision.

The reduplicative-clarification phenomenon (the 1970s *right-right* construction, line 248) is the kind of footnote-worthy detail that makes the chapter feel alive.

### Major concerns
The chapter has \chapter and \section both labelled "A long long way" (lines 1 and 3). Section duplicates chapter title.

The two DALL-E images (figures/black-birds.png at \ref{fig:black-birds} and figures/DALL·E-burggeat.png at \ref{fig:burggeat}) are decoration. Neither does analytical work. The black-bird-vs-blackbird distinction is a structural, not a visual, point: a blackbird is brown when female, a raven is black-bird-but-not-blackbird; the cartoon doesn't show this. The burggeat image is a generic medieval-village stock illustration and doesn't show the bracketing [[burg+geat]+setl] argument the chapter makes.

The Old English declension table (lines 156-165) and its Modern English flattened counterpart (171-181) are a good paired display, but they could be merged into one side-by-side table to make the loss-of-distinctions visible at a glance.

The chapter doesn't connect explicitly to Chapter 1's ordering-of-discrete-combination argument or Chapter 19's dependency-distance work, both of which are relevant.

The two pre-existing tikz dependency arc drawings from Chapter 19 would help here too: the *long long* puzzle is a dependency-locality / structural-position argument and goes naturally with the Chapter 19 vocabulary.

### One concrete suggestion
Build a single comparative figure called "Where can you double an adjective?"

A 4-language by 2-position grid:
Rows: English, Japanese, Russian, Jamaican Creole, North-East Ambae, Turkish.
Columns: attributive (pre-head modifier in NP) | predicative (after the verb).

Cells: example with reduplicated adjective, with a checkmark (grammatical) or asterisk (ungrammatical). Use the existing examples already on the page.

Below this grid, a small inset historical timeline for English specifically:
- 800-1100 (Old English): no two-adjective NPs at all. Compound nouns only (*micel burggeat*).
- 1100-1500 (Middle English): declension loss; first two-adjective NPs appear.
- 1579: first reduplicative-intensification (*small little red flowers*).
- 1600s: *long long* attested.
- 1970s: clarificational reduplication (*right-right*) appears.

The reader sees in one display:
- The pattern is not universal (Jamaican / Ambae break it).
- The pattern is socially / historically established, not innate.
- English's predicative gap is one symptom of a broader historical contingency.

The chapter's argument is a genuinely good one and would benefit from one consolidating figure. Right now the cross-linguistic data is in three separate examples and the historical sweep is in three separate paragraphs. One figure draws them together.

This is a good place for the book to end: a small puzzle, a clear analysis, a cross-linguistic confirmation, a satisfying historical resolution. The chapter is structurally one of the cleanest in the book.
