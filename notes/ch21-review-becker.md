## Chapter 21: A long, long way

### Strengths
This is one of the chapter's strongest closing chapters could be — and in places, is. The reduplicative-intensification puzzle (*a long long road* fine, *the road is long long* not) is a real, narrow, surprising observation. The Hollies lyric pair is a charming way to set it up: the same word, repeated, fine in one verse and impossible in the next. A non-specialist reader has the puzzle handed to them in a form they immediately understand, and that lets the chapter do its real work.

The cross-linguistic data is well-chosen. Japanese (*nagai nagai michi* / *michi ga nagai nagai*) and Russian (*krasivye glaza* / *yeyo glaza krasivye*) both confirm the pattern, lending it the air of universality. Then the chapter flips: Jamaican Creole *im want wan we blakblak* and North-East Ambae *qi-qilodo* both allow predicative reduplicative intensification. So the universal isn't universal. That's a beautiful structure for an argument.

The Pullum-Turkish-woman story (line 22) is wonderful and exactly the kind of empirical anecdote the book's argument needs. A near-zero-English speaker producing *very very good* about the Pope. The chapter then proposes a sensible Turkish-calquing explanation. That's the kind of careful reading that earns trust.

The historical investigation, *micel blæc geat* and the Old English compound-noun loophole that allowed multiple pre-head modifiers to creep in over time, is well-argued. The chapter is doing real diachronic linguistics: it shows how the asymmetry between pre-head and post-head positions arose contingently, not as a logical necessity.

The closing paragraph "we're not programmed to prefer one kind of duplication over the other. We're socialized to do so" (line 236) is the chapter's punchline and exactly the right note to end the book on. It is a clean statement of the book's overall argument applied to one specific case.

### Major concerns
The chapter title and section labels are uncoordinated. The chapter is "A long, long way" with a redundant "\section{A long long way}" at line 3. Clean up.

The discussion of the international students (lines 26 and 240) is good but the chapter doesn't quite return to it cleanly at the end. The closing observation that "you simply don't find more than one adjective after *is*" (line 244) is interesting but doesn't quite connect to the broader argument. *She's nice good* is also bad, but for a different reason than *she's good good*; the chapter should distinguish.

The Old English / Middle English transition is rich but at points overdetailed for a trade book. The four-cell case-table at line 158 (singular/plural × nominative/accusative/genitive/dative) is more morphological detail than a non-linguist reader needs. Trim or move to a footnote / sidebar.

The Gonzalez-Diaz 2018 reference at line 248 introduces a new kind of reduplication ("contrastive focus reduplication," sometimes called salad-salad reduplication) very briefly. *I love you, but not love-love you* is a real phenomenon and could anchor a paragraph; right now it's a single sentence at the end of the chapter. Either develop or remove.

The chapter is the closing chapter and so does double duty: it's both an empirical case study and a place for the book to land its overall argument. Right now it's mostly the first, with the closing thesis-line ("socialized, not programmed") tucked into the end of section 21.3. The book deserves a closing few paragraphs that draw the threads of all twenty-one chapters together.

The "\subsection{In other languages}" section is the right place for the chapter's payoff, but the payoff comes too quietly. The chapter has earned the right, by the end, to make the book's central HPC claim explicitly. Consider: "throughout this book, we've seen that 'ungrammatical' is not one thing but a cluster of things — what's wrong, what's off, what's unconventional, what's foreign, what's profane, what's outdated, what's stigmatized, what's processing-difficult. The Hollies lyric is one tiny window into that cluster." Right now the chapter doesn't quite say this.

### Priority fixes
- Fix the title/section duplication at lines 1-3.
- Trim the Old English declension table or push it to a footnote.
- Develop the Gonzalez-Diaz contrastive-focus reduplication paragraph or remove it.
- Add a closing summary of the book's overall argument. Three or four paragraphs. The chapter has earned them.
- Source-ground the Jamaican Creole and North-East Ambae examples more carefully (verify the citations).
- The international-students vignette could be developed more. They laugh at *the road is long long* but produce *she good good*. That's the chapter's most direct evidence and it's tucked in awkwardly.

### One concrete suggestion
Use the chapter as the closing chapter it should be. Open with the Hollies, walk through the cross-linguistic puzzle, then the contingent-history move (Old English / Middle English, multiple-pre-head-adjective creep), then the Jamaican-Creole/Ambae counterevidence to universality, then a closing two-page reflection on what the whole book has been about. The book has spent twenty-one chapters arguing that "ungrammatical" is a cluster phenomenon, that grammaticality and ungrammaticality are emergent and social, that the same word in different contexts does different work. The closing chapter is the place to say that out loud, and to thank the reader for staying with the argument.

A generative reader who has been pushed back on for twenty-one chapters arrives at chapter 21 ready for the book's most direct statement of position. Currently chapter 21 gives them a lovely case study and a quiet single-sentence punchline. Give them the punchline as a paragraph, then a closing image. That's how a book of this kind earns its final page.

If I had to nominate one place in the book to add a single new thing, it would be at the end of this chapter: a 500-word closing reflection that says, in the author's own voice, what the book has been arguing. Becker would want that. The reader has earned it.
