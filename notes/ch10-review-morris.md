## Chapter 10: Real patterns

### Strengths

The Bert Bell scene is the chapter's gold. A 1946 NFL bribery and gambling scandal. The commissioner sees that inside information about player injuries is poisoning betting markets. He cannot stop the gambling, so he mandates weekly injury reports with three categories: probable, questionable, doubtful. Reynolds connects this institutional history to the appearance of *probable to play* in *The Los Angeles Times* in 1986, then traces it across sports media. That's a beautifully specific trajectory: a single 1947 bureaucratic decision rewires English so that one adjective acquires a complement structure another similar adjective does not have. The story has a date, a person, a scandal, and a linguistic consequence. It is the chapter's argument made concrete.

This is also the chapter's best example of how to read Hurford charitably while disagreeing with him. Reynolds notes that Hurford's claim (\textit{probable} cannot take a *to*-infinitival) was true when Hurford wrote it, may be true now for most speakers, but is not true for sports announcers. That's the right move. It treats Hurford as a serious thinker who got the facts mostly right, but who missed the bureaucratic story behind the exception. No villainy. Honest miss.

The Amelie/Basil/Clara grammar example also works. The cross-linguistic comparison with Azerbaijani and Hixkaryana is brisk and effective.

### Major concerns

This chapter has the most painful surgery scar of any in the book. Lines 101-113 are pure LLM output: a numbered list of "what a functionalist would say," with paragraphs that read "Functionalists emphasize the role of language use in shaping grammar" and "In summary, a functionalist would likely address Hurford's challenge by considering factors such as\dots". It is exactly the format ChatGPT produces when asked "How would a functionalist respond to Hurford?" That entire section needs to be deleted. Reynolds himself makes the better argument in his own voice in lines 53-65 (the diachronic Old Norse vs Old French story).

The "long tail" digression at lines 92-95 reads like a Wikipedia paragraph dropped in. Anderson's *Wired* article is from 2004; the analogy to micro-grammar is fine, but the prose ("the term \textsc{long tail} refers to the strategy of businesses that sell\dots") is encyclopedia voice, not Reynolds.

The chapter has a paragraph that ends with "\textit{Amelie}, \textit{Clara}, \textit{Basil}, and \textit{showed}, plus ." (line 9). The trailing "plus ." is unfinished.

Footnote 53 is a beautifully Canadian touch: thanks to Michael Dipetta for the NHL injury convention. *Matthews is out with an elbow*. That's exactly the kind of detail that makes a trade book live. Promote it from footnote.

On the protagonists question: Bert Bell is the chapter's hero by accident. He shows up for two paragraphs, makes a 1947 decision, then disappears. Reynolds could give him a face. Bell was a colorful man (Bert Bell, son of Pennsylvania politician John C. Bell, married to a Ziegfeld Follies dancer, lost his fortune in the Crash, ended up commissioning the NFL through the Depression). All of that human texture is sitting on the table. Use it. The argument here is "linguistic patterns are partly downstream of bureaucratic decisions made by people"; the chapter only really lands if we see the person.

Hurford the antagonist is treated honorably. The chapter doesn't dunk on him; it disagrees specifically. This is one of the better-handled scholarly disagreements in the manuscript and is a model for how the book should treat its other interlocutors (Chomsky, Postal, Sampson, the prescriptivists).

### Priority fixes

1. Delete the LLM-generated functionalist response (lines 101-113).
2. Cut or rewrite the long-tail Wikipedia paragraph.
3. Finish the trailing "plus ." sentence.
4. Promote the NHL injury-naming detail from footnote to body text.
5. Decide whether to give Bert Bell a paragraph of human texture.

### One concrete suggestion

Open the chapter with the 1946 championship game. Two New York Giants players have been suspended. The commissioner is sweating in his office. Bert Bell looks at his options: he can't stop the betting markets, but he can starve them of asymmetric information. He picks three words: probable, questionable, doubtful. None of them yet meant what it would later mean. Then move forty years forward. *The Los Angeles Times*, November 18, 1986. A column by Mark Heisler. Sentence: *the Raiders had him listed as probable to play against the Browns.* The grammar of English has taken a small step. Not because of poets or philosophers, but because of one man's bureaucratic memo. That's the chapter. Keep it tight, keep the people specific, drop the LLM functionalist riff.
