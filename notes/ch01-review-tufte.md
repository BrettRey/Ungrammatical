## Chapter 01: Stories from syntactic islands

### Strengths
The cow figure (figures/cow.png) is one of the rare visual elements in this book that earns its space, but I'll qualify that below. The two syntax trees for tearful-farmer vs. tearful-cow are exactly the kind of small, paired display I would commend: a structural difference shown in two minimally different graphs. The numbered example sequences (\ref{ex:extraction-intro}, \ref{ex:island-intro}, \ref{ex:non-island}, \ref{ex:non-island-deep}) are typographically aligned to make the gap "_____" track across rows. This is small-multiples thinking, and it is the right approach.

### Major concerns
The cow figure is doing less work than the chapter claims. The DALL-E split-panel image shows two scenes (a farmer in front of a giant cow, and the cow with a tear) but the tearful-cow vs. tearful-farmer distinction is hard to read off the panels at thumbnail size. The PP-attachment ambiguity is what the chapter wants the reader to *see*, and the syntax trees do that job. The cartoon is decoration.

The "explanations" section (lines 190-216) is a textbook-style bullet list of fields (math, physics, biology, psychology). This is an opportunity for a real Tufte table that shows, for each field, the puzzle, its current best explanation, and what makes it satisfying. Instead it's prose padding.

Roughly twenty paragraphs (lines 60-130) on Jeremy Taylor, Ann Fisher, Robert Lowth, Goold Brown read like LLM-padded biography. The reader needs the asterisk's history compressed to a timeline, not narrated chronologically. Especially the closing 10 paragraphs about Taylor (110-130) read as recursively summarising the same point.

The "model of grammaticality" subsection (304-396) is fourteen densely numbered prose items. This is the central scaffold of the entire book and it is presented as a wall of nested enumerate. It is illegible.

### Priority fixes
1. Replace the cow cartoon with annotated trees alone, *or* keep the cartoon but add tiny coloured arrows on each panel showing where the PP "with a tear in his eye" attaches. Make the figure carry its caption.
2. Convert the model of grammaticality (304-396) from prose enumeration to a single one-page diagram (see suggestion).
3. Compress the Taylor/Fisher/Lowth/Brown history into a one-page timeline plate: x-axis 1600-1900, y-axis grammars per decade or notable figures, with annotated callouts. Keep the prose for one or two arresting characters (Fisher being plagiarised, Brown's "fudge!"). Cut the rest.

### One concrete suggestion
The grammaticality model needs a foldout-style single diagram. Sketch:

A central horizontal axis labelled "judgement," running from "fully grammatical" on the left to "categorically blocked (architectural)" on the far right. Above the axis, vertical drop lines mark example types: form-meaning mismatch, processing failure, registers, satiated rare types, LBC violations. Below the axis, three coloured horizontal bands show where reanalysis can reach: "semantic motivation" (covers most of the axis), "social motivation" (covers most), "structural motivation" (covers a narrower middle region), and a black bar at the rightmost end labelled "architectural -- no satiation reaches here."

Each example from the 14-item list (\textit{I have 35 years}, \textit{come here}, \textit{The bread the baker the apprentice helped}, etc.) gets a single tick mark on the axis with a tiny label.

The reader sees, on one page: where any sentence falls on the gradient, why, and what kind of pressure could shift it. The fourteen-item enumeration reduces to a single visual reference that the rest of the book can call back to.
