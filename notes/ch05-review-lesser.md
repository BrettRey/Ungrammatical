## Chapter 05: How grammar feels

### Strengths

This chapter contains the book's deepest material. The "Whose gorilla?" investigation is exactly the kind of detective story trade-book readers love: a 1973 squib, a stuck judgment, an LLM consulted on a whim, friends and family texted, the OED checked, web searches yielding ten naturalistic examples, and finally a Wikipedia revision. There's a real plot here, and a real intellectual humility (the linguists, including the great ones, may have been wrong; or at least, more wrong than the LLMs).

The Sasha-and-Mia singular-they passage is the chapter's emotional peak. Reynolds describes his own ungrammatical reaction to a person he knows and loves's chosen pronoun ("ballistic," he calls it), and the metaphor of the Stroop test (color names printed in mismatched colors) is exactly right. He doesn't pretend to a position he doesn't hold; he reports the friction and explains why he can't dissolve it. That's good writing.

The squirrel-monkey study and the rock hyrax detour are also genuinely interesting, and they feed the chapter's larger claim that grammaticality intuitions repurpose older social-recognition machinery. The Tower of Babel reframing ("God didn't just divide people by making them speak differently, he divided them by making their grammars incompatible") is striking.

The Pullum-as-shop-assistant-piano-player biography is one of the book's small treasures.

### Major concerns

The chapter is twice as long as it should be, and it shows. Three full sections share the same general territory (the Bayesian/predictive-processing account, the Barrett constructed-emotion account, the neuroscientific-perspective account), and the treatments overlap so thoroughly that the chapter loops on itself.

Worse: subsection 4.5 ("Reinterpreting grammatical intuitions through a constructionist lens") is reproduced verbatim twice. Lines 701-715 and 717-731 are identical paragraphs. This is not a stylistic problem; it's a draft hygiene problem.

Worse still: the chapter ends with raw ChatGPT output ("The following is from chatGPT: Modeling the brain as a system that calculates vectors..."). Bullet headers like "1. Representation of Emotions as Vectors" and "Vector Components:" are not English-prose paragraphs and have no place in a Language Science Press book.

There is also a stretch (lines 410-433) of pure LLM filler: a string of literary epigraphs that the chapter doesn't earn or use. "I can't go on. I'll go on." "Memory believes before knowing remembers." Faulkner, Beckett, Pascal, Orwell, Hamlet, Pope, all dropped in without commentary.

Two passages titled "What does (un)grammaticality feel like 1?" and "What does ungrammaticality feel like 2?" suggest the author was trying alternate openings and forgot to choose. Pick one.

The "Pulling off his boxer briefs" example from Fifty Shades is reused in two different sections of the same chapter.

The Bayesian formalism (with the cat and milk and equations) is overwrought. It's three pages to make a one-paragraph point.

The Faulkner sentence "He knew only that his child was his warrant" appears with no setup and no follow-through. It's a beautiful sentence belonging to a different book.

The "double-is" / ISIS passage is good but is partially duplicated in commented-out form.

### Priority fixes

1. Choose ONE theoretical framing for the feeling of grammaticality. The Bayesian/predictive-processing account is the strongest. Cut Barrett (it's redundant with predictive processing) and the standalone neuroscientific-perspective section (likewise).
2. Delete the verbatim-duplicated subsection 4.5.
3. Delete the ChatGPT vector-space output entirely. It's not a draft; it's contamination.
4. Cut the Faulkner-Beckett-Pascal epigraph cascade.
5. Choose ONE "what does it feel like" section. Keep the Proust madeleine, drop the second Proust quote (yes, both passages quote it).
6. Tighten the Bayesian example. The reader doesn't need three equations; they need the intuition.
7. Decide what the "form-meaning mismatch" section is doing here. The duplicated draft fragment ("Certainly. I'll build out the two examples at the end... latexCopy\section{...}") is left over from the LLM exchange and must be deleted.

### One concrete suggestion

Worst sentence: "These boundaries work in subtle ways. Indian English speakers say \textit{I am having two books} naturally. Many other English speakers find this wrong." The example is fine; the framing sentence ("These boundaries work in subtle ways") is a piece of throat-clearing the chapter doesn't need. But the worst single sentence in the chapter is the leftover prompt artifact: "Certainly. I'll build out the two examples at the end, expanding on the concepts of the past tense and definiteness to illustrate the form-meaning mismatch in grammar. Here's an improved version:" That is not a sentence. It is an LLM acknowledging instructions. Delete on sight.

Best sentence: "I can learn a new rule for \textit{they}, but it's a clunky and artificial kludge slapped on top of what is an exquisitely tuned system, one that can do no better than to try to funnel the champagne into a different bottle as it foams forth." This is the chapter's conscience speaking. The metaphor is unexpected, accurate (champagne foaming into the wrong bottle), and humanly honest about where the speaker stands. The book needs more of this voice and far less of the Bayesian-explainer voice.
