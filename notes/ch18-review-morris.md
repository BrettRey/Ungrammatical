## Chapter 18: Swearing

### Strengths

The chapter's central question is the right one and is pitched in the right register: "Is swearing a norm violation because it is obscene, like a couple lustily copulating in a McDonalds booth or a pile of shit on the floor of an elevator, or because it is ungrammatical?" That sentence has comic specificity, the McDonalds detail does work, and the question is non-trivial. There is a real argument to be made that swearing's wrongness in some contexts is grammar-shaped, not morality-shaped, and that the chapter could anatomize that distinction.

The two epigraphs (uncited but clearly from Margaret Atwood, *The Handmaid's Tale*) are atmospheric and well-chosen for a chapter about who is allowed to say what.

### Major concerns

This is the third stub Reynolds flagged in the prompt (along with chapter 11 neurolinguistics and chapter 20 getting grammaticality wrong). Fifteen lines, no chapter heading, no opening, no engagement with the literature, no concrete instances. The prompt asked me to note the gap and sketch what human story or human moment would belong.

What's missing:

1. A real moment of swearing-as-membership. The chapter says "those within the circle have mastered this rebellious grammar," but doesn't show one. A specific scene from a hockey locker room. A specific moment between two close friends. A specific overheard exchange.

2. A real moment of swearing-as-violation. The chapter says "that same word unleashed against a stranger is an attack." Show the attack. Where? When? What did the listener feel? What did the speaker think they were doing?

3. Engagement with Benjamin Bergen's *What the F*. Bergen is on the advisory board list for exactly this chapter. His book is the contemporary canonical treatment of the psycholinguistics of swearing. There are also the Mohr (*Holy Sh*t*), Hughes (*An Encyclopedia of Swearing*), and Bowers and Pleydell-Pearce experimental work on autonomic-nervous-system response to taboo words. None is here.

4. A real grammar-of-swearing observation. The most interesting linguistic facts about swearing are the syntactic ones: *abso-fucking-lutely* (expletive infixation, with metrical-foot constraints), *the fuck out of him* (degree intensifier with restricted host), *fucking ridiculous* vs. *ridiculous fucking*, the different grammatical behaviors of *shit* (mass) vs. *piece of shit* (count), and so on. The chapter's argument that swearing has its own grammar would land hard if the grammar were shown.

5. Cross-cultural data. Different languages swear about different domains: English about sex and excrement, Quebec French about the Catholic Church, Japanese without much tradition of swearing at all. That's a chapter spine.

6. The Bergen finding that swearing is robust to aphasia: aphasic patients who can produce almost no fluent speech can still produce fluent expletives, suggesting that swearing is processed differently from other language. That's a chapter spine.

On the protagonists question: there is currently no protagonist in this chapter. Not a swearer, not a target, not a researcher. The chapter is all in the third-person abstract ("those within the circle"). It's a chapter waiting for a person.

### One concrete suggestion

Open with one specific aphasia patient. A neurologist's case file. A man with massive left-hemisphere damage who can produce almost no fluent speech. He says "yes," "no," and roughly fifteen swear words. Researchers ask him to count to ten; he can't. They ask him to name everyday objects; he can't. They ask him how he feels; he says, fluently, "fucking awful." The split between his lost-language and his preserved-swearing is the chapter's deep argument, that swearing is not just a register choice but a different kind of object in the brain. From there, the chapter can move outward to the social uses (camaraderie and attack), the syntactic structure (abso-fucking-lutely), and the cross-cultural variation (Quebec religion, Japanese reticence). The aphasia opening earns the rest.

If Reynolds prefers a non-clinical opening, the alternative is the moment Lenny Bruce was arrested in Greenwich Village in 1964 for using "obscene" language. A real moment, a real comedian, a real American obscenity prosecution. That scene also opens the chapter.
