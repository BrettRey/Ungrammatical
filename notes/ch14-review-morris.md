## Chapter 14: Grammar and glamour

### Strengths

This is the chapter where the book first finds its trade-book voice. Three short subsections, each anchored to a real, specific, named person doing a specific small linguistic thing on a specific date. Cory Doctorow on *Boing Boing* in 2012 writing "even it's never approved." Reynolds emails him; Doctorow replies "Yup. It's colloquial, but expressive, unambiguous, and colorful." That email exchange, dated July 6, 2012, is a real moment in real time between two men. The chapter then uses it to make a real argument: that a proficient speaker of English can innovate, but only if the innovation does some work. Then it tests the argument: had Doctorow written *even if he's never approve*, the same kind of "deviation," it would have been recognized as a typo. The asymmetry between innovation and error is the book's argument boiled to ten lines.

Then the tracksuit analogy. Friedman in the Times notes that the tracksuit was unfashionable in 2000, then re-emerged. The pairing with grammatical innovation is exact. The detail "wear it with a sweatband and you look like a victim of the endless cycle of New Year's workout resolutions" is specific enough to land.

Then *enshittification*. Reynolds anatomizes the morphology, suffix by suffix, demonstrating that Doctorow's coinage stacked four affixes plus a redundant *en-* prefix. The argument that the redundant prefix is "a masterstroke in neologism, enhancing the word's dramatic flair" is the kind of sentence a trade book about grammar should be writing.

This is the first chapter in the manuscript that mostly sounds like Reynolds, mostly sticks to specifics, and mostly stays out of LLM-padding territory.

### Major concerns

The chapter is short (63 lines) and that's not a problem, but the three subsections feel like three short essays rather than a chapter with a spine. The connecting argument (innovation works when it has a goal and a reference; failure-to-innovate is just error) is right at the start of subsection one but is not reinforced by the time the chapter closes on *enshittification*.

There's a structural oddity: the chapter has only `\subsection{}` headings, no `\section{}`. That probably means the file was patched in from another chapter's draft.

Doctorow appears as a character but a thin one. He's a blogger, journalist, SF author, "somebody really tapped into the social zeitgeist." That phrasing is the chapter's lone moment of generic prose. Doctorow has a real life worth a sentence: a Canadian-British science fiction writer with a long history of digital-rights advocacy, a co-author of *Boing Boing*, a man who has been writing about platforms-going-bad for two decades. Two more specific sentences would land him.

On the protagonists question: this chapter does the cleanest job in the book of treating its subjects as honest reasonable people. Doctorow is a creative innovator, not a mistake-maker. The English-language students who say "even it's never approved" are not idiots; they're learners doing something Doctorow does deliberately, just without his motive. The asymmetry between *learner error* and *deliberate innovation* is treated with care. This is the model the rest of the book should follow when discussing prescriptivists, generative semanticists, AAVE speakers, or anyone else.

There's no antagonist here. That's fine.

### Priority fixes

1. Decide whether this should have section-level structure rather than just subsections.
2. Tighten the connecting tissue between the three vignettes. One sentence at each transition, naming the through-line.
3. Add two specific sentences about Doctorow as a person (briefly, not biographically).
4. Consider whether this chapter should come earlier in the book. It is currently chapter 14, but its argument (innovation requires intent and a reference point) is needed by chapter 3 or so.

### One concrete suggestion

The chapter has a missing fourth movement: a real moment where an innovation didn't take. Doctorow's *even-as-conditional* probably won't catch on; *enshittification* did. The tracksuit fashion comeback succeeded in 2024; many other "comebacks" don't. What separates the innovations that take from the ones that don't? Reynolds gestures toward this in subsection one ("a grammatical innovation that fails to do these things is ungrammatical") but doesn't give the reader a concrete failed innovation. The reader needs to see one. Maybe an attempt to revive *thou*, or an invented gender-neutral pronoun (*ze, xe*), or a Doctorow innovation that didn't catch. Without the failure case, the chapter looks like it's celebrating only successes. With one, the chapter becomes a real anatomy of when grammar takes glamour.
