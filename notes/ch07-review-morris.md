## Chapter 07: The Generation Gap

### Strengths

The chapter has the simplest job of any in the book (introduce generative grammar to a non-linguist) and it almost gets the basic mechanics right. The dust-storm example works as a small worked exercise. *A dust storm* combines into a unit, *cosmic* can combine with *dust storm* but not with *a dust storm* once *a* is locked in, and the reader gets a feel for how compositionality cares about order. That's a real concrete moment.

The Culicover counterexample (*likely* / *probable* / *to be elected president*) is also good. It does a thing the book has not done much: it stops the abstract machinery and shows you a place where the machinery breaks. *Robin is likely to be elected* but *Robin is probable to be elected* is ungrammatical. Why? The chapter signals that this is the kind of brick that breaks a system aiming for "all and only the grammatical strings."

The McCawley quotation in the *New Yorker* is the chapter's one human moment. McCawley telling a journalist that Postal and Lakoff and he think Chomsky's whole framing is "not a coherent notion" is the sound of a real argument among real people in 1972. The reader hears voices.

### Major concerns

This chapter is a stub. Forty-two lines, and the title promises "The Generation Gap" (a smart play on word "generation" in generative grammar plus the late-1960s fight). The chapter doesn't deliver on either the technical or the human side. It doesn't explain what generative grammar is in enough detail to make a non-linguist comfortable. It doesn't tell the story of the Linguistics Wars. It doesn't show what was at stake personally for the people involved. It doesn't even close: section 41 ("Generational failures") is two sentences and a Boeckx quote.

On the protagonists question: this is the chapter where the book most needs to decide how to treat Chomsky. He is the central antagonist of the trade-book's HPC argument, but he is also one of the most consequential figures in twentieth-century linguistics, and a man whose work changed how everyone in the field thinks. Right now the chapter has him as a faceless ideologue: "Chomsky and his supporters" vs. "the generative semanticists." We get one sentence that says "Chomsky's former students" declared the whole idea incoherent. That is the human story (a teacher's students mutinying), and the chapter walks past it.

Postal, McCawley, Lakoff, Ross. Each has a real life, a real career arc, real losses. Lakoff in particular went on from generative semantics to *Metaphors We Live By* and to political linguistics, his career taking the long way around the original wound. Ross became the discoverer of islands and ended up in a kind of academic exile. The book has already mentioned Ross in chapter 1. Bring him back here, in the room where the Linguistics War broke.

This is also where the book has to decide whether it presents Chomsky as villain, victim of his own framework, or honest mistake-maker. Right now, by omission, the chapter looks like it's leaning villain: the Linguistics Wars are summarized by his opponents' triumphal claim that his framework was incoherent. A documentary would not stop there. A documentary would film Chomsky in his MIT office, would show the cost the war had on him, would let him explain why he believed what he believed.

The Boeckx puzzle at the end is a strong move (island repair shouldn't happen in derivational approaches, and yet it does). But it's parachuted in. Without context, the trade reader has no idea why this is supposed to matter. It deserves a chapter, not a tail.

### Priority fixes

1. Decide what this chapter is doing. If it's the technical introduction to generative grammar, it needs another 8-10 pages and worked examples. If it's the human story of the Linguistics Wars, it needs scenes.
2. Write Chomsky as a person. Not necessarily as a hero, but at least as a man with a body, a voice, an actual office at MIT.
3. Bring Postal, McCawley, and Lakoff into the room. Use the *New Yorker* article (Shenker 1972) as a frame. What does it look like when former students publicly tell a journalist their teacher's framework is incoherent?
4. Either expand the Boeckx-island-repair point or move it to the chapter on islands.
5. Mark this in STATUS.md as a stub.

### One concrete suggestion

The book has a missing scene: the moment Chomsky learned his students were turning on him. There's a real one. In the late 1960s and early 1970s, generative semantics flared up at MIT, then broke. Reynolds doesn't have to invent dialogue; he has Randy Allen Harris's *The Linguistics Wars* (now in a second edition) full of letters, classroom recollections, and conference confrontations. Open the chapter with one specific scene from that history: a particular conference, a particular paper, a particular row. Then ask: is the technical disagreement a disagreement over what grammars do, or over what kind of object a grammar is? The chapter gets to its real subject (whether grammaticality is even a coherent notion) faster if a person is asking the question, not a textbook.
