## Chapter 20: Getting grammaticality wrong

### Strengths
The Old English examples are exactly the right kind of evidence for a chapter on this topic. Showing that English once didn't have a relative *whose*, and that the precursor was *þe his* ("that his") is the kind of fact that makes a non-linguist reader sit up. The Middle English transitional case at line 14 (*He had not one daughter who might his heir be*) shows the relative pronoun in its early modern form. These are real philological materials.

The chapter has a clear topic in its title ("Getting grammaticality wrong") and a clear topic in its first section ("Relative *whose*"). That is more focus than several other chapters have.

The chapter is currently a stub of about 26 lines.

### Major concerns
This chapter doesn't exist yet. It's a section opener with three glossed Old/Middle English examples and almost no prose. So the review is mostly about what the chapter should do.

The chapter title promises something specific: "Getting grammaticality wrong." This implies a chapter about how linguists, grammarians, or speakers misjudge grammaticality. The natural anchor is the whose-gorilla case from Chapter 5: Hankamer and Postal got it wrong; CGEL got it wrong; Brett's friends and family and the LLMs got it right. That's the chapter's natural spine.

The Old English material at the start can serve as historical scaffolding: the construction Hankamer and Postal claimed didn't exist actually evolved out of *þe his* via a different path than they assumed.

But the chapter needs more than just whose-gorilla. Other candidates for "getting grammaticality wrong":

1. **The Lowth-style prescriptive errors**: rules that grammarians invented and then wrongly enforced (split infinitives, sentence-final prepositions, *I* vs *me* in coordinations, *whom*).
2. **The 1970s Pullum/Henk-and-Trouble passive case** (already in chapter 3): a syntactic rule was promulgated and then disconfirmed.
3. **Sampson's claim that everything in a corpus is grammatical**: which "gets it wrong" by reduction to absurdity.
4. **The Riau Indonesian / Pirahã arguments about no embedding**: were they right or wrong?

Probably the chapter should pick two cases (the relative *whose* and one other) and treat them carefully.

### Priority fixes
- The chapter as written needs to be built out substantially.
- Connect the Old English material at the start to the chapter's argument. As is, it's three philology examples without a thesis.
- The whose-gorilla case is the natural spine. But careful: the case is already covered in chapter 5 and again in chapter 15. The book has now decided to retell this case three times. **Either decide that this chapter is the deep dive (and prune chapters 5 and 15) or pick a different anchor case for chapter 20.**
- Identify other cases of "getting grammaticality wrong." Lowth's pseudo-rules, the dangling-modifier prohibition, the passive prohibition.
- Tie the chapter to the book's broader argument: getting grammaticality wrong is unavoidable because grammaticality is gradient and contextual. The HPC view predicts that experts can be wrong about edge cases; rigid generative grammar struggles to admit the possibility.

### One concrete suggestion
Open with the diachronic story of *whose*: the construction now exists and is rare, but it didn't exist 1200 years ago, and what we have now is the result of a slow drift from *þe his*. So when Hankamer and Postal looked for *whose* and didn't find it, they were partly right (it's rare) and partly wrong (it exists). Then make the chapter's broader claim: linguists get grammaticality wrong because they trust their own intuitions, because they don't search corpora, because they overclaim from absence of evidence, and because their theories are better at exclusion than inclusion. Three case studies in increasing reach: (1) Whose-gorilla (Hankamer, Postal, CGEL); (2) The 1970s passive constraint (Pullum's Henk-and-Trouble case); (3) The dangling-modifier prohibition (Donaldson's argument that danglers are grammatical but bad-mannered). End with the book's claim that grammar is a homeostatic property cluster, and as such, it admits exceptions, edge cases, and rarity that defies a sharp grammatical/ungrammatical line.

### A note on the existing material
The Old English / Middle English examples at lines 4-26 are a strong start. They establish that the question "what's grammatical" depends on when you ask it, and they give the chapter's diachronic flavor. Keep all three examples; build the rest of the chapter from this foundation.

### Rhetoric that will work (in advance)
The OE example with *þe his* gives the chapter a strong opening: the construction is older than relative *whose*, and the path from one to the other is the kind of grammatical micro-history that makes the whole topic feel earned.

### Rhetoric to avoid (in advance)
The chapter must not turn into "see, even the experts get it wrong! Linguists are silly!" That would be unfair (Hankamer and Postal noticed something real, even if they overstated it) and it would damage the book's standing among the linguists who are part of its target audience. The right tone is the one Brett uses in chapter 5: "Top-notch syntacticians, legendary even." Charity first; correction second.
