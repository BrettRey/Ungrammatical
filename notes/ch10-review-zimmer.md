## Chapter 10: Real patterns

### Strengths

*Amelie showed Basil Clara* is a small masterclass. Within one sentence and three names, the chapter conveys (1) that English has a fixed pattern Agent-Verb-Recipient-Patient, (2) that other languages don't (Azerbaijani, Hixkaryana shown explicitly), (3) that position alone doesn't determine role (*Amelie was shown Basil*), and (4) that English compresses role-information into pattern. This is the kind of opening a science writer would kill for.

The Hixkaryana detail - 500 speakers on the Nhamundá River - is grounding without being padding. The reader knows where to put the language and how rare its order (OVS) actually is.

The *spray*/*load* alternation is a small linguistic gem. *She loaded the truck with hay* / *she loaded hay onto the truck* both work; *She soaked water onto the area* doesn't, even though *soak* and *spray* feel semantically similar. That's the kind of mystery a chapter like this should display.

The *probable to play* discovery is the chapter's best material and one of the book's best stretches. The 1946 NFL bribery scandal, Bert Bell mandating injury reports in 1947 with the three-tier "probable / questionable / doubtful," and *probable* picking up a *to*-infinitive license in sports media starting around 1986 (Mark Heisler's column in the LA Times) - this is a complete causal story for one piece of grammar. The footnote about NHL elbow-injury phrasings ("Matthews is out with an elbow") thanking Michael Dipetta gives the chapter a co-conspirator. That whole section is a model.

The historical *November the fifth* corpus search through EEBO is patient and the 1610 *february the twentith day* example as an intermediate form is exactly right.

### Major concerns

The chapter's payoff is that grammaticality and grammaticalization aren't the same, but the formulation is buried at the bottom: "the forces that cause grammaticalization are not the same as those that give us the feeling of grammaticality." That should be a thesis sentence near the top.

The "long tail" / Chris Anderson / *Wired* magazine paragraph is a digression. The *Wired* article and its 2006 book don't add to the linguistic argument. If the point is that LLMs can absorb micro-grammars that human computational systems struggled with, get there directly.

The closing functionalist enumeration (1. Semantic distinction, 2. Language use and conventionalization, 3. Gradience and acceptability, 4. Diachronic development, plus "Frequency and Entrenchment") is LLM-generated boilerplate. Each item is a paragraph of vague inference. Cut.

The transition into the *Amelie showed Basil Clara* opening has a typo - "plus a mere quartet of words... plus ." (line 9) - that suggests an unfinished thought.

The handoff from *probable to play* into Hurford's *November the fifth* discussion is abrupt. They are both arbitrary-conventionality cases, but the chapter doesn't explicitly tie them together as such.

### Priority fixes

1. Move the thesis sentence ("forces of grammaticalization differ from forces of grammaticality feeling") to the top of the chapter, possibly into the title.
2. Cut the long-tail aside or compress to one sentence.
3. Cut the functionalist enumeration at the end.
4. Tie *probable to play* and *November the fifth* together explicitly: both are micro-constructions whose grammaticality has a historical accident at its origin and a conventional payoff for its persistence.
5. Resolve the typo in the *Amelie* paragraph.

### One concrete suggestion

The 1946 Bert Bell story is the chapter's best anchor and could be even more vivid. The 1946 NFL Championship game was Giants vs. Bears, played at the Polo Grounds, with two Giants players (Frank Filchock and Merle Hapes) accused of failing to report a bribery attempt. The actual scene of Bell's announcement of the injury-report rule, the press reaction in 1947, and the eventual standardization across leagues - this is a great little narrative that bears directly on how a stuffy adjective like *probable* picked up a new syntactic life. Forty years later in a Mark Heisler LA Times column, the construction takes its first attested step into grammar. Trace that. Right now the chapter tells the story; with two more details it could let the reader watch a piece of grammar being born.
