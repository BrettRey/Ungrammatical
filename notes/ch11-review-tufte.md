## Chapter 11: Seeing grammar in the brain (neurolinguistics)

### Strengths
The chapter is a stub: the title is excellent ("Seeing grammar in the brain") and the two sentences here point at exactly the right kind of evidence: pupillometry (Just 1993) and heart-rate variability (Divjak 2024). These are concrete, measurable, body-side responses to grammaticality, which is what a sustained chapter on the neurobiology of grammaticality should be built around.

The Divjak finding noted in the stub is also interesting on its face: the HRV drop is larger when L1 accent makes the errors than when L2 accent does. That single contrast is the seed of a chapter.

### Major concerns
The chapter is eight lines and four sentences. There is nothing yet to review beyond intent.

The intended chapter title implies something about *visualising* grammar in the brain (fMRI, EEG, MEG, electrocorticography). None of that is here. The N400 / P600 ERP components, the perisylvian network, Broca's and Wernicke's areas, BOLD-signal studies of garden-path sentences, and the recent single-neuron predictive-coding work cited briefly in chapter 5 (around line 752 of ch05) are all candidates. None has yet been brought in.

There is no figure, no example, no body of literature reviewed.

The chapter is a placeholder. The other reviews in this series have flagged where the parent book makes claims about predictive-coding, ERP, or single-cell recordings that the neurolinguistics chapter ought to anchor. Right now those claims are scattered across chapters 5, 17, and 19 with no central neuroscientific core to point to.

### Priority fixes
1. Write the chapter. At a minimum it needs:
   - One section on ERP signatures of grammaticality violations (N400, P600, ELAN). These are already mentioned in chapter 5; consolidate here.
   - One section on what fMRI and lesion data say about which violations recruit which regions (prosodic vs. morphosyntactic vs. semantic). One annotated brain figure.
   - One section on the new wave of intracranial single-neuron work (the Nature 2024 study Brett cites in ch05, line 753).
   - One section on the body-side measures already named in this stub: pupillometry, HRV, skin conductance, reading-time eye-tracking.
2. Move all neuroscience claims now scattered through chapters 5, 17, and 19 into this chapter or signpost from them to here.

### One concrete suggestion
Build a single annotated figure called "What the body and brain do when grammar breaks."

A central left-side panel showing a schematic head/brain in lateral view, with marked regions: Broca's area, Wernicke's area, anterior temporal cortex, hippocampus. Each region tagged with the kind of violation it responds to most: morphosyntactic, semantic, integration, prediction-replay.

A right-side stack of four small horizontal time-series, all aligned on a common x-axis from 0 to 1000 ms post-stimulus:
1. Pupil diameter (Just 1993): rises slowly over 0-1000 ms, larger for harder sentences.
2. EEG: shows N400 dip at 400 ms for semantic anomaly.
3. EEG: shows P600 peak at 600 ms for syntactic anomaly.
4. HRV (Divjak 2024): drop centred at ~ 1500 ms, larger for L1-accent errors.

A small caption noting: the body responds to ungrammaticality on a millisecond-to-seconds timescale, with multiple measures agreeing on different aspects of the violation.

This would let the chapter say in one figure what would otherwise take ten pages of citation-stuffed review prose. The figure also builds a consistent visual reference that earlier chapters (especially 5) can call back to without retreading.

For now: the chapter is unwritten. The above is what should be drafted.
