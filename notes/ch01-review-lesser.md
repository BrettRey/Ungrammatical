## Chapter 01: Stories from syntactic islands

### Strengths

The opening conceit, that Chomsky pictured the islands before he found them, is a writer's gift. Most linguistics writing on islands begins with examples; Reynolds begins with the cartography. Good. The Tristan chord and the Herbie Hancock anecdote are both genuinely useful. The Hancock story does a great deal of work cheaply: Miles Davis as the linguistic community that retroactively makes a "wrong" chord right. That's the book's whole argument in two pages of prose someone else wrote.

The Goold Brown excerpt is a treasure. "An egregious plagiarism," "a miserable jumble," "fudge!" It earns its place. Likewise the Ann Fisher discovery: that the first English grammar by a woman invented the masculine-as-generic rule and was promptly plagiarized by a man. That's an anecdote a reader will repeat.

The structural-tree section, including the bull-in-tears figure, is competent and necessary.

### Major concerns

The middle of the chapter buckles under LLM padding. The Jeremy Taylor section is the worst offender. After we get the actual quote (which is splendid), we're told four times what it means. "Taylor's use of \textit{ungrammatical}... shows that the concept of grammaticality... has been part of the English linguistic consciousness for centuries." Then "Interestingly, Taylor's use of the term here is somewhat different from how we might use it today." Then "But in this first instance, Taylor used \textit{ungrammatical} to mean something more like..." Then "This suggests that the concept of grammaticality has evolved over time."

This is a paragraph saying the same thing four ways and pretending to develop. The closing line, "the concept of grammaticality is central to any serious study or use of language," is exactly the sort of sentence that needs a red pen through it.

There is also literal damage. Page 108 begins ", he became a chaplain..." with an orphaned comma. Earlier, "had had been clear only to those who placed deep faith my myths" has both a doubled "had" and "my" for "in." These are scars from prior LLM editing passes.

The "All fields of study face questions" list (Mathematics, Physics, Biology, Psychology, Music, etc.) is filler. Eight bullet points telling us that other fields also have hard questions. Cut to one or two.

The handball analogy goes on too long. It makes its point in two paragraphs and then keeps walking around the field for five more.

### Priority fixes

1. Cut the four-paragraph gloss after the Taylor quote down to one paragraph.
2. Repair the orphaned-comma sentence on Taylor and the "my myths" / "had had been clear" damage.
3. Trim the eight-discipline list of other-field puzzles to two.
4. Cut the handball analogy by half. The relevant point is gradience and context-sensitivity; the soccer detail can support, not occupy.
5. The Model of Grammaticality numbered list (15 items, sub-items, sub-sub-items) is doing too much work too early. Either move to an appendix or distill to a paragraph here and elaborate later.

### One concrete suggestion

Worst sentence: "As such, the concept of grammaticality is central to any serious study or use of language." Cut. The chapter has already shown why grammaticality matters; this sentence converts demonstration into pronouncement.

Best sentence: "Often the ungrammatical sentences linguists are 'starring' are those they've made up themselves." Wait, that was Chapter 0. Best sentence in Chapter 1: "Some linguists would argue that *\data{What did she doubt the idea that I had seen ___?} is ungrammatical in English in the same way that $2+2=3$ is incorrect in arithmetic. Others, though, would argue that the ungrammaticality is much more akin to the way the 'Tristan Chord' was experienced when audiences first heard Wagner's Tristan and Isolde." It works because it stages the book's central disagreement as two physically different things, math versus music, and trusts the reader to feel the gap.
