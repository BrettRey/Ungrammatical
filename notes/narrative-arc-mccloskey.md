## Proposed narrative arc: McCloskey's take

### The arc in one sentence

A reader who arrives sure that grammaticality is a single thing, knowable by feel, leaves convinced that it is a cluster of partly cohering forces, knowable only by example, and that this is no concession but the actual shape of the thing.

### The opening

Chapter 1's job is to make the reader feel a small but unmistakable jolt of recognition: "I had no idea this was strange." The voice that anchors the contract is yours, not Schleicher's. You, in a Toronto classroom or at the kitchen table, hearing *long, long road* in a Hollies lyric and noticing what English does that Old English didn't, what Japanese does differently again, what Jamaican Creole does in *blakblak*. That is the colleague's voice, leaning over the table with a coffee. The reader signs the contract because they like that person and they want to hear the next example. Schleicher's asterisk, Sweet's repurposing, and Householder's unease are inside this chapter as historical detour, not as the chapter itself. The first chapter performs the whole argument quietly, without the technical vocabulary. It promises the reader that they will not need a glossary to follow the book.

### The development

The middle does three things, in three movements, each earning the right to make the next stronger claim.

Movement I: *What grammaticality looks like up close.* The asterisk and the islands story (current chapter 1's best material), gradient judgements (current 2), pragmatic salvage and form-meaning clash (current 3 and 15 fused), and real patterns versus prescribed ones (current 10). At the end of this movement the reader knows that the asterisk is an evidentiary tool, not a verdict, and that linguists who work with it are arguing about the same kinds of things courts argue about with witnesses.

Movement II: *Where the feelings come from.* Intuitions as detectors (current 5, expanded to be the hinge), then the social and moral analogues (current 4 and 6 fused into "Grammar and the moral world"), then fashion and codeswitching as the same shape outside language (current 14 and 16 fused). This movement is where the cross-domain payoff finally cashes in. The reader sees that moral and grammatical intuitions are not metaphorical cousins but homologues: cluster-bound, convention-bound, partly cognitive, partly social, fully real.

Movement III: *Where grammaticality comes from and where it stops.* History (current 12), variety boundaries and the no-grammar challenge (current 9 and 13 fused), efficiency and processing (current 19), and the question of what would not be a possible language (current 17). The reader now sees that the same cluster works prospectively, retrospectively, and comparatively.

### The turn

The pivot is the moral chapter, recast. Until then the reader has been told, in many domains, that grammaticality is convention plus cognition plus history. They have nodded politely. The turn happens when they realize that what they already believe about moral judgment, and have always believed, has the same shape: clustered, defeasible, real, contested, learned, and not arbitrary. That is the moment they cannot un-see the cluster. Everything that follows about codeswitching, fashion, processing, and varieties is read through that recognition. The HPC view is not announced at the turn; it is felt.

### The resolution

The book ends twice on purpose. First, an argument-resolving close: a short chapter that names what the reader has been seeing, calls it the homeostatic property cluster, and gives them the technical vocabulary as a gift, not a hurdle. (This is the eleven-point model, demoted to its proper place: the moral of a parable, not its first move.) Then the parable closes the book: a brief coda built from the *long, long road* material that opened it, returned to with the reader now able to hear it differently. The takeaway in one sentence: *what feels inevitable about grammar is mostly the residue of contingency, convention, and a few real cognitive limits, and that is not a deflation but a description.*

### Why this arc serves the HPC argument

HPC is a claim about how a real category can be both clustered and bounded without being defined. Such a claim cannot be made first. It has to be earned by examples that the reader has already accepted as real. The current draft front-loads the model and then spends nineteen chapters illustrating it; readers experience this as being told the answer and then quizzed on it. The proposed arc inverts that. It builds the cluster in the reader's mind chapter by chapter, in domains where they already half-believe it (morality, fashion, codeswitching), then names it. The HPC view is the spine because the book builds toward the moment when the reader supplies the spine themselves.

### Chapters in proposed order

1. *A long, long road.* Reduplication across English, Old English, Japanese, Russian, Jamaican Creole, North-East Ambae. The whole argument in plain prose; asterisk-history as detour.
2. *The asterisk and the island.* Schleicher to Ross, the asterisk as evidentiary tool, the islands story. (Current 1's best material, model removed.)
3. *Degrees and judgements.* Gradient grammaticality, sentence acceptability, what intuitions report. (Current 2.)
4. *Salvage and clash.* Pragmatic rescue, form-meaning mismatch, "not enough." (Current 3 and 15 fused.)
5. *Real patterns.* What corpora show; the gap between prescribed and produced. (Current 10.)
6. *Feelings of grammaticality.* The hinge: intuitions as metacognitive detectors. (Current 5, expanded.)
7. *Grammar and the moral world.* The turn: the homologous shape of grammatical and moral judgement, including correctness politics. (Current 4 and 6 fused.)
8. *Fashion, code, and other clusters.* Cross-domain consolidation; codeswitching shown as natural, not deficient. (Current 14 and 16 fused.)
9. *Becoming ungrammatical.* Diachrony; *very/much*, Caxton, the trajectory of conventions. (Current 12.)
10. *Where one grammar stops.* Variety boundaries, the Riau Indonesian challenge, what counts as having grammar. (Current 9 and 13 fused.)
11. *Efficiency and the body.* Dependency locality, heavy-NP shift, processing pressure. (Current 19.)
12. *Impossible and possible.* What languages do not occur and why; the limits of the cluster. (Current 17.)
13. *The cluster, named.* The HPC account stated plainly, with the eleven points as the moral, not the manifesto. (Demoted from current 1.6.)
14. *Coda: the long road back.* A brief return to chapter 1's example, heard differently. (Adapted from current 21.)

Cut as freestanding chapters: 7 (generative grammar history, fold into 2), 8 (coordination, fold into 7 the moral chapter), 11 (neurolinguistics stub, commission or cut), 18 (swearing stub, commission or cut), 20 (getting it wrong stub, fold into 4).

The book is fourteen chapters, not twenty-one, and the reader walks out under their own power.
