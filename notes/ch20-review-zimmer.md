## Chapter 20: Getting grammaticality wrong

### Status

This chapter is a stub, flagged in the task brief. The current text is roughly 25 lines and consists of one example walking the reader from Old English (where there were no relative pronouns at all - subordinate clauses including relatives used *þe*) through 1325 ("the first real relative *who* in the way we know it today") into the Old English equivalent of relative *whose* (the *þe his* construction in the Psalms-like sentence "Eaðig bið se wer, þe his tohopa bið to Drihtne" / `Blessed be the man whose hope is in the Lord').

### What belongs

The chapter title promises a survey of cases where linguists, grammarians, or speakers have judged a structure ungrammatical and turned out to be wrong. Given the rest of the book, the obvious anchors are:

1. **The "missing" independent relative *whose*.** This was the chapter-5 set piece and the chapter-15 follow-up. Hankamer and Postal 1973 said it didn't exist; the OED has examples back to 1325; LLMs and naive speakers alike rate it grammatical. This is the canonical case of getting grammaticality wrong, and the chapter title invites it.

2. **Geoffrey Sampson's 1974 "OVS languages don't exist."** Sampson's claim was made before Hixkaryana (mentioned in chapter 10 as an OVS Cariban language with 500 speakers on the Nhamundá River) was widely documented in the linguistic literature. Desmond Derbyshire's 1979 *Hixkaryana* dissertation pulled the rug out from under that claim. The history of universals-claims being demolished by fieldwork is the chapter's territory.

3. **Pirahã and recursion.** Dan Everett's claim (chapter 4 and chapter 17) that Pirahã lacks recursion has been hotly contested. The fact that the controversy is still live shows how hard it is to settle a grammaticality claim about a small language.

4. **Center-embedded sentences are "ungrammatical."** *The horse the man the dog bit raced fell* was at one point informally treated as ungrammatical. Christopher and Miller's work on processing constraints showed it was just unparseable, not ungrammatical. That distinction matters.

5. **The various 18th-century prescriptive rulings that turned out to be Latin imports.** The split-infinitive ban, the no-preposition-at-the-end rule, the *never use the passive* rule - all started as Latin-grammar transplants and were never grammatically wrong in English.

6. **Linguistic prescriptivism on AAVE, Singlish, and other non-standard varieties.** Treated as ungrammatical by Standard speakers, fully grammatical within their own speech communities (chapter 13).

### Priority fixes

The chapter cannot be reviewed; it can only be sketched. The author has two strong cases already developed in the book (*whose* and OVS Hixkaryana). The chapter's natural function is to bring the *getting it wrong* arc to a head and remind readers of the cumulative case the book has been making: that confident linguists' grammaticality judgments have repeatedly turned out to be wrong.

### One concrete suggestion

The relative-*whose* opening currently in the chapter is the right opening. Continue it. Old English had no *whose*. Middle English developed it. Hankamer and Postal 1973 declared a particular form of it nonexistent. Naive speakers and the OED disagreed. The author found web examples and consulted family by text. The Wikipedia entry got rewritten. That is a complete arc and should be the chapter's spine. Then add Sampson on OVS-being-impossible and Hixkaryana, briefly. Then add prescriptive Latinate rulings, briefly. Then close with the methodological lesson: linguists' confident judgments, however eminent, are sometimes wrong, and the test is empirical engagement with actual usage. That's the one chapter the book really needs to land its central argument.
