## Chapter 11: Seeing grammar in the brain

This chapter is currently a stub of seven lines plus two citations. There's no chapter to review, but there is a chapter to design. I'll sketch what should be here, given the book's shape and what surrounding chapters already do.

### Strengths (of what's here)
The two citations chosen are well-targeted. Just (1993) on pupillometry is a foundational psycholinguistic measurement paper, and Divjak (2024) on heart-rate variability and article errors is recent, on-topic, and makes a sociolinguistic point that fits the book's argument: the body responds differently to grammatical error depending on whether the speaker is L1 or L2. That's the kind of finding that does double duty, supporting the cognitive claim and the social claim simultaneously. The seed is good.

### Major concerns
The chapter risks two failures of orientation. First, neurolinguistics is the field where generative grammar has its most empirically engaged research community. The N400, P600, ELAN, anterior negativities literature is real and large, and a generative reader picking up this chapter will expect it to engage that work. If the chapter doesn't, the reader will close the book. Second, neuroimaging and electrophysiology evidence is consistently overinterpreted in popular books, and Brett's house style flags this kind of overreach. A trade book on grammaticality that says "the brain has a grammar module" will alienate the cognitive scientists; one that says "the brain has nothing like a grammar module" will alienate the generativists. The chapter's path is between these.

The bigger concern is what the chapter is for in the book's overall architecture. Chapters 5 (How grammar feels) and 7 (Generative gap) already do significant cognitive-science work. If chapter 11 is going to repeat the predictive-processing story or the constructed-emotion story, it shouldn't exist. If it's going to do something different, that something needs to be specified.

### Priority fixes (i.e., chapter design)
- Decide what this chapter does that chapter 5 doesn't. My suggestion: chapter 5 covers metacognitive feeling; chapter 11 covers the somatic and unconscious markers (pupillometry, HRV, ERPs, fMRI). The chapter's title "Seeing grammar in the brain" promises the second; deliver on it.
- Engage the ERP literature seriously. The N400/P600 distinction is the core experimental finding the field has produced about grammaticality, and it's at least partially sympathetic to your gradient/constructed view (P600 effects scale with severity; semantic and syntactic violations recruit different circuits but in graded ways).
- Engage the aphasia literature. Broca's-aphasia patients can produce content words but lose syntactic structure. That looks, on its face, like evidence for a separable grammar module. A book skeptical of binary grammaticality has to engage this evidence.
- Treat the Divjak finding carefully. The L1/L2 asymmetry in HRV response is sociolinguistically loaded. Make sure the reader sees what's loaded about it.

### One concrete suggestion
Build the chapter around a single experimental paradigm, taken seriously. Sprouse and colleagues' island-acceptability experiments are the obvious candidate. Their work is widely respected by both generative and non-generative researchers, and it has produced findings that complicate a simple "grammar is binary, judgement is gradient" picture. Walk a non-specialist reader through one experiment, in detail, with attention to what the data actually show. Then bring in the ERP work briefly. Then close with the sociolinguistic Divjak finding. That gives the chapter a real shape: from formal experiment, through neural correlates, to social differentiation.

A generative reader who picks up this chapter and finds Sprouse engaged seriously will read the rest of the book in good faith. A generative reader who finds the chapter avoiding the experimental literature will not. Chapter 11 is therefore higher-leverage than its current length suggests. Ten pages of careful neurolinguistics will earn the rest of the book the readership it wants.

### What would belong here, summarized
- Pupillometry and HRV as somatic markers of effort/error.
- The N400 / P600 distinction in ERP studies.
- Sprouse-style acceptability experiments on islands and other purported categorical violations.
- The aphasia evidence, especially what it does and doesn't show about modularity.
- One careful section on individual variation: not all brains respond the same way to the same "error."
- A closing pivot back to the book's thesis: that grammaticality looks like an HPC cluster of overlapping mechanisms, not a single module.
