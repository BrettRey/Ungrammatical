## Chapter 10: Real patterns

### Strengths
The Amelie/Basil/Clara example is the chapter's strongest piece of writing. It's economical, it does what good linguistic exposition should do (locates the same situation in three differently-ordered languages and lets the pattern do the work), and it ends in a precise claim that grammar is data-compression for a particular social group. That last claim is the chapter's real contribution and it is genuinely interesting. A generative reader who values formal economy will find this congenial; a sociolinguist who values community will too. The double-pleasing here is rare and worth keeping.

The "Bert Bell and the 1947 NFL injury report" investigation is also gold. It is exactly the kind of empirical detective work that distinguishes a good trade book from an LLM-generated survey. You traced *probable to play* to a specific year, a specific institutional decision, and a specific 1986 *LA Times* citation. That is real source-grounded scholarship in the wild. It also makes a serious philosophical point: Hurford's claim that *probable* can't host a *to*-infinitive turns out to be empirically false in a specific register, and the falsity has a sociologically traceable origin. That's the kind of move that earns a generativist's respect, because it plays by the field's evidential rules and wins.

The November-the-fifth EEBO investigation is similarly strong. Walking the reader through the corpus evidence (zero examples 1470s-1620s, then three, eleven, thirty-two, sixty-seven) is precisely the kind of work that earns trust. A generative reader who is suspicious of the chapter's larger argument will, at minimum, recognize that the author is doing real work.

### Major concerns
The chapter ends with an uncredited LLM passage at lines 101-113 ("A functionalist approach to grammar..."). It uses the bullet structure and the in-summary closer that mark generated text. It's also a different voice from the chapter's actual prose. This has to come out before any reader sees it.

The Hurford engagement is the chapter's most important argumentative move and it's currently incomplete. You note that Hurford's *likely*/*probable* contrast is empirically wrong (because *probable to play* exists), and you make the historical argument about Old Norse vs. Old French. But you don't quite name what you're doing: you're showing that Hurford's challenge to semantic accounts succeeds, but only against a particular kind of semantic account. The constructional, usage-based account survives just fine. A generative reader needs to see this argument made, not just gestured at. Right now line 65, "the forces that cause grammaticalization are not the same as those that give us the feeling of grammaticality," is the chapter's punch line and it's a single sentence. It deserves more.

The chapter title "Real patterns" is good but does some unannounced work. "Real patterns" is a Dennett term, and Dennett's argument is that real patterns are those that reward a predictive stance. You allude to Dennett at chapter 5 (line 56) but never quite state the position here, where it would actually do work. If the chapter is doing Dennett, say so.

The Chris Anderson "long tail" detour at lines 92-95 is an aside that doesn't go anywhere. Either commit (Hurford's complaint is a long-tail problem; LLMs are showing we can absorb the long tail) or cut.

The Hixkaryana example at line 5 should be flagged as OVS (object-verb-subject), one of the rarest word orders, and credited to Derbyshire's fieldwork. As written, it's used as if the reader will recognize it.

### Priority fixes
- Cut the LLM-generated functionalist summary at the end.
- Develop the Hurford engagement. The chapter currently makes the right move but doesn't argue it. Spend two paragraphs distinguishing what's true and what's false in Hurford's challenge.
- Decide whether the chapter is doing Dennett's "real patterns." If so, name it. If not, retitle.
- The NFL/probable-to-play story is so good that it deserves to be the chapter's spine, not a digression in the middle. Open with it.
- Trim the long-tail aside or develop it.
- Source-ground the Hixkaryana. Derbyshire's fieldwork is the canonical source.

### One concrete suggestion
Reverse the chapter's structure. Open with the *probable to play* discovery: a generative reader hears that Hurford 2012 challenged semantic accounts of *likely*/*probable*; you then say "I checked," and lay out the 1986 LA Times citation, the NFL injury-report origin, and the Old Norse / Old French history. Then you can ask the chapter's question: what kind of "pattern" is grammar, that this kind of micro-history can move it? That's where Dennett's real patterns belong, and that's where the chapter's argument should land. The Amelie/Basil/Clara opening is good but it does a different job (it shows what grammar compresses); the *probable to play* investigation is the chapter's actual argument. Lead with the argument; use Amelie/Basil/Clara to set up the data-compression frame in the second half. A generative reader will find the chapter much harder to dismiss when its central claim isn't tucked in the middle.
