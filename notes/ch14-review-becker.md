## Chapter 14: Grammar and glamour

### Strengths
This chapter does something the rest of the book has been promising and rarely delivering: it makes the parallel between grammatical and fashion judgements without insisting on the parallel. The tracksuit-then-pair-glasses move is the kind of analogy a trade book lives or dies on. A grammatical innovation can fail or succeed for the same kinds of reasons a fashion choice can: who wears it, in what context, with what intent, with what cultural reference points. Cory Doctorow saying "even it's never approved" is not the same kind of thing as Brett's ESL students saying it, even though the surface form is identical. The chapter makes that distinction crisply and without scolding either Doctorow or the students.

The Doctorow personal-correspondence anecdote is genuinely useful. It does something the book has needed: it gives a single named, attested user of an innovation who can be asked about intent. "Yup. It's colloquial, but expressive, unambiguous, and colorful." That's the kind of evidence that a generative reader can engage. It also makes the chapter's argument concrete: grammaticality is, in part, about whether the user is recognised as competent.

The footnote on *dozen* / *of* is a nice little linguistic mini-essay that reveals some real CGEL knowledge.

The *enshittification* analysis is the chapter's most fun writing. The morphological dissection (shit + ify + ic + ate + tion + en) is a teaching moment that brings a non-specialist reader through derivational morphology painlessly, and the framing of Doctorow as someone who "knows what he's doing" is the right tonal choice. A generative reader will read this and find it both informed and charming.

### Major concerns
The chapter is short and reads as three subsections rather than a sustained argument. Three good subsections, but not a chapter. The opening doesn't introduce the chapter's question; the closing doesn't draw conclusions. The reader who finishes the chapter has enjoyed the ride but isn't sure what to take away.

The chapter's argument that "a grammatical innovation will only work if it achieves some kind of semantic or pragmatic goal" (line 14) is the central claim, and it's stated once. It's also stated rapidly. A serious generative reader will want to know how this differs from saying "ungrammatical strings sometimes catch on, and when they do they become grammatical." That's the position you're arguing against (or are you?), and it's left unspecified. The fashion analogy doesn't quite do the work; what does the work is the requirement that the speaker be competent and the cultural-referent point. Develop that.

There's a tribal-membership concern with the framing of ESL students. You write "my English-language students say and write things like *even it's never approved* (meaning 'even if it's never approved') all the time, but it's ungrammatical for me — likely for you too — and I correct them." The reader is offered a position alongside the teacher correcting the learner, then offered the position of the *Boing Boing* reader respecting the proficient speaker's innovation. That asymmetry is precisely what the chapter is about, but you don't quite reflect on the asymmetry from inside it. A reader who is themselves an L2 English speaker, or who works with L2 speakers, might feel that the chapter takes the teacher's position as default and the learner's position as the one being explained. Some self-reflection on this would do work.

The Vanessa Friedman quote on tracksuits is good, but the analogy gets repeated three times in the section and the third repetition feels like padding. The paragraph at line 30 ("The tracksuit's journey...") reuses the same image after the *I have a pair glasses* example has already done the work.

### Priority fixes
- Open with a clear chapter question. Something like: "What's the difference between a grammatical innovation and an ungrammatical mistake?"
- Develop the line "to do that, there needs to be some established connection between the innovation and the effect, some linguistic or cultural point of reference." This is the chapter's actual claim. It deserves a paragraph, not a sentence.
- Reflect on the teacher/L2-speaker asymmetry from inside it, even briefly. The chapter has the makings of an interesting argument about how class, status, and authority enter into grammaticality judgement; right now it's at the surface.
- Trim the third tracksuit paragraph.
- Cut the "Doctorow says, 'Three suffixes is grand'" Seussian aside if it's invented; if it's a real Doctorow comment, source it. Right now it reads as if the author is putting words in Doctorow's mouth.

### One concrete suggestion
This chapter is doing something the rest of the book hasn't done well: it's making the HPC argument by analogy without saying "HPC." The book's central claim — that "ungrammatical" picks out a cluster of overlapping properties that don't reduce to a single criterion — gets quietly demonstrated here by the parallel to fashion (also a cluster, also not reducible). Lean into this. Add a closing paragraph that draws the parallel out: "fashion and grammar both work this way, and 'wrong' in both domains depends on who, when, with what reference, with what intent. Throughout this book I've been arguing the same thing about grammaticality. Here it is, in a single chapter." That kind of explicit thesis-naming is what the book has been avoiding for honourable reasons (don't lecture the reader), but a reader who's been with you for fourteen chapters will reward a moment of explicit theorizing. A generative reader, in particular, will appreciate the chapter that finally puts the cards on the table. Right now the chapter is too coy about its own argument.
