## Chapter 14: Grammar and glamour

### Strengths

This is the book's most successful chapter at the trade-book register Reynolds is aiming for. It's short, focused, and built around three small case studies (the Doctorow "even it's never approved," the tracksuit fashion analogy, the Doctorow neologism "enshittification") that earn their keep individually and collectively.

The Doctorow correspondence detail ("It's colloquial, but expressive, unambiguous, and colorful") works. Reynolds didn't have to email the author; he did, and the reader gets the small frisson of seeing how a grammatical innovation is intentionally deployed by a proficient speaker. The footnote ("Personal communication, July 6, 2012") is just right.

The argument that lands is: "It's simply not the case that a proficient speaker of a language can get away with anything. A grammatical innovation will only work if it achieves some kind of semantic or pragmatic goal." That's the chapter's real claim, and it's tight.

The tracksuit analogy is the right kind of analogy for a trade-style book on grammar: a fashion item that traveled from unfashionable to chic depending on context and accessories, the way a phrase travels from ungrammatical to stylistic. Vanessa Friedman's quote ("Wear a sweatsuit with a sweatband and sneakers and you look like a victim of the endless cycle of New Year's workout resolutions") is doing the work of three theoretical paragraphs.

The "enshittification" section is the chapter's bravura piece. Reynolds traces the morphology one suffix at a time (-ify, -ic, -ate, -tion, en-) and shows that the word's success is not arbitrary but a specific, calibrated playing of English's affixational possibilities against the cultural moment. The "Three suffixes is grand," says Doctorow, "but I can do more" with apologies-to-Dr.-Seuss is a moment of real wit.

The footnote on \textit{a dozen} versus other collectives ("a dozen of them" but not "a dozen of words") is the kind of CGEL-savvy aside that demonstrates the book's grammatical chops.

### Major concerns

The opening section heading reads "\subsection{Even it's never approved}" but no parent \section{} appears. The sub-section is an orphan structurally. Same for "Tracksuits" and "The glory of \textit{enshittification}." This is fixable LaTeX hygiene, but it suggests the chapter was assembled from pieces and never given a top-level frame.

The chapter doesn't connect the three case studies as systematically as it could. The reader is asked to draw the parallel (Doctorow's "even" innovation = stylistic choice = enshittification = grammar-and-glamour) but the chapter could load this more explicitly without overdoing it.

There's a footnote ("Most collectives, like \textit{group}, \textit{bunch}, \textit{lot}, \textit{pile}, \textit{heap}, and \textit{shitload}") that buries a lovely observation. \textit{Shitload} is a connecting bridge to the enshittification section that the chapter could exploit.

The Friedman / Zimmer quotes near the start of the enshittification section are stacked. Pick one or use both for distinct purposes.

### Priority fixes

1. Add a chapter-level opening paragraph that frames all three case studies. Right now the chapter starts with "\subsection{Even it's never approved}" with no setup.
2. Promote the three subsections to sections (or add a parent section with framing).
3. Either drop the Zimmer quote or use it for a different point than the Friedman quote.
4. Surface the \textit{shitload}-to-\textit{enshittification} connection from footnote to text.
5. Consider adding a closing paragraph that makes the grammar-as-fashion claim explicit. The chapter has earned the right to say it directly.

### One concrete suggestion

Worst sentence: "A grammatical innovation that fails to do these things is ungrammatical." This is the chapter's argumentative payoff and it should be more memorable than this. Right now it's a flat sentence after a list. Try: "A grammatical innovation that does none of those things isn't an innovation. It's just an error."

Best sentence: "It's a perfectly cromulent word, the kind any linguist loves: new, creative, flexible, and evocative." It works because it embeds another famous Simpsons-coined word ("cromulent") as the very tool used to describe a Doctorow neologism, and the reader who catches the wink feels rewarded. The sentence enacts what the chapter is arguing.
