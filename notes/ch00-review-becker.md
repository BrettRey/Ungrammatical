## Chapter 00: Introduction

### Strengths
The asterisk is a brilliant organizing object for a sociologist's eye. You're showing the reader a guild mark, a small cult sign that working linguists use a hundred times a week without thinking about it, and you're letting them in on its history. That move respects the tribe (the asterisk has a real story, scholars made it, it isn't arbitrary) while also stepping outside the tribe enough to ask why this little symbol does the work it does. That's exactly the right tonal balance for a book that wants to be heard by people inside and outside the field. The CGEL six-example battery is also rhetorically smart: you are not picking on a strawman or on bad linguists, you are pointing at the most prestigious descriptive grammar in English and saying "look at the strangeness right at the heart of our best work." A reader inside the tribe will not feel mocked by that.

### Major concerns
The chapter doesn't yet tell the reader what's at stake for *them*. Schleicher and Sweet are well drawn (when the prose isn't padded), but the introduction reads like an origin myth for a symbol rather than a promise about why the next 21 chapters are going to repay attention. A book of this kind needs to deliver, in the introduction, an answer to the question every reader silently asks: "what am I going to be able to see, by the end, that I can't see now?" The closing paragraph gestures at the puzzle ("how we know which sentences sound right") but keeps it small and personal. The book's actual ambition (that "ungrammatical" names a homeostatic property cluster across moral, political, fashion, codeswitching, and neurological domains) is invisible here. A generativist picking this up has no preview of the argument they will need to be ready to disagree with. They might disengage before chapter 7.

There is also a tribal-membership move I want to flag. The Householder citation arrives in a single sentence and leaves. To a generativist reader, that is a discreet wave to the descriptivist side of the room. Either build it out or cut it: a hostage citation reads as flag-planting, not argument.

### Priority fixes
- Add one paragraph near the end forecasting the book's argument: that "ungrammatical" doesn't pick out one thing, that the same word does very different work across domains, and that the resulting picture is unfamiliar but coherent.
- Tell the reader, early, what kind of reader you're writing for. Right now the imagined audience could be a non-linguist or a colleague; the prose hedges. Pick.
- Trim Schleicher and Sweet by about a third, especially the inspirational adjectives. They tell a generativist that you are not in serious mode yet, and that's a bad first impression.
- Close with a hook, not a summary. The "what grammar is, how we know it, why it matters" formula is too generic for the work the rest of the book does.

### One concrete suggestion
Add a single short section at the end called something like "What this book is for." Two paragraphs. First paragraph: name the puzzle directly; the same word ("ungrammatical") gets used by syntacticians, by teachers correcting students, by people calling someone's clothes wrong, by neuropsychologists describing aphasia, by language reformers, by codeswitching researchers, by those who police political speech. They can't all mean the same thing, and yet they all use the same word. Second paragraph: tell the reader what you'll do about it. Don't argue HPC yet. Just promise that by the end, they'll see a shape they didn't see before. That promise is what makes a generativist keep reading past the moment when you stop agreeing with their tradition. It also gives a non-linguist something to hold onto when the technical examples get weird in chapter 1.
