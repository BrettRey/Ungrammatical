## Chapter 16: Unravelling babel

### Strengths

The Tomo-in-the-kitchen scene is the chapter's anchor and one of the book's most quietly effective moments. Toronto, 2003, SARS, the family at home with the author's mother. Yoko speaks Japanese to Tomo. The author speaks Japanese except when reading. Mom speaks only English. One day, during a pause in a Japanese conversation, Tomo turns to his grandmother and starts explaining what Yoko and the author had been saying. The author realizes the toddler has not just sorted Japanese from English but has sorted *who understands what*. That is a Zimmer-class scene: a small specific moment from which a whole framework follows.

The Inya/Heike Wiese anecdote (a German three-year-old refusing to speak English to British in-laws because for her English is "the Daddy register" and the in-laws are not Daddy) is exactly the right kind of supporting case. Two stories of the same insight made by two different children in two different households.

The vervet/green-monkey "leopard" call as a fixed sign-meaning pair (Price 2014) is a good biological anchor for what's distinctive about human language: humans don't have fixed sign-meaning pairs, we have communicative situations.

The Smith 2019 framework of "showing and guessing" is invoked twice (which is once too many) but it's a useful framing.

### Major concerns

The chapter opens as a bullet-list of notes-to-self ("• Now the whole earth had one language and the same words. So begins...", through ten more bullets ending "• Swearing feels right in some context and wrong in others. Perhaps this is because..."). The first 15 lines are an outline. They need to be rewritten as prose.

The Wiese block quote about Inya appears twice (once in the bullet list, once again after the Tomo scene). This is a duplicate-paste signature.

The Smith 2019 paragraph about showing-and-guessing also appears twice, near-identical wording.

The chapter has a real thesis - "a language is simply a communicative situation" or "com-sit" in Wiese's term - but it's stated bluntly midway through and never developed. The Grieve 2023 quote that ends the chapter introduces a major hypothesis (situational complexity trades off against grammatical complexity) and then stops.

The chapter title ("Unravelling babel") points at a Genesis frame that's introduced in the bullet list and abandoned. If the chapter is about Babel, where is Babel?

The chapter is supposed to be about codeswitching but the actual codeswitching content is one sentence ("if two people share the context of 'English' and 'Japanese', they can mix constructions from both in the same sentence and it will be grammatical"). No actual examples of codeswitching are given. No Spanish-English Tex-Mex, no Cantonese-English Hong Kong, no Hindi-English. This is the major content gap for a chapter titled codeswitching.

### Priority fixes

1. Convert the opening bullet list into prose.
2. Cut the duplicate Wiese block quote and the duplicate Smith summary.
3. The Tomo kitchen scene should be the chapter opening, not buried halfway through.
4. Add at least one sustained example of bilingual codeswitching, with linguistic analysis. The Poplack 1980 work on Spanish-English codeswitching in NYC Puerto Rican Spanish ("Sometimes I'll start a sentence in English y termino en español") is the canonical reference, and her constraints on where switches can happen are real and grammatical.
5. The Grieve 2023 hypothesis (situational complexity trades off against grammatical complexity) is interesting and could be the chapter's thesis - either develop or cut.

### One concrete suggestion

Codeswitching has been beautifully studied in specific bilingual communities. The Khmer-English speakers of Long Beach. The Cantonese-Mandarin-English of San Francisco's Sunset District. Most relevant to the chapter, given Tomo's Japanese-English childhood: there is research on Japanese-English bilingual children in North America, and the patterns of when particles like *ne* or *yo* attach to English clauses are real and grammatical. Find a specific community and a specific researcher. Right now the chapter says "languages can mix" without showing what mixed grammar looks like in any concrete pairing. Take one twenty-second utterance from a real bilingual conversation, gloss it, and explain why a grammatical codeswitch differs from an ungrammatical one. That's the chapter's missing center.
