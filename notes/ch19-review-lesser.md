## Chapter 19: Communicative efficiency

### Strengths

The driving-on-the-right / driving-on-the-left analogy for head-direction consistency is the chapter's strongest pedagogical move. Reynolds takes the dependency-locality principle (a relatively dry computational-linguistics finding) and grounds it in highway-design logic. Right-side driving means right-side exits; head-first languages mean post-head dependents. The structural rationale is the same: minimize complex crossings.

The Highway 427 contra-lateral exit photo and explanation extend the analogy nicely. Sometimes engineering forces a left-side exit; sometimes language forces a contralateral structure (heavy NP shift, the passive). The "good reason for it" framing is exactly right.

The dependency-distance counts on the trash-out examples (1+2+6=9, 1+12+16=20, 0+4+6=10) are the right way to make the abstract concrete. A reader who follows along will feel the math in their gut.

The (a) through (g) sequence on "I threw the trash out" is excellent. Each version adds one more word to the "trash" NP and the reader feels the pressure to switch order build until "I threw out the smelly kitchen trash from under the sink that had been sitting there for two weeks" arrives as a relief. That's pedagogy through reading-as-experience, which is what trade books should aspire to.

The convoluted-neural-network-architecture passive/active comparison (dependency distance 0 versus 21) is well-chosen. The aside ("No matter how many times you've been told to avoid the passive voice, I think you'll agree that the passive is better here") is the chapter at its plainspoken best.

The Gildea (2007) finding on alternating dependents minimizing dependency length is a real theoretical anchor.

### Major concerns

The chapter is short and largely free of LLM padding, which is good. But it suffers from being titled "Communicative efficiency" and then operating almost entirely in the dependency-locality dimension. The efficiency hypothesis has many strands: word-choice efficiency, frequency-length tradeoffs (Zipf), morphological compaction, redundancy/robustness tradeoffs. The chapter touches none of these. Either the chapter should narrow its title ("Dependency locality") or broaden its substance.

The footnote on sentences with literal head-final determiners ("I grant you wishes three" / "o mistress mine") is charming but unincorporated.

The opening paragraph reads slightly AI-explainerish ("Languages are not arbitrary in their structure but are shaped by a balance between..."). The book's voice is stronger when it comes through.

The Japanese examples in (\ref{ex:take-a-rest-Distance-J}) are presented without translations or glosses (just Japanese characters). For a trade book that may be read by non-Japanese-speaking audiences, gloss them.

The chapter doesn't connect to the larger book's argument about HPC grammaticality. Why does dependency locality matter for the book's overall thesis? The chapter would be stronger if it landed the connection: dependency-locality preferences are one of several constraints in the cluster that defines grammaticality.

### Priority fixes

1. Decide whether the chapter is about communicative efficiency broadly or dependency locality specifically. If broadly, add Zipf and morphological-compaction material. If specifically, retitle.
2. Gloss the Japanese examples.
3. Tighten the opening paragraph. "Languages are shaped by a trade-off between information transfer and ease of processing" is one sentence; the current opening is two.
4. Add a closing paragraph that ties dependency locality to the book's HPC argument: this is one of the cluster of properties (together with conventionality, frequency, community-recognition) that constitutes grammaticality.

### One concrete suggestion

Worst sentence: "The \textsc{efficiency hypothesis} posits that languages evolve to achieve this balance." This is the AI-explainer voice ("posits," "evolve to achieve this balance"). The chapter has just told us in plain language that languages are shaped by a workspace-organization principle; it doesn't need this academic restatement. Cut.

Best sentence: "Just as in the highway 427 exit example, there's a good reason to adjust the structure to accommodate unusual situation." Even with the small typo (missing "an" before "unusual"), the sentence is doing the chapter's work: making heavy NP shift feel like the same kind of thing as a contralateral highway exit, both of them principled deviations from the default for a good reason. Reynolds' best move all chapter is the parallel between civil engineering and grammar, and this sentence holds that parallel together.
