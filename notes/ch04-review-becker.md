## Chapter 04: Morality and grammaticality

### Strengths
The premise is genuinely interesting and the right kind of move for a trade book about HPC: a single English word ("ungrammatical") gets used in moral, social, and linguistic registers, and the chapter wants to take the moral register seriously rather than dismiss it. That's the path away from simple descriptivism-vs-prescriptivism polemic. The double-modal data via Steven Coats is good. It shows the reader that you can do real corpus work in service of an argument that *isn't* about scolding non-standard speech, but about a rare and intricate construction that most people will never have heard. Coats's Ontario-only-three figure is the kind of fact that makes a reader trust the chapter.

The Jahangir Khan opening is a charming attempt at the kind of analogy a popular book needs. And the genuine observation that competition can be cooperative-at-a-larger-scale (sport, science, law) is sociologically interesting.

### Major concerns
This chapter is currently the most LLM-padded in the book. The Parfit framework is stated once, then re-stated, then synthesized, then re-synthesized, with two parallel "Ethical Perspectives on Grammaticality" sections (4.7 and 4.7 again, lines 78 and 90), and then the same material is scaffolded yet again at line 361 ("Climbing the Linguistic Mountain"). The reader who reaches 460 will have read minor variations on the same Parfit pitch four times. The phrase "could be justified to others in a way no one could reasonably reject" appears verbatim in multiple places. This is what a generativist will see if they open the book to a random page in chapter 4: prose generated to fill the chapter, not prose written to make an argument. That impression then taints everything else in the book, including the parts that are excellent.

Beyond the redundancy, the Parfit application is itself a hostage to fortune. Parfit's "climbing the same mountain" framework, even at full strength, needs to do persuasive work to make the analogy fit. Right now the chapter announces the framework, declares the unification, lists the seven views, and finishes. There's no actual argument that, say, deontology and consequentialism *converge* on a grammaticality verdict. The reader is told the views form a unified theory, never shown.

There's also a tribal-membership move: "elitist power view" and "social justice by any means necessary" are presented as caricatures. Both labels are unkind to the people who hold those positions seriously. The first sounds like a strawman of prescriptivism; the second sounds like a strawman of critical sociolinguistics. Most readers in either camp will recognize themselves badly distorted.

The final and structurally serious concern: the ethics-of-language angle is a real chapter, and it deserves to be done with one framework, taken seriously, and applied to one or two careful examples. As written, the chapter is less an argument than a survey of frameworks the author hasn't yet decided among.

### Priority fixes
- Cut all three duplicate "Ethical Perspectives" sections down to one. This is line-level work but transformative.
- Drop the seven-frameworks survey. Pick one (probably contractualism, which actually fits the social-convention argument you're making) and use it to do the work.
- Either commit to Parfit's "same mountain" claim with a real demonstration or let Parfit go.
- Replace "elitist power" and "social justice by any means necessary" with neutral labels. The chapter's argument doesn't need partisan packaging.
- The Pirahã reference at line 5 (an "ideas" stub) and the "(true?)" parenthetical at line 7 mean source grounding hasn't happened yet. Don't write past these markers.

### One concrete suggestion
The chapter has buried inside it a much sharper book chapter: "When grammar judgment becomes moral judgment." That's the real subject. The reader is asked to notice that *correct* and *incorrect* in English carry both linguistic and ethical weight, that "ungrammatical" can mean "wrong" in two senses, and that this isn't an accident. Replace the ethical-frameworks survey with a single argument: in some cultures and registers, grammaticality is moralized; this is a real fact about how the word works, not a confusion to be cleared away. Then bring in the dangler-hating philosophy professor, the Orwell passive-voice tradition, the social registers around *me and him*, and Coats's double modals, and show the moralizing pattern across all of them. That's an argument; what's currently here is a glossary. A glossary won't bring an opponent across.
