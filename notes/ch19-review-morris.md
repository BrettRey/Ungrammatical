## Chapter 19: Communicative efficiency

### Strengths

The Highway 427 / Eglinton Avenue analogy is the chapter's centerpiece, and it works. A real Toronto interchange where the off-ramp goes left because the right-side design would have been more complex. The map (Figure \ref{fig:427map}) is a real visual document of a real exception to a real default. The chapter then transposes this analogy to grammar: heavy NP shift puts a long noun phrase to the right, against the usual ordering, because the alternative would create even longer dependency distances. The geographic analogy is specific, located, and Canadian. It earns its keep in a way the chapter's abstract claims couldn't.

The "trash" examples are a clean piece of pedagogy. *I threw it out / I threw the trash out / I threw the kitchen trash out / I threw the smelly kitchen trash out / I threw the smelly kitchen trash from under the sink out (?)*. As the noun phrase grows heavier, the *out*-final version becomes worse and worse, and the reader can feel the tipping point in their own mouth. That's the chapter's argument as a felt experience.

The convolutional-neural-network passive vs. active example is also smart. The chapter shows that the passive sentence has a 0-word dependency distance between subject and verb, while the active sentence has 21. "No matter how many times you've been told to avoid the passive voice, I think you'll agree that the passive is better here." That's the chapter pinning the prescriptivist about passives to a measurable fact.

### Major concerns

The chapter is one of the most coherent in the manuscript, but it's structurally short (131 lines) and underdeveloped at the edges. The opening jumps directly into "the efficiency hypothesis" with a Futrell 2020 footnote and no setup. A trade reader doesn't know who Futrell is, what the efficiency hypothesis is in plain language, or why anyone disagrees. (Hint: not everyone agrees. The efficiency hypothesis is one school of functionalist linguistics; nativist accounts give different explanations.)

Section structure is irregular: a `\subsection{}` heading at line 9 with no parent `\section{}`. There's a centered double-em-dash scaffolding marker at line 96.

The chapter's strongest argument is implicit: that conventional patterns get explained by underlying functional pressures (efficiency, dependency-locality), and that the unusual patterns survive when they pay for themselves. That's an HPC-style argument: a cluster of features that hangs together because of underlying causal mechanisms. The chapter should make that connection explicitly, since the book's overall thesis is HPC. Right now this chapter feels disconnected from the HPC framework it's supposed to support.

On the protagonists question: this chapter has no people. Richard Futrell is in a footnote. Daniel Gildea has a citation. The Highway 427 designers are anonymous. The chapter's argument is that grammar is functionally adapted to dependency-locality pressure, but no human is shown adapting. There's no parent simplifying syntax for a child, no copy editor reordering a sentence, no court reporter shortening a complex clause. The chapter is the book's most abstract.

The chapter also has the book's thinnest treatment of disagreement. There are linguists (Newmeyer, for example) who argue the efficiency hypothesis is overstated. That conversation isn't here.

### Priority fixes

1. Open with one specific human at one specific moment doing dependency-locality work without knowing it (a writer reordering a sentence, a child re-shuffling a phrase, a translator).
2. Explicitly connect the chapter to the book's HPC thesis. Efficiency is one of the homeostatic pressures that holds grammar's clusters together.
3. Acknowledge that not all linguists buy the efficiency hypothesis. Briefly cite a counter-position.
4. Build out section structure (no orphan subsection).
5. Cut the centered scaffolding marker at line 96.

### One concrete suggestion

Open with a real journalist editing a long sentence under deadline. Or a copy editor at the *New York Times* reordering "a stack of distinct layers that transform the input volume into an output volume through a differentiable function" toward the end of a sentence. Then ask: why does this version work better? Why is the editor's instinct correct? Then bring in dependency-locality. The chapter has the right idea (functional pressures shape conventions, conventions shape what feels grammatical), but it needs a human at the keyboard. The Highway 427 example is good for the analogy; it doesn't substitute for a person.

Connection to other chapters: the *probable to play* story in chapter 10 is a great companion piece. Both show that grammar is responsive to functional pressures (information economy in chapter 10's case, dependency locality here). The book should foreground that they're related arguments.
