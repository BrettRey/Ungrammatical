## Chapter 05: How grammar feels

### Strengths

The opening barrage of sensory questions - is ungrammaticality "sand in your shoe, a branch that scratches you as you walk by, an ice-cream headache, the sting of a mosquito, the glare of headlights?" - is the most Zimmer-ready prose in the book so far. The interoceptive turn (proprioception, intrafusal fibers, Ruffini endings, CO2 levels in the blood) is genuinely science-writing. The Proust madeleine quote is well-chosen and well-deployed: it gives a reader a name for the kind of feeling-about-a-feeling the chapter is theorizing.

The "Whose gorilla?" investigation is some of the best material in the manuscript. Hankamer and Postal's 1973 squib, the *whose* paradigm with its ostensible hole, the tour through the OED finding 14th-century instances Hankamer and Postal didn't know existed, the pings to twelve friends and family by text who unanimously rated it 6 or 7 - this is a working linguist following a lead. The wikipedia-as-source-of-truth subplot is true, contemporary, and revealing. The mismatched judgments between Hankamer-Postal-CGEL on one side and naive speakers + LLMs on the other is the chapter's most interesting puzzle.

Mia, the daughter, and Sasha-the-roommate-who-uses-they: a lived-house anecdote that anchors the singular *they* discussion. The "champagne foaming into the wrong bottle" image for the effort of overriding an automatic interpretation is good.

The squirrel monkey study (Ravignani 2013) and the rock hyrax aside are genuine surprises that earn their place.

The ISIS-construction section, with the EconTalk examples, is well-handled.

### Major concerns

The chapter is enormous (900 lines) and contains massive duplications that signal an unfinished merge of multiple drafts: the sensation paragraph appears twice (line 27 and 402), the Proust quotation appears twice (lines 31 and 406), section 5.5 "What does (un)grammaticality feel like 1?" and 5.6 "What does ungrammaticality feel like 2?" do largely the same work. The "Reinterpreting grammatical intuitions through a constructionist lens" subsection appears twice in succession, word for word. The Form-meaning mismatch section is duplicated with a "latexCopy" marker in the middle. The chapter ends with a pasted ChatGPT response on emotions-as-vectors.

The Bayes-theorem worked example with $P(A_1) = 1.0$ and $P(B|A_2) = 10^{-15}$ (note: $P(A_1) = 1.0$ is wrong - probabilities of distinct constructions can't both be at ceiling) reads like a textbook detour the rest of the chapter doesn't need. The entire predictive-processing section says, more clearly and more efficiently, what the equations are pretending to formalize.

The "Grammar and the Sacred" section, opening with the Tower of Babel, then sociology, then Indian English, then African American English, is hand-waving on a topic the next four chapters arguably handle more rigorously. Either commit (with citations to Durkheim, with at least one ethnographer) or cut.

Lisa Feldman Barrett's constructed-emotion theory gets two redundant subsections of plot summary. We never see the experiments. We never see Barrett. We get her conclusions paraphrased.

The string of unattributed quotations on lines 414-431 ("He knew only that his child was his warrant," "I can't go on. I'll go on," Pascal, Hamlet, Orwell on 2+2=5) is a notebook of epigraphs that didn't get placed.

### Priority fixes

1. Cut every duplicate. This is the single biggest editorial intervention the chapter needs - probably 200-300 lines.
2. Remove the pasted ChatGPT response on emotion vectors at the end.
3. Pick one frame: predictive processing, or constructed-emotion, or the Bayes math. They are all variants of the same idea. Right now the chapter argues each three times.
4. The "Whose gorilla" investigation deserves to be the spine, with everything else organized around it as commentary. Right now it sits as the chapter's centerpiece but is followed by long stretches of theory it has already concretized.
5. Cut the Bayesian worked example or rebuild it with realistic numbers and a less artificial pair of options.
6. Anchor or cut the "sacred" section; resolve the unattributed quotations.

### One concrete suggestion

The N400 / P600 ERP findings get a paragraph of casual paraphrase ("semantic speed bump," "syntactic pothole"). These are real waveforms, recorded from real scalps in real labs. Find one published trace - Kutas and Hillyard, 1980, on the N400 - and walk the reader through what a participant actually did. Sat in a chair, electrodes greased to the scalp, sentences flashed one word at a time on a screen. *I take coffee with cream and ___* and the screen says SOCKS. The voltage dips 400 ms later. That deflection on the strip chart is the closest neuroscience has come to seeing the feeling of ungrammaticality. The chapter spends pages theorizing it. One paragraph showing it would be worth more than all of them.
