## Chapter 15: What's ungrammatical

### Strengths

The Geoff Pullum email about *The Rubymar is the ship to have been sunk by the Houthis* is one of the most patient pieces of investigative grammar writing in the manuscript. Pullum sees the BBC sentence and emails Reynolds. They suspect the *to have been* needs licensing (like *more* needing *than*). They try *first*, *only*, *largest*. *Largest* is a superlative; the others aren't. The hypothesis fragments. They consult COCA. They try *seems to be*, find it works, then realize *is* (present tense) is the trouble; *was*, *were*, *been* are all fine. They land on a tense-clash hypothesis (present-tense matrix vs. infinitival perfect subordinate). The chapter takes the reader through the live process of two linguists working a problem together by email. That is exactly the kind of human-scale intellectual labor a trade book should foreground.

The Old French to Modern French passé-composé story is also strong. Phonological erosion makes the simple past tense ambiguous, which makes the present-perfect more attractive as a substitute, which over centuries lets the present-perfect drop its present-tense anchoring and become a plain past tense. The cross-paradigm conjugation tables earn their space because they show the reader the erosion happening in form. This is a real piece of historical linguistics, told inside a chapter on grammaticality, and it earns its keep.

The Spiro-Ex-Lax dialogue (McCawley & Morgan 1973) is wickedly funny and exactly the right kind of trade-book linguistic humor. *Spiro conjectures Ex-Lax* is ungrammatical until you've heard the question *Does anyone know what Mrs. Nixon frosts her cakes with?* This is the chapter at its best.

### Major concerns

The chapter is enormous (632 lines) and contains about three chapters worth of material. The *Rubymar* puzzle, the passé composé, the Ex-Lax case, and the *whose* return all warrant their own treatments. Right now they sit on top of each other.

The *whose* section starting at line 366 mostly recapitulates the chapter 5 *whose* discussion. The chapter signals this ("Earlier in our exploration of linguistic intuitions\dots") but then redoes most of the work. The reader has already met Hankamer and Postal, the gorilla example, the punch-drinking, the OED. Either chapter 5 should be shorter and chapter 15 should be the first full treatment, or chapter 15 should refer back without re-explaining.

The "world tour of *whose*" section (line 563) is the chapter's worst LLM-touch. German *dessen*, Spanish *cuyo*, Persian, Japanese, all surveyed in two paragraphs each, with the closing four-bullet summary "What can we learn from this linguistic world tour" that reads like a high-school essay's conclusion. It does not sound like Reynolds.

The closing five-bullet list ("Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence; Even native speakers' intuitions can be misleading\dots Language is always full of surprises") is exactly the kind of platitudinous summary the writing-style rule flags as AI-tic.

The "ivory-billed woodpecker" comparison gets used three times across the chapter (lines 421, 437, 615). Once is enough.

There's a "[ob come back to this cb]" placeholder at line 241. There are double-em-dash centered scaffolding markers at 43, 149, 243, 269. The chapter ends with a "We first noted this connection in Chapter 1, and here it provides further insight" sentence followed by a stray closing quotation mark.

On the protagonists question: the chapter's heroes are Reynolds and Pullum, working a real puzzle in real time over email. The villains are Hankamer and Postal, but Reynolds treats them with care: they were "ferreting out" a rare bird and missed it. The 1970s ("the 70s being something of a strange time") gets a glancing dig. McCawley and Morgan are also handled with affection. The closing pieces are kind to the linguists who got it wrong: they were doing what linguists do.

### Priority fixes

1. Resolve the overlap with chapter 5. Make one full treatment of *whose*, gesture at it from the other.
2. Cut the world-tour section and the five-bullet final list.
3. Fix all "[ob come back to this cb]" placeholders.
4. Cut the double-em-dash scaffolding markers.
5. Decide whether the chapter is one chapter or three.

### One concrete suggestion

Open with the email exchange with Pullum on the *Rubymar* sentence. Reynolds and Pullum, like grandfather and grandson detectives, slowly closing in on what's wrong. The chapter then has its model of grammar-investigation in human form: two specific people, one specific BBC sentence, real research, real revision of hypothesis, real arrival at a tentative explanation. From there, build outward to the historical case (passé composé), the contextual case (Ex-Lax), and the discourse-structural case (*whose*). The world-tour can become a one-sentence note: similar patterns appear in German *dessen* and Spanish *cuyo*. That's enough. The chapter as it stands wants to be a textbook chapter; it should be a detective story.
