## Chapter 20: Getting grammaticality wrong

### Strengths
The chapter starts with the right kind of evidence: glossed Old English and Middle English examples (\ref{ex:kyninga}, \ref{ex:doȝter-wo}, \ref{ex:þe-his}) showing the historical absence and gradual emergence of relative *who*/*whose*. This is exactly the kind of philological grounding the chapter needs.

The single dated landmark - "It was only around 1325, in the midst of Middle English, when we start to see a real relative *who* in the way we know it today" - gives the reader a concrete chronological pivot.

### Major concerns
The chapter is a stub. 26 lines, three glossed examples, no conclusion, no figure, and only one of three intended sections (4.1 Relative *whose*) is begun.

The chapter title "Getting grammaticality wrong" implies a chapter about *errors of analysis*: cases where linguists have decided something is ungrammatical when it isn't, or grammatical when it isn't. The opening section is well-aligned with this thesis (the centuries of pre-existing relative *whose* missed by Hankamer-Postal), but the chapter never broadens beyond one example.

The book's *whose* arguments now span at least three chapters: 5 (intuitions, ~80 lines on *whose*), 15 (what's ungrammatical, ~190 lines on *whose*), and 20 (this one, ~26 lines on *whose* in Old/Middle English). At this rate the whole book is becoming a *whose* monograph wearing a costume.

There is no figure, no table, no comparative display.

### Priority fixes
1. Decide whether *whose* lives here, in chapter 5, or in chapter 15. It cannot live across all three.
2. Write the rest of the chapter. The title promises a broader argument about how linguists get grammaticality wrong; that argument is not on the page.
3. Build a Middle English to Modern English timeline showing the emergence of relative *who* and *whose* (see suggestion).
4. Add other cases of "getting grammaticality wrong" beyond *whose*: examples where prescriptivists were proven wrong by data; examples where descriptive linguists missed a productive form.

### One concrete suggestion
Build a single horizontal timeline figure called "When did English get a relative *whose*?"

X-axis: years, 800 to 2024.
Three labelled tick marks:
- ~ 800: *þe* + possessive pronoun (the *þe his* construction at \ref{ex:þe-his})
- ~ 1325: first attested relative *who* (Brett's earliest example)
- ~ 1611: King James Bible "the man whose these are" (from Chapter 5's OED data)
- 1973: Hankamer & Postal claim independent relative *whose* doesn't exist
- 2002: CGEL agrees
- 2018: OED still has examples
- 2024: GPT-4 / Gemini / 12 naive speakers all rate it 6/7

Above the timeline, an annotated band showing when the *whose* was attested in corpora (continuous from ~1325) and below the timeline an annotated band showing when linguists thought it didn't exist (1973-present, but with cracks appearing in 2024).

The figure compresses the chapter's central irony into a single display: linguists have spent fifty years claiming a form doesn't exist while the language has been using it for seven hundred years.

This one figure could carry chapter 20 if the chapter wants to be a single-case-study chapter. If it wants to be a broader survey of "getting grammaticality wrong," then add 3-5 more such cases (passive deprecation, split infinitive, singular *they*, *can/may*) each treated in 2-3 paragraphs and unified by a single comparative table at the end.

For now: the chapter is essentially unwritten. The above is what should be drafted, and the figure is what would let the book's recurring *whose* discussion settle into one well-illustrated home rather than reappearing in three different chapters.
