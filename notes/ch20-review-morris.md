## Chapter 20: Getting grammaticality wrong

### Strengths

The 25 lines that exist are scholarly and specific. The Old English data point (þonne ealra oþra kyninga þe in middangearde æfre wæron) is a real anchor. The 1325 example of relative *who* coming online (*He nadde bote an doȝter wo miȝte is eir be*) is the kind of small datum a book about grammar can travel on. The *þe his* construction as the precursor to relative *whose* is a real piece of historical English grammar.

### Major concerns

This is the third stub Reynolds flagged in the prompt. The chapter title promises "getting grammaticality wrong" but only one example is in the file: relative *whose*. That example is also already discussed in chapters 5 and 15. The section is thin and the title is bigger than the section.

What the chapter title promises and the file does not deliver:

1. A tour of historical and contemporary linguistic claims that turned out to be wrong, and what made them seductive in the first place. Chomsky's claim that *colorless green ideas* and *furiously sleep ideas* were equally remote from English (refuted by Pereira). Hankamer and Postal on relative independent *whose* (chapter 5). 1970s claims that passive by-phrases couldn't refer to the matrix subject (chapter 3). The mid-20th-century claim that double modals weren't grammatical anywhere (chapter 4). The claim that ending sentences with prepositions is ungrammatical. The claim that *Mommy sock* was structureless rather than ambiguous. The claim that English split infinitives were ungrammatical because Latin couldn't split. The claim that Riau Indonesian was a creole.

2. A pattern across these errors. The book has been gesturing at a pattern: linguists treating their own intuitions as universal, treating absence-of-evidence as evidence-of-absence, treating the asterisk as a yes/no rather than a many-shaded judgment. This chapter could be the place where that pattern crystallizes.

3. The chapter could engage with how the field corrects itself. Not just the errors but the corrections. Reynolds and Pullum email each other about *Rubymar* in chapter 15. Cuneo and Goldberg write a paper showing islands are pragmatic. The OED quietly recorded the existence of a *whose* that field-leading linguists missed. That self-correction is a story.

On the protagonists question: this chapter is the book's chance to treat its antagonists with the most generosity. The linguists who got things wrong were not stupid; they were doing the best they could with the tools and data they had. Hankamer and Postal in 1973 had no corpus and no internet. Chomsky in 1957 was using statistical models that didn't exist yet. Many of the "errors" the book catalogues were defensible at the time and only look wrong from hindsight.

The Errol-Morris-shaped thesis that fits this chapter is exactly what the prompt asks: are these linguists villains, victims of their framework, or honest people who made the mistake anyone would make? Reynolds has the chance to commit to the third answer here, in this chapter. That commitment shapes how the rest of the book reads.

### Priority fixes

1. Develop the chapter from stub to full draft.
2. Lay out three or four canonical "errors" of the field, each with a name, a date, and a paper.
3. For each, show the seductive force of the claim: why was it credible? What did it look like to be a sincere linguist holding it?
4. For each, show the correction: who pushed back, when, with what data, in what spirit?
5. Argue explicitly that getting grammaticality wrong is not a moral failure but a structural feature of doing this work without enough data, without enough context, with too narrow a sample.
6. Mark stub status in STATUS.md.

### One concrete suggestion

Open with John Payne, the CGEL co-author who wrote the chapter saying independent relative *whose* didn't exist. He's working with Reynolds on the *much*-as-NPI paper many years later. Reynolds asks Payne about the *whose* judgment. Payne doesn't get defensive. He says, more or less, "I thought it was right at the time. I was wrong." That moment, a senior grammarian admitting he was wrong about a published judgment, is the chapter's emotional spine. From there, the chapter becomes a tour of self-correction, of how grammarians work in good faith and still get things wrong, and of what the asterisk actually represents (a snapshot of one person's intuition at one moment, not a verdict).

That is not what this chapter looks like at the moment, but it could be. The chapter currently has 25 lines about Old English relative pronouns. It needs to be much more, and it needs to be a tribute to honest mistake-makers rather than a catalogue of errors.
