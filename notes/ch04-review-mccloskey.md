## Chapter 04: Morality and grammaticality

### Strengths
A handful of buried gems. Steven Coats's YouTube corpus and the rarity statistic for *would might* (one in 24 million words; comparable to *exigence*) is striking and earns its keep. The technical paragraph explaining why double modals violate Standard English (modals are defective, lack a plain form, can't follow another modal) is the chapter's only piece of solid linguistic explanation, and it works. The Jeff Brown anecdote (philosophy supervisor demanding no passives, in a chapter on translated Nietzsche full of passives) is the kind of small ironic story the book needs. The opening question, "Could speech have real-world negative consequences by virtue of its being ungrammatical?" is a real question worth a chapter.

### Major concerns
This chapter is a disaster zone. It is the worst LLM-pollution in the manuscript so far. The chapter contains what appears to be **three separate drafts of the same "Ethical Perspectives" section, all left in.** Section 3 is one draft, section 4 is a duplicate of section 3, section 9 is a third draft of the same material with Parfit added. Section 5 is also called "Ethical Perspectives on Grammaticality." A reader will lose all confidence the moment they hit the second iteration of the same paragraph headers (Pragmatism, Virtue Ethics, Consequentialism, Deontology) appearing twice, then a third time.

The Jahangir Khan section (lines 28-58) is encyclopedic biographical filler that has nothing to do with grammaticality. It reads as if the prompt was "introduce the idea of cooperation by talking about a famous athlete." The five-paragraph biography ends with "In conclusion, while cooperation is a guiding principle in many areas of life, it's not an absolute" -- the Wikipedia of moral philosophy.

Bentham at line 17 receives the same wallpaper treatment ("Bentham's musings on language, writing, and grammar offer another dimension to his character"). Parfit at lines 487-507 likewise.

The example "Habla español" on line 604 is glossed wrong: gloss requires three lines, the verb-form translation should be "speaks" but the prose then says "He/She speaks Spanish" without acknowledging that this is a third-person form, not a generic. This may not survive copy-edit.

The "You're right. I apologize for missing that formatting detail and for not addressing the mirroring aspect" line at 231 is a literal LLM apology accidentally included in the manuscript. **Cut immediately, before any reader sees it.**

There is also the third example at 539, "Janet brought their own lunch", which is not the kind of singular *they* that has been controversial; the controversial cases are "every student brought their own lunch" (with quantified antecedent) or known-singular antecedents. Janet is just a name; the example is misframed.

### Priority fixes
- Pick **one** version of "Ethical Perspectives" and delete the other two. Probably the Parfit-framed version (section 9) is the keeper.
- Cut the Jahangir Khan biography to one paragraph, or replace it with one line about non-cooperation. The squash anecdote is doing none of the work.
- Remove the literal LLM apology at line 231.
- The double-modal section is the only piece of the chapter that earns its space. Build around it.
- Decide whether morality is even the right frame. The chapter never quite answers its own opening question. If it is, drop the seven-frameworks taxonomy entirely; pick two (consequentialism and deontology, say) and apply them to *one* example carefully.

### One concrete suggestion
Reset. Replace the entire chapter with a 2,000-word piece organized like this: (1) Open with the question "Is bad grammar morally bad?" and the historical fact that people have always treated it that way. (2) Three short cases: the dangling modifier as moral failing; "Me and him" as social marker; "He be working" as racialized judgment. (3) The Parfit move can be a single paragraph: maybe these moral framings are climbing the same mountain, but the mountain is sociolinguistic, not ethical. (4) Coats's double-modal story as a coda: real grammatical difference, no moral content. The chapter as it stands is six chapters long and says less than this single rewrite would.

### Rhetoric that works
"Coats tells me he finds just three in my home province of Ontario." One sentence does what 200 paragraphs of "ethical framework" cannot: establishes the writer as someone with a community, a place, and a working relationship with a real linguist.

### Rhetoric that doesn't
"In conclusion, while cooperation is a guiding principle in many areas of life, it's not an absolute. Just as Khan's dominance in squash was built on a foundation of strategic non-cooperation, there are times in language, and life more broadly, when going against the norm can be not just beneficial, but transformative." This is text generated by a machine that has run out of things to say. "Just as X, similarly Y. The key lies in." Cut without mercy.
