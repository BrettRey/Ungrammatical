## Chapter 13: Across language boundaries

### Strengths

The Putnam and O'Hern study is the chapter's documentary heart. Two graduate students at Catholic University in the early 1950s, Father George N. Putnam and Sister Rosina O'Hern, walking the inhabited alleys of Washington D.C., interviewing 74 of 88 known adult Black residents, carrying tape recorders, recording residents reading *Aesop's Fables*. Then the experiment: twelve speakers tell the same story, 70 listeners rank them by social status, and the rankings correlate with actual status. The detail is specific. The names are real. The methods (a priest and a nun with tape recorders, in 1955) carry their own quiet drama. Reynolds even notes the Labov criticism (unsystematic, unclear what the judges were reacting to). That treatment is balanced. The study lives.

The BIN-camouflage point is the chapter's other strong moment. \textit{They've BIN living in Chicago} is not the same proposition as \textit{They've been living in Chicago}; the capitalized BIN means a long time. A Standard-English speaker hears the camouflaged version as the un-camouflaged one and never notices that something different has been said. That's the chapter's argument about cross-dialect ungrammaticality at its best: the Standard-English listener is not just rejecting non-Standard forms, sometimes they don't even register them.

The Spears quotation about all-Black schools and student-led language policing is harrowing and necessary. Students upbraiding peers for habitual *be*. That's the kind of internal-community detail trade readers don't usually get.

### Major concerns

The chapter opens with three paragraphs of register-tourism: academic English, legal English, literary English, advertising English, sports commentary, Bill Withers, Scots, Singlish, pidgins, creoles, multilingualism. None of it is in the writer's voice. The cadence is "X showcases Y, with Z." This is one of the patterns the rules in `.claude/rules/writing-style.md` flag (formulaic openings that scene-set without committing to a person or a place). Cut.

The "Just how different is it?" subsection is good (four grammatical differences in two words of \textit{Ain't nobody}), but the Italian comparison is parenthetical and doesn't quite land. It's the right move but it should be expanded into a genuine cross-linguistic moment.

The "One language, multiple grammars" section at line 94 is just six bare bullet points (Intentionality, Clarity, Familiarity, etc.) with the word "Balinese" floating above them. This is a notes-to-self section.

The "Gendered language" section asks one of the chapter's best questions ("Is your feeling upon hearing a masculine person say *Oh my gosh!* fundamentally the same as when you hear an Anglophone say *I no like that*?") and then drops the question. That question is a chapter on its own. It echoes the morality and politics chapters and could be one of the book's pivots: when does a register mismatch feel like ungrammaticality, and when doesn't it?

On the protagonists question: Putnam and O'Hern are the chapter's protagonists, but only briefly. Where are they on the day they walked into the first alley? Did they wear their cassocks? Did they introduce themselves as students of Father Furfey? Were the residents reading *Aesop's Fables* aware they were being recorded for a sociological study? What did the priest and the nun think they were doing? That is precisely the kind of historical situation Errol Morris would film. Reynolds has the citation; it's worth a longer treatment.

Also: the Spears quote names "all-Black schools" Spears attended. The first-person voice is right there. The chapter could lean on Spears as a witness, not just a citation.

### Priority fixes

1. Cut the opening register-tourism paragraphs (lines 3-11).
2. Build out the gendered-language question into either a section or a bridge to a later chapter.
3. Fill or cut the "One language, multiple grammars" section. Right now it's a placeholder.
4. Consider whether Putnam and O'Hern deserve more space. They probably do.

### One concrete suggestion

Open the chapter inside one of those alleys in 1953. Father Putnam and Sister O'Hern with a reel-to-reel recorder. A resident reading *Aesop's Fables* aloud. The fox and the grapes. The microphone. The cassock. The 74-of-88 ratio. Then the experiment, two years later, the rankings, the correlation. Then the question Reynolds is leading toward: it's not just that non-Standard English is judged differently, but that listeners with no exposure to a particular non-Standard variety can still rank its speakers by social status from sound alone. Then bring in BIN as the corollary: not just judgment, but invisibility. There are entire grammatical distinctions Standard-English speakers don't even hear. The chapter is about that asymmetry between dialects: who hears whom, and who is heard out the wrong way. The opening register-tour can come out completely.
