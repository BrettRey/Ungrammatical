## Chapter 16: Unravelling babel (codeswitching)

### Strengths
The Tomo anecdote (lines 24-30) is the chapter's emotional and intellectual center. A toddler in a Toronto kitchen during SARS, witnessing his parents speak Japanese, then turning to translate for Grandma. This is the chapter's argument in concrete form: he doesn't just know English and Japanese, he knows who understands what. That's the right concrete observation to anchor a chapter on bilingual grammaticality.

The Wiese / Inya story is even better. A three-year-old who treats English as "Daddy's quirk" and refuses to use it with her British in-laws. The implication that grammaticality is community-relative, not language-relative, is real and underexplored. Wiese's term "com-sit" (communicative situation) is exactly the right level of abstraction.

The Grieve quotation about situational diversity vs grammatical complexity is the right theoretical anchor. Languages adapted for a wider range of contexts may be grammatically simpler precisely because grammatical work is being done elsewhere.

### Major concerns
This is a notes-file chapter that has not yet been written. The chapter opens with a fifteen-bullet list of the argument-to-be (lines 3-15). Then a meta-paragraph at line 22 ("The ultimate point of the chapter, eventually, will be...") tells the reader what the chapter will eventually argue. Then the Tomo anecdote. Then the Wiese quote (which has already appeared in the bullet list at line 13, verbatim). Then a paragraph from Smith 2019 (which has also already appeared in the bullet list at line 7, verbatim). Then the Grieve quote.

This is a draft sketch. Nothing is integrated. The reader sees the same Wiese quote twice and the same Smith summary twice. The chapter's title ("Unravelling babel") is great but the chapter doesn't deliver on it.

There is no actual discussion of code-switching despite the chapter title in the table of contents. The reader doesn't learn what code-switching is, what its grammatical patterns look like (intrasentential vs intersentential, the Equivalence Constraint, the Matrix Language Frame), or what counts as ungrammatical code-switching. Other than the Tomo and Inya anecdotes, the empirical material is absent.

The chapter is also short (48 lines), much shorter than its topic warrants. Code-switching is a major area of linguistics with rich grammaticality phenomena (e.g., the famous Spanglish constraint that you can't switch language between an auxiliary and its main verb).

### Priority fixes
- **Build out the chapter.** Right now it's 80% notes and 20% draft. The Tomo and Inya stories are the emotional spine; build outward from them.
- Cut the bullet list at the top (lines 3-15) once its content has been integrated into prose.
- Eliminate the duplicate Wiese and Smith quotations.
- Add actual content on code-switching: the Equivalence Constraint (Poplack), the Matrix Language Frame Model (Myers-Scotton), and the famous "Why make Carol *sentarse atrás* pa' que everybody has to move *pa' que se salga*" type examples that show grammatical code-switching.
- Decide whether the chapter is about code-switching specifically or about com-sits generally. The current title ("Unravelling babel") suggests the latter; the table of contents calls it "codeswitching." Pick one and commit.

### One concrete suggestion
Build the chapter around three increasing scales of multilingual grammaticality. (1) Inya treating English as "Daddy's register" and refusing to use it with grandparents: a child's confusion of language with com-sit, which is in fact half-right because com-sit is the more fundamental category. (2) Tomo turning from his parents' Japanese conversation to render it in English for Grandma: a toddler's mastery of who-knows-what, which is the everyday work of bilingual life. (3) Adult code-switching: bilingual speakers who alternate languages mid-sentence in highly systematic ways, with grammaticality constraints that are themselves bilingual ("Why make Carol *sentarse atrás*..."). End with the chapter's payoff: grammaticality is not the property of a language; it's the property of a communicative situation between speakers who share or fail to share resources. Bring back the Tower of Babel: the story is wrong because pre-Babel one-language-one-meaning was never how language worked.

### Rhetoric that works
"It wasn't just that he knew there was a difference between Japanese and English; he also knew who understood what language." (line 30). One sentence carries the chapter. The reader feels the toddler's social-cognitive achievement. The argument follows from there.

### Rhetoric that doesn't
"In this way, communication is like a game where one person has to think about the best way to get their message across, considering how the audience will interpret their words or actions, and the audience has to has to think about the best way to interpret them." (line 32, repeated from line 7). "has to has to" is a typo, but the larger problem is that the sentence is generic. The chapter gives a beautiful concrete example (Tomo translating for his grandmother) and then summarizes it back into vague abstraction. Trust the example. Cut the summary.

### A note on the citation in line 13
