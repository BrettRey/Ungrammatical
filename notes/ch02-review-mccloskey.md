## Chapter 02: How grammatical can you get? (Gradient grammaticality)

### Strengths
The Mr. Boyko anecdote is good rhetoric: it earns a comparison between code and prose without preaching about it. The "Poof, you're a snack" pun introduces the double-object/resultative ambiguity in a way no formalist would dare and no child would forget. The five-step "you're allowed ___" cline (a-e) is the chapter's core contribution; it shows gradience without arguing for it. And the pit-bull, sandwich-versus-burrito, and Brancusi cases give a non-linguist reasons to care about category troubles. Good rhetorical instincts here, you trust your stories to do the work.

### Major concerns
The chapter is structurally broken. There is an unfinished list at lines 99-103 ("It may"). Section 3 has no title (the second `\section{}` is empty). The chapter opens "Another view is evident in Chomsky's very early work" but no first view has yet been presented; that orphan opener is confusing. Sampson is invoked four times before the reader knows who he is, then properly introduced only at the end. This is a draft that has been moved around and not stitched back up.

The Aristotle/classical-categories paragraph at line 192 contains a duplicate sentence ("This approach... assumes that categories have clear-cut boundaries and can be neatly defined by a set of necessary and sufficient conditions" appears twice, separated by a few clauses). Classic LLM cut-and-paste artifact.

There is one egregious overreach: the IAAF Pistorius story is misremembered. The IAAF banned him; the CAS overturned the ban. He never competed in the 2008 Olympics (his appeal succeeded in 2008 but he didn't qualify time-wise). He competed in 2012. **Source-grounding violation, fix before any reader notices.**

The legal-categories digression goes on too long, three full cases when one would do. Rosch and prototypes are introduced but barely used: the *worth* example is good but feels orphaned. The progressive-aspect history is genuinely interesting but the Figure includes the spurious 1560 datapoint when the text says the first attestation is 1564.

### Priority fixes
- Restore section structure: open with a clear "two views" framing (Chomsky's binary; Sampson's corpus). Both are mentioned in passing now; neither gets fair statement.
- Finish or cut the unfinished list (lines 99-103).
- Title or remove the empty `\section{}` (line 134).
- Fix the duplicate Aristotle sentence (line 192).
- **Source-check Pistorius**: he didn't compete in 2008, he didn't apply to the IAAF for the Olympics; the case was about IAAF rule 144.2(e) re competitive advantage. Cut the case or rewrite it from the actual record.
- Cut the third legal-categories case (Brancusi or Panera-Qdoba) to keep momentum. Two is plenty.
- Resolve "It's the aspect of autumn." Either own this lyrical move (it's good) or cut it.

### One concrete suggestion
The chapter's argument is gradience. Build it on a single example. Use the "you're allowed ___" cline (a-e) as the spine of the chapter. Open with it. Use the Mr. Boyko anecdote as your model of what binary grammaticality looks like (code compiles or it doesn't). Use Sampson's "everything in a corpus is grammatical" as the foil. Use the progressive-aspect history to show that gradience is real over time as well as across speakers. End with the prototype/category section as the theoretical home for the gradience. Right now the chapter has eight sections that pull eight directions; gradience never lands as the dominant idea. The reader should leave saying "grammaticality has degrees" and remembering five gradient sentences. They will leave remembering Pistorius and a burrito.

### Rhetoric that works
"\textit{Worth} is the ostrich of adjectives or the bat of mammals." This is the book's voice at its best, image, wit, payoff in one line. Do more of this.

### Rhetoric that doesn't
"These cases underscore the classical view's limitations and strengths in categorization. While the classical approach provides a necessary foundation for legal and practical determinations, it also encounters challenges when faced with evolving societal and cultural norms. The resolution of these disputes reflects a broader legal and societal recognition of the need for definitions that accommodate changing perceptions and values, demonstrating compromises between the classical view and the nuanced reality of categorization." This is a paragraph an LLM wrote because it had reached the bottom of a section. It says "we have written a section, and now it is over." Cut every word.
