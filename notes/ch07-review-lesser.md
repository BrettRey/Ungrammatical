## Chapter 07: The Generation Gap

### Strengths

This is the leanest chapter in the book so far. Forty-two lines, but the lines do their work. The dust-storm walkthrough of discrete combination is a small marvel of pedagogy. Reynolds takes a single phrase ("a dust storm") and shows in eight sentences why discrete combination is a strange thing in nature, why "cosmic dust" works but "cosmic a dust storm" doesn't, and why order of combination matters. A trade reader will follow this. A linguistics student should envy it.

The Culicover counter-example (likely / probable) lands at the right moment. The chapter has just persuaded the reader that abstraction is good and lists are bad, and Culicover walks in with a pair of sentences showing that some lexical idiosyncrasies refuse to abstract away. This is the right place for that bomb.

The McCawley-to-the-New-Yorker quote is well-chosen and carries genuine intellectual drama: in the early seventies, Chomsky's own students declared the master's central concept incoherent. That's a story in itself, and it sits at the chapter's natural pivot point.

The Boeckx coda on island repair is dense but substantive: if a derivational grammar generates only grammatical sentences, how does it ever produce something that has to be repaired? The chapter has earned this question.

### Major concerns

The chapter is too short to do its title justice. "The Generation Gap" promises an exploration of how generative grammar fragmented in the 1970s and what that fragmentation means for the concept of grammaticality. We get a setup, a McCawley quote, and a brief gesture at "the way the language evolves is towards systematicity, while at the same time allowing new irregularities into the system." That sentence, which is the chapter's actual thesis, deserves its own development.

The "yin and yang" phrasing is one beat too cute and undercuts what is otherwise the chapter's most precise sentence.

The chapter ends by raising Boeckx's puzzle and not addressing it. The reader has been promised a generation gap; they get a single Boeckx quote and a section break.

There is also a small typographical scar: a stray ", plus ." at the end of line 9. Likely a leftover edit.

### Priority fixes

1. Develop the McCawley quote. What did Postal and Lakoff actually argue, and how does Reynolds's HPC view position itself against both Chomsky and the generative semanticists? The chapter is positioned to be a hinge of the book; it should act like one.
2. Write the second half of the "Generational failures" section. Boeckx's question deserves an answer, even a tentative one.
3. Decide whether the chapter is named "The Generation Gap" because of generative grammar or because of generational shifts (parents to kids). If it's the second meaning that you want, the chapter needs new examples (children's grammar diverging from parents'); if the first, the title is fine.
4. Repair the "plus ." trail.
5. Cut "yin and yang" to "the back-and-forth between order and disorder" or simpler.

### One concrete suggestion

Worst sentence: "But this seems highly inefficient. Wouldn't such a grammar present significant challenges in storage and use?" Two sentences asking the same question. Cut to: "This seems inefficient." The book's voice is strongest when it stops checking in with the reader.

Best sentence: "The way the language evolves is towards systematicity, while at the same time allowing new irregularities into the system in a constant yin and yang of order and disorder." Strip the yin and yang and you have the chapter's central insight: language pulls toward system but admits irregularity continuously, and grammaticality lives in that pull. That's a sentence that wants to be a paragraph. Make it one.
